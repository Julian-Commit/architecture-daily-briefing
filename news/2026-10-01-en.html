Discarded wool becomes a cladding tile you can hang straight onto an existing wall: Harvard's Grinham Research Group breaks wool's protein chains with an alkaline solution to make a dual-density tile aimed at retrofit without excavation — on show until today at the Oslo Architecture Triennale
Sources: Dezeen (September 27) · Architectural Record (triennale review) · Wallpaper* (triennale review) · Dezeen (September 21, triennale highlights) · Grinham Research Group (Harvard University) · Oslo Architecture Triennale
The Grinham Research Group at Harvard University, led by Jonathan Grinham, has developed an insulating cladding tile made from recycled wool, presented in the main exhibition of the ninth Oslo Architecture Triennale as an installation titled Upcycling Wool Ecologies, with the tiles mounted on a timber frame. The triennale's theme is "What if nature comes first?", its director and curator is Line Ramstad, it runs from September 17 to October 1, 2026, and its venue is Sofienberg Kirke — a deconsecrated 19th-century church in Oslo's Grünerløkka neighbourhood — with admission at 100 Norwegian kroner. Wool is discarded in quantity as a residual material by the agricultural and textile industries, and what the group saw in it were three properties useful to building: it insulates, it resists fire, and it is antibacterial. The manufacturing route is chemical: an alkaline solution breaks down the material's protein chains, and a biodegradable additive is introduced to turn it into a plastic, mouldable material. The finished product is multi-layered: a low-density back panel acts as an insulating board, while a denser outer panel provides durability and weather resistance. The point of that build-up is that the tile can be applied directly to the exterior of an existing building, avoiding the costly work of digging into a wall to upgrade its insulation — which is to say it is aimed not at new construction but at retrofit.

Goldring Woldenberg Riverfront Park sits in the heart of New Orleans, Louisiana, near Jackson Square. URBANISM
Vietnam's Urban Development Law takes effect today: five chapters and 66 articles, letting Ho Chi Minh City raise its own staffing by up to 20%, spend municipal budget on TOD compensation and resettlement, and pilot policies that diverge from existing law for up to five years
Sources: Vietnam Investment Review (September 30) · Saigon Giai Phong (SGGP English) · Vietnam News
The law was passed by Vietnam's 16th National Assembly on August 24, 2026, at its first extraordinary session, with 465 of the 474 deputies present voting in favour (about 93%), and takes effect on October 1, 2026. It runs to five chapters and 66 articles, with mechanisms and policies aimed chiefly at special-class cities — Ho Chi Minh City above all — and at special economic zones. Key provisions include devolving authority to city people's councils, people's committees and their chairpersons to issue legal documents containing provisions that differ from central approvals; allowing cities to decide their own total number of civil servant and public employee positions, up to 20% above the centrally assigned figure, provided they balance their own budgets; permitting city governments to use municipal budget for compensation, resettlement and urban development within transit-oriented development (TOD) areas; and diversifying infrastructure financing to include the private sector and public-private partnerships. The law further allows Ho Chi Minh City to pilot policies diverging from existing laws or National Assembly resolutions for up to five years, with special tax incentives applying from the 2027 tax year. Officials characterise the shift as moving from "urban management" to "facilitating and developing urban areas"; for the property sector, clearer rules give businesses "a more favourable basis for assessing costs, timelines, and legal risks."

Building a park over a river: New Orleans uses existing freight piers as its foundation and turns a decommissioned plastics warehouse into a cultural pavilion — at the price of a doubled budget
Cross-checked against: The Architect's Newspaper (September 30, completion review) · Axios New Orleans (April 6, first look) · WWL-TV · Fox 8 New Orleans (April 7) · KMEZ 102.9 (April 7) · EskewDumezRipple and Hargreaves Jones (designers)

Design concept: the park's foundation is the residue of freight infrastructure. Goldring Woldenberg Riverfront Park lies in the heart of New Orleans near Jackson Square. Its first major phase covers more than six acres and opened on April 16, 2026, for the City of New Orleans and the Audubon Nature Institute, which jointly funded it. Architecture is by EskewDumezRipple, with managing principal Christian Rodriguez as project lead; landscape architecture is by Hargreaves Jones, with principal Mary Margaret Jones. The Architect's Newspaper published its completion review on September 30. The fundamental site condition here is not that the park is beside a river but that it is over one: a substantial portion sits on existing concrete piers, originally part of freight wharf infrastructure, reaching out from the bank above the surface of the Mississippi. Mary Margaret Jones puts it plainly — "You're over a river." That matters because it changes the nature of the work. This is not a riverbank that can be cut and filled at will but a set of existing structures whose load capacity and geometry were already fixed by a previous industrial use, with the park added as a second layer of occupation on top.

Spatial strategy: not making a park so much as completing 2.25 miles of waterfront. The project's real scale is not captured by the six-acre figure but by what it connects. New Orleans's riverfront public space had been fragmented — several unconnected stretches of park wedged between freight and commercial facilities, with no continuous route along the water. This phase joins two formerly separate parks into a 2.25-mile continuous walking and cycling corridor from Spanish Plaza to Crescent Park, described in local coverage as among the longest continuous riverfront parks in the United States. The design priorities for this kind of stitching project differ from those of an ordinary park: success turns less on how well any single node is resolved than on whether the connections at either end genuinely work. Internally, the park is layered by intensity of use — the festival lawn is tiered to preserve sightlines from the back rows through to the water, with swings and benches turned toward the river, alongside hammocks, earthen plantings and cooling misters. In a subtropical humid climate the misters are not decoration; they are among the conditions determining whether anyone is willing to linger in summer. Playground equipment is custom-made by Kompan on motifs drawn from Louisiana's native fauna — alligator, frog, turtle and fish.

Construction and conversion: decommissioned plastics warehouse to metal-mesh-clad pavilion. A decommissioned plastics warehouse stood on the site. Rather than demolish it, the design converts it into a cultural pavilion called the NOLA Front Porch, clad in metal mesh and fitted with rainwater harvesting. Three points are worth recording. First, retaining the warehouse structure retains its existing long spans and clear height, which is a ready-made condition for assembly and performance uses that need column-free space — achieving comparable spans in new construction would cost considerably more. Second, metal mesh is a sensible envelope choice in a hot, humid climate: it ventilates, it does not hold water, it is easy to maintain, and it visually lightens an industrial volume so that it does not sit awkwardly in the middle of an open lawn. Third, the name is not merely rhetorical. The front porch is the central social space of the Southern American house — half-outdoor, half-public — and applying it to a public building facing the river names a specific way of using it. Published material does not disclose how the piers' load capacity was reassessed, what flood elevation was adopted, or how the warehouse structure was reinforced, and this briefing will not infer them.

Context and significance: the rise from $15 million to $30 million is part of the case. The project was originally budgeted at $15 million and rose to $30 million during the pandemic, a full doubling. For architecture students that figure should not be filed away as a footnote about project management but read as the characteristic risk profile of waterfront adaptive reuse: the true condition of existing structures only becomes fully visible once construction begins, while over-water working, waterside protection and material price volatility all deteriorated simultaneously between 2020 and 2022. More notable is that the project reached completion even after the budget doubled, which usually indicates a non-market component in the funding structure — here, a city government and a nature institute funding it jointly. The case is more interesting still read alongside the Rust Belt cities nearer Syracuse: many hold stretches of waterfront occupied by freight facilities now out of service, and New Orleans offers a relatively uncommon answer. Rather than clearing the site and starting over, it accepts the piers and the warehouse as given, and makes them the park's structure and its building.

Mitte Oblique Garden / RAD+ar, Jakarta: 1,100 m² of commercial space that dismantles the usual boundary between building, landscape and climate using oblique geometry
Sources: ArchDaily (October 1) · RAD+ar (architect; Daniel Susanto, Antonius Richard Rusli)
The project is in Jakarta, Indonesia, at 1,100 m², a commercial building completed in 2026, photography by Ernest Theofilus, with Lucas Dharma Sourice, Yusron and Winston Goey on the design team. The architects position it as a spatial intervention — "a project that reclaims the human scale within the hyper-density of Jakarta." The method is to dismantle the conventional divisions separating building, landscape and climate: courtyards and gardens are not residue left over outside the building but are interlocked with commercial space across several levels through oblique geometry, so that ventilation and shading become products of the plan itself rather than equipment added to it. The structure is concrete with timber elements. The architects describe it as a new vision for Asian megacities; set against comparable tropical commercial projects, what makes this one legible is that the climate strategy is worked into the section rather than stopping at the facade.
House Around the Corner in Kugahara / some ( ) + Tezzo Nishizawa Architects, Ota, Tokyo: 102 m² on a corner lot that opens a garden toward the neighbours instead of presenting them a blank wall
Sources: ArchDaily (October 1) · some ( ) and Tezzo Nishizawa Architects (architects; Sotaro Miyatake, Tezzo Nishizawa) · Structure: Yoshiyuki Hiraiwa, Takayuki Fujimoto, Rika Ikeda / HSC
The house is in Kugahara, Ota, Tokyo, at 102 m², completed 2025, built by Eishin Construction, photographed by Benjamin Hosking, in wood, steel and concrete. The standard treatment of a Tokyo corner lot is to face the two streets and wall off the two neighbouring boundaries, betting all the daylight on the street side. The architects inverted that, opening a court toward the adjacent properties instead — "a garden toward the adjacent properties, bringing light and air into the block's interior." In plan, an angular grid carves out triangular intermediate zones that let sightlines pass between the two streets. Both street elevations are gabled and given equal weight, refusing any hierarchy and so avoiding the usual front-facade-plus-back-facade arrangement. The case reads well against the blank-wall-to-neighbour default in dense districts: on a site of roughly 100 m², which side the court opens to determines daylight and ventilation for the interior of the whole block, not merely for this one household.
PetroChina Shanghai Advanced Materials Research Institute / Perkins&Will, Lin-gang, Shanghai: 72,000 m² planned as an infinity symbol, with 21,000 m² of laboratories built as eight reconfigurable platforms for about 500 researchers
Sources: gooood (September 30) · Perkins&Will (architect) · Tongji Architectural Design (Group) Co., Ltd. (local design partner)
The institute is in the Lin-gang Special Area, Shanghai, on a 20,600 m² site with 72,000 m² of total built area, completed August 2025 for PetroChina Shanghai Advanced Materials Research Institute Co., Ltd. Design is by Perkins&Will with Tongji Architectural Design (Group) Co., Ltd. as local partner. The plan takes the form of an infinity symbol, generated by two offset rectangular volumes organised around landscaped courtyards, answering the architects' theme of "from 0 to ∞." Spatially it departs from the linear layout conventional to research buildings, using an open stair to connect multiple levels and link exhibition space, auditorium, library and workspaces onto one route, so that shared circulation produces interdisciplinary contact. The 21,000 m² of research space comprises eight platforms plus shared facilities, in modular and flexible layouts to accommodate later reconfiguration and equipment upgrades. The facade is perforated aluminium panels and glass, forming what the architects describe as "continuous horizontal elements like unfolding book pages." High-performance photovoltaics are integrated, meeting China's Three-Star Green Building standard.

Not slow to build — slow to approve: UK housing permissions hit a fourteen-year low, and the sharpest fall is in schemes of three or more homes, meaning the upstream pipeline for housing at scale is breaking
Cross-checked against: Home Builders Federation, Housing Pipeline Report (September 30) · Architects' Journal · Building Design (BD Online) · Building · Property Week · PBC Today · Planning Geek

The data: three separate record lows landing in the same quarter. The Home Builders Federation (HBF) Housing Pipeline Report, published September 30, gives three sets of figures. First, the quarterly number: 45,315 homes received planning permission in England in Q2 2026, down 21% on the previous quarter and the lowest quarterly figure since 2012. Second, the annual number: 214,515 homes permitted across the UK in the twelve months to Q2 2026, the lowest annual total since 2013, down 8% year on year and 36% below the 2017 peak. Third, and most consequential — the figure most easily lost behind the headline — counting projects rather than units, only 1,234 schemes of three or more homes were approved in the quarter, the lowest quarterly figure on record; the annual figure of 5,783 for the same category is also the lowest on record. Units and projects hitting record lows together means this is not a matter of a few large sites failing to come forward. Development at medium scale and above is thinning out across the board.

The distance from policy: 58%. That annual figure of 214,515 equals 58% of the 370,000 permissions a year identified in the government's National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF). The relationship between 370,000 and 300,000 is worth spelling out: the delivery target is 300,000 net additional homes a year, and the NPPF holds that sustaining that delivery requires roughly 370,000 permissions a year upstream, because permission does not equal a start on site, and a start does not equal a completion — there is leakage at every stage of the pipeline. On that measure, current permission levels are already insufficient to support the delivery target, and the gap is not marginal. By sector, social housing permissions fell 33% year on year between January and June, against 17% for the private sector — the steeper fall being in precisely the part of the system meant to provide a floor.

The mechanism: the problem is scheme viability, not processing speed. HBF attributes the decline to "weak effective demand, constrained access to affordable mortgage lending and subdued market conditions," compounded by taxes, levies and layered policy requirements bearing on viability. HBF chief executive Neil Jefferson puts it this way: "The cumulative impact of higher taxes, increasing costs and layers of new policy requirements is making more and more sites unviable." The report supplies one concrete mechanism: the Building Safety Levy, launching next month, has already caused 36% of small and medium-sized housebuilders to delay, redesign or cancel schemes. This is the part worth the attention of architecture students, because it locates the fall upstream of submission rather than inside determination. Developers run the numbers, judge a site unviable, and never file an application at all. Reading figures like these as evidence of an inefficient planning system points in the wrong direction; the operative variables are cost, finance and the accumulated weight of compliance requirements.

Context and significance: World Architecture Day on October 5 is themed "Housing is a Human Right. Build It." The coincidence of timing deserves a mention. The International Union of Architects (UIA) has set that theme for World Architecture Day on October 5, 2026, alongside the UN's World Habitat Day theme of "Adequate Housing for All." The HBF data supply an unflattering footnote to the slogan: in a developed economy that names housing shortage among its most urgent urban challenges, the upstream pipeline for supply at scale is narrowing. Two things follow for students. First, the boundary conditions of housing design are shifting — once viability determines whether a project exists at all, the relationship between cost, compliance and design decisions moves forward into the concept stage rather than waiting for technical design. Second, the data point toward an expansion of professional role: the UIA's call is precisely for architects to extend their work into housing policy, affordability and the reuse of existing stock, rather than stopping at the drawings. This briefing reports the figures and the parties' own statements, and takes no position on UK housing policy.

IN DEPTH
Turn wool into a plastic, then press it into tiles: Harvard's Grinham Research Group gives retrofit an insulating skin that needs no wall excavation — and the whole Oslo Triennale is doing the same thing, making the material first and deriving the detail after
Cross-checked against: Dezeen (September 27) · Architectural Record (triennale review) · Wallpaper* (triennale review) · Dezeen (September 21, triennale highlights) · Grinham Research Group (Harvard University) · Oslo Architecture Triennale

Material innovation: break the protein chains and the fibre becomes mouldable. The starting point for the Grinham Research Group at Harvard University, led by Jonathan Grinham, is a fact about supply: wool is discarded in quantity as a residual material by the agricultural and textile industries, and it already possesses three properties that are useful in building — it insulates, it resists fire, and it is antibacterial. The obstacle is that wool is a fibre, not a board, and cannot serve as a building component as it stands. The group's route is chemical: an alkaline solution breaks down the material's protein chains, and a biodegradable additive is introduced to turn the result into a plastic material that can be pressed into shape. That step is the heart of the research, because it replaces the existing product category of wool insulation batt with something else entirely — a wool-based board that can be formed, can carry load, and can face the weather. On performance, the consistent description across reports is that the finished material is strong and water-resistant, and suited to retrofit work.

Detail strategy: two densities inside a single tile. The product is not a homogeneous board but a multi-layer build-up: a low-density back panel carries the insulating function, effectively serving as an insulation board, while a denser outer panel provides durability and weather resistance. This one-tile-two-densities arrangement deserves to be noted on its own, because it resolves a long-standing contradiction in insulation materials. Thermal performance wants low density — porous, air-filled — while weather resistance and impact resistance want high density, and the two are naturally in conflict within a single material. The conventional answer is to separate them in sequence on site: fix insulation board, then build a finish layer over it, which means two trades, two sets of fixings and two opportunities for thermal bridging. Compressing both densities into one prefabricated component means a single installation operation on site. For a climate zone like Syracuse's, with real winters, the practical value of that is greater than it would be in a temperate one.

Why retrofit: the point is to avoid opening up the wall. The group is explicit about the intended application: the tile hangs on the exterior of an existing building. The background is that the most expensive step in energy retrofit is improving the thermal performance of an existing external wall, which conventionally means digging into it — adding insulation from the inside or within the build-up, which entails stripping finishes, emptying rooms and destroying existing interiors. The cost and disruption of that sequence is the real reason a great many existing buildings have never been retrofitted at all. An externally applied insulating tile sidesteps the whole operation: nothing internal is touched, and a layer is simply added to the facade. It is a characteristic trade of appearance for cost — accepting that the elevation will change, in return for a retrofit that can be carried out without residents moving out. The triennale showed the work mounted on a timber frame, which is precisely a display of that tile-by-tile installation logic.

Context and significance: an entire triennale making its materials first. The work appeared as the installation Upcycling Wool Ecologies in the main exhibition of the ninth Oslo Architecture Triennale, which ran from September 17 to October 1, 2026 and closes today. The theme is "What if nature comes first?", the director and curator is Line Ramstad, and the venue is Sofienberg Kirke, a deconsecrated 19th-century church in Oslo's Grünerløkka neighbourhood, with admission at 100 Norwegian kroner. Ramstad describes the curatorial intent this way: "We hope to learn from the solutions that already exist and to imagine our built environment on nature's terms." The main exhibition gathers more than thirty international projects whose common thread is the use of bio-based, locally sourced, recycled or low-embodied-carbon materials. Returned to that context, the wool tile is not an isolated material experiment but a specimen of a method: none of these projects selects from an existing materials catalogue; each makes the material itself first and derives the construction and the junctions afterwards. The practical note for students is that this route is migrating from research institutions toward practice, and the capabilities it demands — material testing, chemistry and construction together, plus an understanding of the retrofit market — do not overlap neatly with conventional drawing-based training.

Curatorial concept: aimed squarely at modernity's most durable promise. The second Copenhagen Architecture Biennial is titled "Elements" and runs September 9 to October 15, 2027. The organiser is the Copenhagen Architecture Forum (CAFx), founded in 2014, renamed in 2024, based at Halmtorvet 27 in Copenhagen. By the organiser's own curatorial statement, this edition turns from the inaugural call to "Slow Down" toward a deeper reckoning with the elemental forces that architecture has long sought to master and increasingly must learn to inhabit — a shift that calls directly into question one of modernity's most persistent promises, technological control over nature. Founder and chief curator Josephine Michau frames it this way: "With 'Elements', we ask what happens when we stop pretending we ever had that mastery, and start designing from dependence instead? The buildings that will serve us best aren't the ones that shut the world out, they're the ones that let us feel it."

Structure: four elements, four exhibition strands, and a symposium about regulation. The entire programme is organised around fire, earth, air and water, comprising four exhibitions, a symposium called Assemble!, and an open call for pavilions, exhibitions and research proposals. The most telling thing in that structure is where Assemble! is pointed: at policy and regulatory structures, not at the appraisal of work. That is consistent with how CAFx describes itself as an institution, operating along three strategic lines — Exhibitions & Inquiry, Learning & Education, and Policy and Law — the third explicitly aimed at the institutional conditions for sustainable building practice. In other words, this biennial does not treat "elements" as an aesthetic motif; it wires the theme to building codes and regulatory systems. If one is to design from dependence, how far current regulation permits that is a question that has to be handled at the same time. The two further questions in the curatorial statement — "Who is protected and who is exposed? What is maintained and what is allowed to fail?" — are likewise questions of distribution rather than of form.

The programme spans Copenhagen's Cultural District and over 60 venues across the city, those announced so far including Vandkulturhuset Papirøen, the water culture house designed by Kengo Kuma, along with Simian, Kunsthal Nord and CAFx's own Halmtorvet 27. The open call addresses architects, designers and researchers, inviting proposals for new pavilions on sites in the heart of Copenhagen that respond directly to the Elements theme, with exhibition and research proposals solicited in parallel. For students at Syracuse Architecture this is a genuinely usable route to submission: pavilion open calls at biennial level typically do not restrict entry to registered practices, and graduate teams and independent researchers have been selected historically. CAFx also has one exhibition running before the biennial that indicates its curatorial disposition: "Window Behaviorology – The Working Window," October 8, 2026 to January 15, 2027.

The inaugural edition ran in 2025 under the theme "Slow Down," drawing coverage from Dezeen, ArchDaily, Wallpaper*, ICON, STIRworld and the Architects' Journal among others, with reviewers generally reading "slow" as both metaphor and construction methodology. In a field already dense with architecture biennials — Venice, Sharjah, Seoul, Chicago, Lisbon, Tallinn — the survival question for a new one is positioning. On the evidence of its first two themes, Copenhagen has chosen to keep its subject on the material and institutional conditions of building: slowing down concerns time and method of work, elements concerns natural forces and regulation, and neither follows the national-pavilion logic of exhibiting finished work.
