EDITOR'S NOTE

One thread runs through today's stories: how to use an existing thing a second time, and what that costs. Orms has filed plans to convert London's 177-metre BT Tower into a hotel, keeping the original concrete structure in part to hold on to the carbon already spent building it — and reopening the 35th-floor viewing gallery after nearly fifty years closed. LOT-EK has stacked five upcycled shipping containers into a community schoolhouse in New Orleans, its ground floor lifted three feet against flooding. In Mumbai, a pavilion cuts, rotates and suspends municipal precast drainage pipes into a demountable civic room. The London Design Festival opened on 12 September with a bamboo gridshell as its Landmark Project, clad in Indian shields and farmers' rain covers. Meanwhile the MTA released renderings for eighteen Interborough Express stations — fourteen miles of existing freight corridor, $5.5 billion. The Tallinn Architecture Biennale simply made the question its title: How Much? Cost is moving from the closing balance sheet to the opening brief.

In Depth
Don't demolish, because the carbon is already spent: Orms treats a 1960s telecoms tower's concrete as an asset, not a liability
Cross-checked: Dezeen · The Architects' Journal · Building Design · Wallpaper* · Londonist · Time Out London

London practice Orms has submitted a planning application to the London Borough of Camden to convert the 177-metre BT Tower into a hotel for the American hotelier MCR. The application covers the Grade II-listed tower, the adjacent podium and the 1930s Howland Building. On the current timetable, work starts in 2029 once the building is fully vacated, with opening in 2033.

Concept: repricing the existing structure. The line in this application most worth an architecture student's attention is the justification for keeping the concrete — that it preserves the significant carbon already invested in its construction. It is a clean example of the embodied carbon argument entering a formal planning document: structure is no longer merely a technical inheritance to be assessed for compliance, but a bill already paid that demolition would write off. Set against the rest of this issue, it asks the same question the Tallinn biennale puts on its banner — How Much? Cost is not settled once at completion but runs the length of a building's life, and a 1960s tower is worth keeping precisely because the carbon price of rebuilding it is one nobody can afford today.

Spatial strategy: a tower designed for equipment, now asked to hold people. BT Tower was built as a microwave relay tower: circular in plan, a concrete core at its centre, and a narrow ring around it sized for equipment rather than occupation. The published typical floor plan for the main tower shows just four rooms arranged around that core — a number that states the difficulty plainly. A narrow circular ring cannot accommodate the double-loaded corridor of a conventional hotel; room depth and orientation are dictated entirely by the geometry, and the room types resist standardisation. The tower's key count was therefore fixed by its structure from the outset, and the hotel's economics have to work through scarcity rather than scale. This is a textbook case of constraint transmitted through a typological conversion: the existing structure does not merely shape the elevation, it determines the commercial logic of the new use.

Construction and heritage: subtract from the tower, add to the podium. The works fall into three categories. Subtraction: removing the modern additions accumulated since the 1960s to reinstate the tower's historic character — with listed buildings, taking later material away is generally an easier consent to win than adding new. Alteration: redesigning the facade at the top of the tower. Addition: extending the podium buildings by three to four storeys and putting a heated, infinity-edge pool on the podium roof. Concentrating new volume on the podium rather than the tower is standard practice on a listed structure: the tower's silhouette is the thing being protected and cannot move, while the podium is the part able to absorb change. The pool follows the model of MCR's TWA Hotel in New York, with priority for hotel guests and public booking subject to availability.

Context and consequence: public access carries the application. BT Tower was completed in 1964, and the rooftop revolving restaurant has been closed to the public since an IRA bombing in 1971 — the current application does not propose restoring it. The public commitments sit elsewhere: the 35th-floor viewing gallery reopens to the public for the first time in nearly 50 years, and a new public square is created at street level. "Our vision has always been to honour the BT Tower's remarkable history while reimagining its future," said MCR's Tyler Morse. Those commitments remain at application stage, and whether they are delivered depends on consent and on execution nine years from now. As a disciplinary sample, though, the case is complete: a piece of infrastructure made redundant by technological change is being argued back into use on three grounds at once — carbon, heritage and public accessibility. Those three are becoming the standard configuration for getting a large British retrofit through planning.

Can Amor de la Isla in Mallorca: broken tiles recomposed into terrazzo, oil funnels as lampshades, and paint as the main new material
Sources: designboom · KOSMOS Architects · Blanca Gardelegui
Can Amor de la Isla, a townhouse in the village of Banyalbufar in Mallorca's Serra de Tramuntana, has been renovated by KOSMOS Architects with Blanca Gardelegui. The house sits on a steep hillside reachable only by narrow pedestrian routes, and the difficulty of getting materials up it became the direct justification for reusing what was already there. The inventory is specific: broken ceramic floor tiles cut and recomposed into a single large terrazzo surface; an existing timber door cut in half to make a folding one; discarded oil funnels and ceramic roof tiles turned into lampshades; a cast-iron manhole cover used as the base of the stove; rebar caps as door handles; traditional Mediterranean chain curtains in place of solid kitchen doors, screening appliances without closing off the view; and existing windows and facades repaired rather than replaced. A pulley system on the facade lifts supplies vertically through the compact house, with an added spherical light that doubles as a night-time marker. "Paint is used as the main new material," the architects note — spatial relationships are established through colour rather than structural alteration. Photography by Luis Díaz Díaz.

Urbanism & Planning URBANISM
In Depth
Fourteen miles of freight track, 18 new stations, $5.5 billion: the Interborough Express takes on a problem the subway has left unsolved for a century
Cross-checked: The Architect's Newspaper · Gothamist · amNewYork · Planetizen · Brownstoner · PIX11 · Office of the Governor of New York

On 9 September 2026, Governor Kathy Hochul announced that the Interborough Express had advanced in design and environmental review, and the MTA released station renderings. The project is a 14-mile light rail line from the Brooklyn Army Terminal in Bay Ridge to Roosevelt Avenue in Jackson Heights, with 18 stations, an estimated cost of $5.5 billion, and Jacobs and HDR as architects.

Planning concept: not a new line, but the activation of one that has existed for over a century. The IBX route was not drawn from scratch. It follows existing CSX and Long Island Rail Road freight corridors, principally the Bay Ridge Branch. That choice determines the character of the entire project: no large-scale land acquisition, no tunnel boring beneath dense neighbourhoods, and therefore a cost and schedule in a completely different order of magnitude from any new subway line. The price is that the alignment is set by history rather than by present demand — route and station positions can only be adjusted within the existing corridor. This is infrastructure reuse in the strict sense, and it shares its logic with the rest of today's issue: the existing thing precedes the design, and the design's job is to judge what it can still carry.

Network strategy: the missing link in a radial system. The New York subway is radial, centred on Manhattan, which has long forced travel between Brooklyn and Queens to detour through it. IBX is the crosstown connection. The official figures: more than 900,000 residents along the corridor, roughly 160,000 projected daily riders, and transfers to 17 subway lines, 51 existing bus routes and the LIRR. The value of the line is not its own ridership but the fact that it strings 17 nodes of the existing network into a lateral ring — a single branch changing the topology of the whole map. For planning students, it is one of the clearest contemporary cases of network effect outweighing line effect.

Station design: surface stations, and accessibility written into the standard. The renderings show glass-and-steel outdoor stations with multiple elevators, digital displays and subway transfers. The MTA's station standards list durable floor tiles, elevators with wider cabs, enhanced lighting, digital signage and improved audio announcements. The item to notice is the elevators. Accessibility coverage across the existing subway remains low and retrofitting is extraordinarily expensive; IBX's 18 stations are new-build and accessible from the first drawing. In New York that is a policy outcome, not merely a technical clause. The decision to stay at grade matters too: surface stations can be light, daylit and cost-controlled, but they must resolve their edges against the blocks on either side — and the freight corridor runs largely through industrial and warehouse land, where 18 new stations will put land-use pressure on their surroundings immediately.

Timeline and dispute: design accelerates, service does not. Speaking to amNewYork on 10 September, the MTA chair said that even with the design phase speeding up, IBX is at least five years from carrying riders; the agency's earlier guidance put service in the 2030s. That gap is the part of this story most worth reading properly. A rendering release is usually taken to mean a project is nearly built, but for a $5.5 billion scheme still in environmental review, renderings function as a bid for political and public support, not as a construction announcement. The span of years between proposal and possible opening is the thing to remember: at city scale, what separates drawing from reality is rarely design capability. It is funding, approvals and political cycles.

Materials & Technology MATERIALS
In Depth
Bamboo, shields and rain covers: Studio Saar and Atelier One engineer three low-tech materials into a gridshell on a London street
Cross-checked: Architecture Today · London Design Festival · Dezeen · Wallpaper* · Aesthetica · Elle Decoration UK

The 24th London Design Festival runs from 12 to 20 September 2026 across eleven design districts. This year's Landmark Project, The Pangolin Shield, is a collaboration between architecture practice Studio Saar and structural engineers Atelier One, supported by Secure, sited on Strand Aldwych — the pedestrianised street beside Somerset House.

Structure: the gridshell is the only form that lets bamboo span. Bamboo's mechanics are specific: high tensile strength, reasonable compressive strength, but small sections with natural taper and curvature, so a single member carries bending poorly. That is exactly what a gridshell addresses. It converts loads from bending into axial compression and tension along a curved surface, letting a large number of slender members act together through a doubly-curved grid. It is why contemporary bamboo engineering almost always arrives at gridshells, tensegrity or reciprocal systems and almost never at post-and-beam frames. Atelier One has long worked on unconventional structural systems, and reconciling that geometry with bamboo's material inconsistency to a buildable tolerance is the technical content of this project — what the organisers describe as demonstrating how a traditional material can be engineered into a precise contemporary structure while keeping embodied carbon low.

The skin: two traditional Indian objects treated as components, not ornament. The covering is layered from Indian police shields and the woven rain covers known as "knups", used by farmers in north-eastern India. Overlapped across the shell like a pangolin's scales, they form a canopy that shades while admitting light. The insight is that these objects were already designed as discrete units for covering and protection: they have curvature, lapping edges and a degree of stiffness, which makes them functional equivalents of roof tiles before anyone touches them. The design work is not applying them to a surface but recognising the constructional properties they already carry in their original context, then reassembling them at a different scale. As an answer to the recurring question of how to bring vernacular materials into contemporary practice, this is the uncommon correct one: inherit the constructional logic rather than imitate the form.

Concept: the name does half the argument. The pangolin is the world's most trafficked mammal, and its overlapping scales are both its natural armour and the reason it is hunted — the protective mechanism is also the source of vulnerability. The installation takes its name from that, and sets out to examine protection and vulnerability, craft and technology, East and West, along with displacement and cultural exchange. Laying police shields, objects of authority and suppression, alongside agricultural rain covers, objects of livelihood and shelter, on a single surface is the argument itself. It should be said that encoding politics into construction this way depends on the viewer knowing where these objects come from; without the wall text, it reads first and foremost as a handsome bamboo canopy.

Consequence: urban installations are becoming the open test bed for low-carbon construction. Read this alongside two other stories in today's issue and the pattern sharpens: The Black Schoolhouse in New Orleans uses five recycled shipping containers as primary structure, and the Re:Concrete pavilion in Mumbai cuts and reassembles municipal precast drainage pipes into a demountable civic room. All three refuse the default path of new material plus new component, taking structural performance instead from existing objects, low-tech craft or reusable parts. The difference is risk. Installations are temporary, with low load and durability demands, so they can run ahead of the codes; a permanent building reproducing the same logic still has to clear fire, durability and inspection standards. The real function of an event like the London Design Festival is to give these not-yet-standardised methods a place to be seen and tested in public.

Re:Concrete in Mumbai: 2.1-metre municipal precast drainage pipes cut, rotated and suspended into a demountable civic room
Sources: designboom · NewsBeep
Re:Concrete, a competition-winning proposal on Mumbai's coast, turns the precast concrete drainage pipes normally buried beneath the city into an open civic room above ground. The components are standard precast pipe sections roughly 2.1 metres in diameter and 2.5 metres long; cut, rotated and suspended at varying heights, the reclaimed cylinders serve as walls, thresholds, seating and circular light wells. The proposed process begins by identifying and inspecting surplus, rejected or decommissioned pipes that remain structurally sound, and only then proceeds to assembly. The assembly itself is modular, dry-connected and reversible — the configuration can expand, contract or be reorganised according to the quantity and condition of pipes available locally. The question it tests is a narrow and useful one: can concrete enter a second architectural life without first being crushed back into aggregate?

Academia & Exhibitions ACADEMIA
In Depth
"How Much?": Tallinn takes cost off the closing balance sheet and puts it in the middle of the gallery — and the gallery is the best exhibit
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · Archisearch · TAB · Parametric Architecture · D5 MAG · Estonian Centre for Architecture

The eighth Tallinn Architecture Biennale (TAB 2026) opened on 9 September 2026 and runs to 30 November, with an opening week from 9 to 13 September. The theme is "How Much?", curated by Kertu Johanna Jõeste, Ra Martin Puhkan and Siim Tanel Tõnisson of Estonia's Stuudio TÄNA with Mark Aleksander Fischer (Estonia) and Mira Samonig (Austria).

The premise: turning "cheap" from an insult back into a subject of study. Architecture exhibitions habitually show outcomes — photographs, models, drawings — with cost appearing on the last line of the label, if at all. TAB 2026 inverts that order. The show examines the relationship between constraint, cost and architecture: how economic pressure shapes the built environment, and how it then defines what counts as architectural value. The premise holds because the discipline's central anxiety over the past decade has shifted from form to affordability. Housing crises, construction inflation, rising material and labour costs — each compresses the room a designer has to work in. Putting "how much" on the banner concedes that this is no longer a technical appendix but the starting condition.

Venue as argument: Linnahall states the case. The main curatorial exhibition is staged in Linnahall, the concrete megastructure built in Tallinn for the sailing events of the 1980 Moscow Olympics and largely unused since — one of the Baltic region's best-known pieces of stranded heritage. Housing an exhibition about cost and value inside a building that cannot be brought back into service because maintenance and renovation are too expensive requires no wall text to explain: it is venue, exhibit and counter-example at once. A building can be judged worth it on completion and shelved four decades later because nobody can afford to repair it. Cost is never a single calculation; it is a judgement running the length of a building's life. That is the sentence the exhibition speaks through its site.

The schools' show: five institutions, one question. Running in parallel, the International Architecture Schools' Exhibition "Capital-A Affordable Architecture" is at the Estonian Museum of Architecture from 10 September to 30 November, with work from the University of Applied Arts Vienna, UMPRUM Prague, the Royal College of Art London, the Kharkiv School of Architecture and the Estonian Academy of Arts. The wordplay in the title is worth unpacking: capital-A Architecture, the discipline as art, set against affordable, and aimed squarely at a long-standing split. What enters the histories is generally work built without regard to cost, while the overwhelming majority of the built environment is produced under severe budget constraint — and the latter has barely entered the teaching canon. Setting five schools the same question is itself a question put to the curriculum. The participation of the Kharkiv School of Architecture carries additional weight here: discussing affordable architecture under wartime conditions, the constraint is not an academic premise.

Why students should care: this is the year's most useful inverted case library. The practical value of TAB 2026 is not new form but a body of projects that start from constraint. Conventional case study trains you to realise an intention; an exhibition like this trains you to decide what gets abandoned and what must be kept when resources fall short — which is the judgement most professional practice actually makes daily. The biennale also runs Installation and Vision Competitions, whose winners were announced earlier and offer a second line of reading alongside the main show. The exhibition continues to 30 November; for readers in Europe or with autumn travel plans, it is a rare instance of economics placed directly on the gallery floor.

The 2026 London Design Festival runs from 12 to 20 September, nine days spread across eleven design districts, with forums, talks, product launches, open showrooms, exhibitions, installations and workshops covering fashion, architecture, interiors, furniture, technology, materiality, art, craft and digital design. Sources: Dezeen Events Guide · Archisearch · TAB · Estonian Centre for Architecture
Part of the Tallinn Architecture Biennale, the International Architecture Schools' Exhibition "Capital-A Affordable Architecture" is on view at the Estonian Museum of Architecture from 10 September to 30 November 2026, gathering work by students and tutors from five institutions: the University of Applied Arts Vienna, UMPRUM Prague, the Royal College of Art London, the Kharkiv School of Architecture and the Estonian Academy of Arts. The title sets capital-A Architecture — the discipline as art — against affordable, pointing at a persistent split in architectural education: the work that enters the canon is largely built without regard to cost, while most of the built environment is produced on tight budgets. The schools' show is a fixture of TAB and has long served as a cross-section of what European architecture education is currently preoccupied with.
