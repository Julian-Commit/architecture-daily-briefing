EDITOR'S NOTE

The day's most consequential event happened on Fifth Avenue: on 15 September the Metropolitan Museum of Art broke ground on the Oscar L. Tang and H.M. Agnes Hsu-Tang Wing, making Mexican architect Frida Escobedo the first woman to design a wing in the museum's 156-year history. Her answer to the facade is a set of celosías — a limestone lattice proportioned 80 per cent solid, 10 per cent clear glass, 10 per cent stone screen with glass behind it. One assembly is asked to do three jobs at once: shade the galleries, frame Central Park, and protect the collection.

Three other stories are likewise about method rather than subject. The Benghazi 2050 plan delivered by a seven-party consortium led by Zaha Hadid Architects is not a master plan but a Digital Planning Hub meant to keep running after handover. Intro Columbus topped out at 44 CLT panels a day to become the tallest mass timber student housing in the United States, ten weeks ahead of target. And a new ETH Zürich study throws usefully cold water on low-carbon concrete: what governs how fast the rebar corrodes is mostly climate, not the mix.

80 per cent solid, 10 per cent clear glass, 10 per cent stone screen: Escobedo compresses shading, framing and conservation into a single facade
Cross-checked: The Architect's Newspaper (16 Sept) · The Art Newspaper (15 Sept) · ARTnews · Hyperallergic · 6sqft · Archinect

Concept: unfinishedness as a starting point. Escobedo's point of departure was the museum's own uncarved stone. On the Fifth Avenue facade, blocks above the columns that were meant to be carved into figures were left permanently as rough masses when funds ran out — the Met's most famous evidence of incompletion. Her formulation: "It suggested the idea of becoming — that architecture, and perhaps institutions too, are never entirely finished. They continue changing, accumulating, and adding meaning." This is not decoration on a press release. A building that must replace the former Lila Acheson Wallace Wing and stay inside the existing footprint begins from the premise that a museum is a thing repeatedly rewritten.

Material and assembly: the three-part ratio. The facade borrows from celosías — the Spanish term for a lattice or screen, long used in Mexican architecture and traceable to Arab and North African traditions of shading screens. Escobedo translates it into limestone. Per 6sqft, the elevation is proportioned at 80 per cent solid, 10 per cent clear glass, and 10 per cent stone screen with glass behind it. That ratio has to reconcile three requirements that pull against one another: galleries for modern and contemporary art need tight control of direct light to protect the collection; visitors need a visual connection to Central Park; and the envelope is expected to shade the building and cut energy use. The value of a lattice is precisely that it is not a binary of open and closed. The thickness of the stone creates depth, depth produces self-shading, light is attenuated rather than cut off, and sightlines are released at particular angles.

Spatial strategy: a three-storey base and two setbacks. The five-storey volume reads as a three-storey base, a recessed fourth floor and a further setback at the fifth. The direct product of that section is 18,500 square feet of terrace across the upper floors. The existing Iris and B. Gerald Cantor Roof Garden grows from 7,500 to 10,000 square feet and moves to the fourth floor, with a café on the fifth. The nature of that change deserves attention: the Cantor Roof Garden had been open only during the warmer months, and after relocation and expansion the outdoor rooms facing the Manhattan skyline and Central Park become available year-round. For a museum receiving visitors in the millions, converting seasonal space into year-round space is a gain as real as added gallery area.

Background and consequence. The 69,500 square feet of new exhibition space enlarges the twentieth- and twenty-first-century galleries by close to half — long considered the Met's weak flank relative to MoMA and the Whitney. The $670 million has been raised in full and entirely privately, covering construction and part of an endowment, within an overall transformation of roughly $1.5 billion; the wing opens in 2030. For students, what is worth retaining is the constraint set: no enlargement of the footprint, no alteration to the museum's established presence on Fifth Avenue, and a contemporary building to be placed beside a Classical elevation. The answer offered is a screen with thickness, not a form.

La Salle School, Bogotá: 26,095 square metres in which FP Arquitectura treats void, landscape and circulation as teaching instruments
Sources: ArchDaily (16 Sept) · FP Arquitectura
The Colombian office FP Arquitectura has completed the La Salle School in Bogotá: 26,095 square metres, finished in 2025, for the client Colegio de La Salle, with lead architects Iván Forgioni, José Puentes and Juan José López and Constructora Marval as general contractor. This is a well-established Latin American approach: in a temperate climate, corridors, terraces and courtyards are made semi-outdoor and carry the social programme, reducing the interior area that requires mechanical conditioning.

Delivering a dynamic digital twin instead of a master plan: Benghazi 2050 turns planning from a finished product into a tool that keeps running
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (17 Sept) · designboom (17 Sept) · Zaha Hadid Architects · Prior+Partners · Buro Happold · Systematica

Concept: changing what a plan hands over. The notable thing about Benghazi 2050: Next Generation City Plan is not its form but the nature of its deliverable. A conventional master plan is a product frozen at one moment: it hardens a set of assumptions into drawings and targets, the city then departs from those assumptions, and the drawings lose validity year by year. Here the two outputs are a Vision and Spatial Strategy plus a Digital Planning Hub, the latter explicitly described as a dynamic digital twin — something that evolves as new information is added rather than dating as time passes. ArchDaily and designboom both stress the same point: the proposal defines no single architectural intervention.

What is inside the hub. As disclosed by the team, the platform integrates geographic information systems (GIS), three-dimensional urban models, population data, land-use information, transport networks, infrastructure detail, environmental resilience studies and future planning scenarios. Its practical function is to provide a shared evidence base, so that a specific proposal can be weighed within the context of the whole city rather than approved in isolation. The significance is governmental more than technical. Benghazi has been through prolonged conflict and destruction, which makes baseline data on tenure, population and existing built conditions a scarce resource in its own right — and multiple parties each holding their own version of that data is a common starting point for planning failure.

Spatial strategy: connection over fragmentation, restoration over demolition. The vision's principles are summarised as connection over fragmentation and restoration over demolition. Three concrete components follow: public transport and pedestrian routes stitching together the historic core, the waterfront, residential neighbourhoods and metropolitan centres; new employment and knowledge districts to absorb growth; and nature zones integrated into the city to support water management and ecological diversity. Benghazi is a historic port city on Libya's Mediterranean coast, and one explicit aim is to reconnect the city to its waterfront and its historic centre — the two things port cities generally lost over the twentieth century.

Division of labour, and why it matters. The consortium's allocation of roles is worth recording on its own: A&O TEC and Prior+Partners on regional and urban planning, economic strategy and governance; Homa on land use, built form and socioeconomic strategy; Systematica on transport and mobility; Buro Happold on the natural environment and utilities; Fichtner on energy and infrastructure; Silicon Shore on web services and hosting; and ZHA on spatial vision, scenario design, digital platform interface design and technology integration. In other words, ZHA's role here is not to supply form but to integrate a spatial vision with a digital interface — a shift in position worth noting for a practice known above all for its formal language. The whole framework is defined as adaptable, allowing the city's transformation to be measured, evaluated and adjusted continuously through to 2050.

Austin City Council voted unanimously on 10 September 2026 to require that projects seeking to build larger than normally permitted near the city's future light rail stations include affordable housing on site. In Depth
Forty-four CLT panels a day: what Intro Columbus actually achieved is not being tallest but handing the saved weeks to the other trades
Cross-checked: The Architect's Newspaper (16 Sept) · Construction Dive (16 Sept) · Building Design+Construction · Wood Central · DLR Group · Engineering News-Record

Structure: one concrete podium, twelve timber floors. Intro Columbus stands at High Street and Ninth Avenue in Columbus, Ohio, next to The Ohio State University: 13 storeys, 158 feet (about 48 metres), 242,000 square feet, 186 units and 493 beds. The sectional logic is the most common one in tall timber today — a single-storey concrete podium takes the commercial frontage, fire and wet-area requirements, with twelve fully timber floors above. The frame is prefabricated glued laminated timber (glulam) columns and beams with cross-laminated timber (CLT) floor panels, joined wood-to-wood with Simpson Strong-Tie metal connectors. The timber was manufactured by SmartLam North America in Alabama; DLR Group served as design architect, architect of record and interior designer; Harbor Bay Ventures is developer, Elford Construction general contractor, and Forefront Structural Engineers the structural engineer.

Schedule: 44 panels a day, two and a half floors every two to three weeks. The building topped out on 8 September 2026, roughly ten weeks ahead of an original November target, about a year from groundbreaking to structural completion. The governing figure is an average of 44 pre-cut CLT panels installed per day, advancing two and a half floors every two to three weeks. It is worth pausing on the causation. CLT is fast not because wood is light but because the panels are cut in a factory and arrive needing only lifting and connecting — site work shifts from fabrication to assembly. Forefront Structural's principal put the consequence plainly: "Every day we save on the superstructure is a day the other trades get back, and that compounds." That sentence explains what ten weeks early actually means: enclosure, interiors and MEP begin in parallel sooner, rather than the calendar simply moving forward.

Carbon and provenance: the number that gets skipped. Building Design+Construction reports a projected reduction of 1,128 metric tons of embodied carbon relative to conventional concrete-and-steel construction; Wood Central describes the same figure as carbon sequestered in the frame. The two framings point to the same order of magnitude but count different things, which is worth flagging when citing it. More transferable is the provenance: manufacturing and sourcing through SmartLam in Alabama cut transport-related emissions by roughly 55 per cent compared with European timber alternatives. That holds for timber projects anywhere, including those now advancing in China — in the embodied carbon ledger of long-span and tall timber, transoceanic shipping is routinely underestimated, and it happens to be the one line a procurement decision can change directly.

Why student housing. The project is the tallest mass timber student housing in the United States and the first mass timber building in Columbus, with delivery planned for summer 2027 for the 2027–28 academic year. There are good reasons student housing became the opening for tall timber. Unit layouts repeat heavily, which suits modular prefabrication; the construction period must align strictly with the academic calendar, so schedule certainty carries unusual value; and occupants are receptive to exposed wood — half the building retains exposed timber ceilings, including all bedrooms and living rooms, meaning the timber is simultaneously structure, finish and schedule strategy, and the suspended ceiling trade disappears. The distance between a demonstration project and standard practice is usually closed by exactly this kind of highly repetitive building type.

New ETH study: in low-carbon concrete, corrosion rate is governed mostly by climate rather than mix — wet can be 100 times faster than dry
Sources: ETH Zürich (September) · Nature Communications · TechXplore · Anthropocene Magazine
Cristhiana Albert and colleagues at ETH Zürich have published "Rethinking concrete durability for low-carbon concretes through climate-informed corrosion modelling" in Nature Communications. The modelling framework quantitatively couples time-resolved climate data, moisture transport within low-carbon concrete, and steel corrosion kinetics, examining three concrete mixes across four locations with sharply different climates: Zurich, Bergen, Manaus and Huailai, China. The findings come in two layers. The initial carbonation phase may indeed proceed faster in low-carbon concrete; but after carbonation, what determines how quickly the embedded steel rusts is principally weather and humidity rather than the mix — steel in wet concrete can corrode up to 100 times faster than steel in dry concrete, while the differences among the three mixes studied had a far smaller effect. The study therefore points at an institutional problem: current standards aim to protect steel from corrosion for as long as possible, and in practice that orientation disadvantages environmentally preferable new concretes relative to traditional ones, hindering their adoption.
The original 1968 moulds, restored and reopened: a brand-new Futuro House lands in a Helsinki botanic garden
Sources: Archipanic · Helsinki Design Week · Marimekko · Time Out Helsinki
The Futuro House, designed by the Finnish architect Matti Suuronen in 1968, is an elliptical prefabricated dwelling of fibreglass-reinforced plastic (FRP) raised on legs, conceived to be lightweight, easily transported and installable almost anywhere. Its manufacturer, Polykem, built about twenty before going out of business in 1973. What is new here is a manufacturing story: a small team learned that the original moulds still existed some 300 kilometres north of Helsinki, drove up, reached an agreement with the family that had kept them, restored the moulds and built a new Futuro exactly as the originals were built. During Helsinki Design Week the unit lands in the Kaisaniemi Botanic Garden, its interior reimagined by Marimekko using prints by the Danish textile artist and designer Vibeke Rohland, created during a year-long residency at the company's printing factory in Herttoniemi. Revivals of 1960s plastic prefabrication usually stop at replicas or models; this one went as far as reopening the original tooling.
Structure as teaching aid: SOM's Grimes Engineering Center at Berkeley exposes every connection, including a shape memory alloy tension-rod system
Sources: The Architect's Newspaper (16 Sept) · SOM · AIA California (2026 Design Award) · Daily Californian
The Grimes Engineering Center, designed by SOM (Skidmore, Owings & Merrill) for the University of California, Berkeley, is a 35,570-square-foot glazed addition costing roughly $110 million, funded entirely by philanthropy. It opened on 21 April 2025 and has received a 2026 AIA California Design Award; The Architect's Newspaper published a feature assessment on 16 September 2026. The building overlooks Memorial Glade at the centre of campus, sitting on the axis running from the Campanile to the Classical Core, its transparent volume set against the Brutalist character of the original building below it. The detail that should interest architecture and engineering students is a construction decision: the structural systems are intentionally exposed, so that engineering students can directly examine connections, gussets, braces, a rod-hung stair, and a shape memory alloy (SMA) tension-rod system. Shape memory alloys recover their original form after being deformed under load; their value in seismic tension rods lies in small residual deformation after an earthquake. Installing a still-emerging component of this kind in a teaching building and leaving it visible effectively turns the building itself into a teaching aid.
Five $1m prizes aimed at construction technology: Airbnb's accelerator makes building faster and cheaper its own separate track
Sources: Airbnb newsroom (14 Sept) · The Architect's Newspaper (15 Sept) · Smart Cities Dive
Of the three tracks within the $250 million Housing Accelerator, the third — alongside capital deployment and policy advocacy — is innovation: Airbnb will establish five awards of $1 million each for innovators and entrepreneurs developing technologies and designs that make homes easier, faster and cheaper to build. The track is worth the attention of readers following materials and construction because of where the money comes from. Over the past several years, demonstration projects in 3D-printed construction, modular prefabrication and cement-free binders have depended largely on government subsidy, university research funding or building-products companies' own budgets. This is prize money from a platform company, judged explicitly against the cost of delivering a single home. Criteria shape which technologies rise: measured by easier, faster and cheaper, the likely beneficiaries are repeatable and scalable methods rather than one-off demonstrations.

In Depth
Moving the origin of Brutalism off Europe: 28 countries, 140 buildings, eight essays, and a question about whose modernity
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (17 Sept) · Blue Crow Media (publisher) · STIRworld · Prestel (Brutalist Japan, same field) · MoMA (West African modernism, for comparison)

What the book is arguing with. The standard narrative of Brutalism begins in Europe: the term derives from the French béton brut, was consolidated into a movement in 1950s Britain by Alison and Peter Smithson and the critic Reyner Banham, and spread outward from there. What Brutalist Asia takes on is the assumption buried in "spread outward" — that Asia's fair-faced concrete architecture was a passively received stylistic import. The book's central contention is that architects across Asia did not transplant a style but "adopted, questioned, and transformed" the language in response to their own political, climatic and cultural conditions. The argument is not about whether Brutalism existed in Asia, but about where its origin point is placed.

Scope and structure. Published by Blue Crow Media as part of the press's long-running Brutalist and Modernist series, the book ships from mid-September 2026. It covers 28 countries, 140 buildings and more than 150 photographs, with eight original essays by Bob Pang, John Barr, Bhawna Dandona, Christele Harrouk, Kyung Taek Lee, Caryn Paredes-Santillan, Pinai Sirikiatikul, Soroush Mohajery and Amirhosein Rad, treating Japan, Korea, Hong Kong, Thailand, the Philippines, India, Iran and Lebanon respectively. That table of contents is itself part of the argument: placing Hong Kong beside Beirut, and Tehran beside Manila, means no longer sorting by distance from London.

The selection: the known and the barely printed. The book includes acknowledged landmarks — Le Corbusier's Palace of Assembly at Chandigarh, Kenzo Tange's Yoyogi National Gymnasium in Tokyo, Khalil Khoury's Interdesign Building in Beirut (1973–97) — alongside a substantial body of work that has rarely appeared in publication, such as Joseph Philippe Karam's The Egg, Raoul Verney's Red Cross (1973) and Khalil Khoury's Manar Complex (1982). The range of architects is wide: Kenzo Tange, Paul Rudolph, Balkrishna V. Doshi, Raj Rewal, Charles Correa, Kim Swoo Geun, Leandro V. Locsin and Le Corbusier, together with a cohort of Lebanese practitioners — Joseph Philippe Karam, Khalil Khoury, Georges Khoury, Antoine Romanos and Grégoire Serof. The density of the Lebanese material deserves particular notice: Beirut's modernist output of the 1960s and 1970s has long been obscured by the narrative of the civil war.

Why the book is useful in a studio. Fair-faced concrete spread through Asia for a set of reasons distinct from Europe's. Climate: heavy walls and deep overhangs carry real thermal value in hot, humid, high-insolation regions. Economy: in newly independent states without mature steel supply chains, concrete was the more obtainable material. Politics: large public buildings were a direct means for new nations to assert modernity and autonomy. MoMA's Architects of Liberation: Modernism in Western Africa — some 450 works, seven countries, 50-plus lenders, through 2 January 2027
Sources: MoMA exhibition page · The Architect's Newspaper (15 Sept) · Architectural Record · Domus
Architects of Liberation: Modernism in Western Africa opened at the Museum of Modern Art on 5 July 2026 and runs through 2 January 2027 in the Robert B. Menschel Galleries on the museum's third floor; The Architect's Newspaper published an assessment on 15 September. It is the first major exhibition to examine African modern architecture from the late 1950s through the early 1980s in the context of political independence in the region, spanning seven countries — Benin, Cameroon, Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, Nigeria, Senegal and Togo — with approximately 450 architectural drawings, scale models and archival photographs drawn from the collections of more than 50 lenders across 17 countries. It is organised by Martino Stierli, The Philip Johnson Chief Curator of Architecture and Design, with guest curator Ikem Stanley Okoye (associate professor, University of Delaware) and curatorial associate Mallory Cohen. The exhibition's argument is that newly independent African states wanted to project, both inward and outward, a forward-looking, progressive and cosmopolitan self-image, and that modern architecture carried that aspiration while engaging the political ideas of Pan-Africanism and Africanization.

The essay takes the houses and everyday scenes of Studio Ghibli's animation as its analytical object, considering how transitional or liminal spaces — porches, entry vestibules, kitchens, stairs — carry the ritual function of returning home, and how the films treat the ordinary itself as a source of the sacred through repeated depiction of mundane domestic actions and spatial sequences. Spatial analysis using fictional imagery of this kind is a common entry exercise in architectural theory courses; its value lies less in the conclusion than in the requirement to reduce a felt quality back to specific spatial elements and sequences.
