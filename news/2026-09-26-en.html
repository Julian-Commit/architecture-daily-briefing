EDITOR'S NOTE

Today's issue asks one question from four sides: for whom does architecture solve problems, and how does that work get handed on. In Stavyshche, Ukraine, a disused school has become 42 permanent apartments for families displaced by the war — housing, not a shelter — with roughly 800 square metres of photovoltaics on a new timber roof, 850 square metres of asphalt prised off the courtyard, and 200 square metres kept open to the whole town. A year after its founder's death, the American landscape practice Coen+Partners has completed a generational handover, its three equal partners holding work that ranges from a single Minneapolis plaza to a 124-kilometre corridor in Riyadh. In Hokuto, Japan, a 47.1-square-metre shrine office treats seismic design as a matter of not fixing the column bases. And "Peter Salter: Drawings" opens today at the Architectural Association, showing previously unseen drawings from 1985 to 2020 by an architect celebrated for drawing and known for very few buildings. One editorial note: our duplicate check found that MALBA's underground expansion, the Met's Tang Wing and the Jencks Award have all run recently in this briefing, so they are not repeated here.

Office for Future turns a disused school in Stavyshche, Ukraine, into 42 apartments: 1,900 square metres for displaced families, some 800 square metres of photovoltaics on a new timber roof, 850 square metres of asphalt prised off the courtyard
Sources: ArchDaily (25 Sept) · Office for Future (architects, Florence / Lausanne) · Charity Foundation Housing for IDP (client and funder)
Titled "Dvir – The Shared Courtyard," the project is in Stavyshche, Kyiv Oblast, Ukraine, designed by Office for Future, a practice with offices in Florence and Lausanne; ArchDaily published it on 25 September. A disused school building of roughly 1,900 square metres has been converted into 42 apartments for families who are internally displaced persons (IDPs), plus around 200 square metres of shared indoor space. There are four apartment types, from studios to three-bedroom units, including fully accessible apartments for people with disabilities. The technical strategy pairs the existing structure with new timber: selective internal demolition reorganises the plan, while a freestanding timber walkway added outside handles access to every apartment without consuming interior area. A new timber roof carries approximately 800 square metres of photovoltaic panels, and the envelope has been given a full energy retrofit. The site moves are equally specific — roughly 850 square metres of asphalt have been removed (desealing), with some of the existing pavement retained as pedestrian paths through the landscaped courtyard. Shared facilities include children's play areas, meeting rooms and space for cultural activities, open to local residents and not only to tenants, so the building continues to serve as a gathering place for the town. The funder is the Charity Foundation Housing for IDP; this is the third such intervention it has promoted in Ukraine.

A year after its founder's death, landscape practice Coen+Partners completes a generational handover: three equal partners take over, headcount has more than doubled since 2019 to about 45, and Riyadh's $23 billion, 124-kilometre Sports Boulevard is on the boards
Sources: The Architect's Newspaper (25 Sept) · Coen+Partners partner expansion announcement · Coen+Partners founder memorial page (22 Oct 2025)
The American landscape architecture firm Coen+Partners has completed its transition to a new generation of leadership. Founder Shane Coen died in 2025 at the age of 61; the practice is now led by three equal partners — Thomas Doyle, Robin Ganser and McKenzie Wilhelm — supported by seven directors. The ownership transition began in 2022, and the three became equal owners in 2023. The firm has more than doubled in size since 2019 to around 45 staff, with offices in Minneapolis and Riyadh, Saudi Arabia; in 2024 it was split internally into three studios by type of work. On the handover, Doyle says: "Shane was a presence, and he is missed. But from an operations and design-ethos standpoint, we are continuing the mission." The range of current and recent work is instructive: Peavey Plaza in Minneapolis, a rehabilitation of the downtown landmark completed in 2019 after more than twelve months of community engagement; the Crystal Bridges expansion in Bentonville, Arkansas, completed in 2025, adding 114,000 square feet and growing the campus footprint by half; Sports Boulevard in Riyadh, converting a power corridor into roughly 124 kilometres (77 miles) of public space as part of Saudi Vision 2030, at a reported $23 billion; and The Shenandoah in Virginia, a 640-acre wellness and hospitality centre with 120 guest rooms, ongoing. The practice keeps the position it was founded on, which it calls "contextual minimalism."

Materials
A 47.1-square-metre shrine office makes seismic design a matter of unfixed column bases: Sobokuya combines ishiba-date and niho-korobi in Yamanashi, leaning every column outward in two directions and resting the feet on bare stone
Sources: designboom (24 Sept) · ArchDaily project page · Archello · Arch2O · Sobokuya Inc. (design and construction)
The building sits within the precincts of Fujitake Shrine in Hokuto, Yamanashi Prefecture, Japan, and serves as the shrine office — the administrative space for day-to-day shrine business. Total floor area is just 47.1 square metres. Sobokuya Inc. both designed and built it, working with PRIVATE Architect Studio; the lead designers are Hisashi Imai and Yuko Sano, and it was completed in 2025. Two traditional Japanese timber techniques are used together. The first is ishiba-date, in which column feet are left entirely unanchored and rest directly on stone footings. The second is niho-korobi, in which every column leans uniformly outward in two directions. Combined, they send loads along diagonal vectors, while the unfixed bases let the structure shift slightly against the stone during an earthquake — designboom describes the result as "an early form of base isolation." Because the columns lean, every beam had to be located and marked in three dimensions before it could be cut. Materials are local: the exposed roof frame is larch felled on the slopes of the Yatsugatake range, with red pine taiko beams in the main frame. Sobokuya designed and hand-made the joinery in its own workshop, deliberately leaving the chisel marks visible. The roof is clad in Corten steel, chosen so that it will patinate. Deep eaves and lime plaster walls produce what the architects call a restrained, ceremonial character, and two large openings track seasonal light across the floor. The stated spatial reference is the sumo ring at Mizu-waka-su Shrine on Oki Island.

"Peter Salter: Drawings" opens today at the Architectural Association: running to 5 December, it gathers previously unseen drawings made between 1985 and 2020 by an architect with very few built works, including full-scale construction details and furniture drawings
Sources: Architectural Record listings · Architectural Association Public Programme · AEX / architecture-exhibitions.com · Wikipedia entry on Peter Salter (biography check)
The exhibition "Peter Salter: Drawings" opened on 25 September 2026 at the Architectural Association (AA) Gallery in London and runs to 5 December. It presents drawings by the architectural designer and educator Peter Salter, including a substantial body of previously unseen material produced between 1985 and 2020. As described by the organisers, the drawings balance Salter's architectural poetics with technical expertise, working across three registers: architectural construction details, atmospheric perspectives, and large-scale plans and sections. Among the exhibits are full-scale drawings of construction details and pieces of furniture — which, in the organisers' account, reveal a deep knowledge of materials and the way they behave, as well as care for how buildings come together. Salter was born in 1947 and took his Diploma at the AA in 1980. In the early 1980s he worked with Alison and Peter Smithson, and through that decade collaborated with Christopher Macdonald as Macdonald and Salter, producing unbuilt speculative projects distinguished by "highly developed and evocative drawings, which are concerned with qualities of construction and spatial atmosphere." In 1995 he became Head of School at the University of East London, succeeding Ron Herron, and since 2006 he has been Professor of Architectural Design at Cardiff University's Welsh School of Architecture. In 2004 he received the RIBA Annie Spink Award for his contribution to architectural education. He practises as Salter+Collingridge with Fenella Collingridge; their principal built work is Walmer Yard in London, four bespoke houses. Per Architectural Record, the exhibition coincides with the release of the book Peter Salter: Drawings.

In Depth
How a practice continues once its founder is gone: Coen+Partners' four-year ownership transition, three studios, and a jump in scale from one plaza to 124 kilometres
Cross-checked: The Architect's Newspaper (25 Sept) · Coen+Partners partner expansion announcement · Coen+Partners founder memorial page (22 Oct 2025) · Coen+Partners people page · ASLA "Inside the LA Studio" course material · Landezine practice archive

Design concept: what gets inherited is a position, not a style.Generational handovers at architecture and landscape practices are usually the item industry news passes over in a line — some names change, the work continues. The Coen+Partners case is worth opening up, because what is being handed over is explicitly stated as a method rather than an appearance: since its founding the practice has described its position as "contextual minimalism." Both halves of that phrase carry weight. Minimalism points to formal restraint; contextual demands that the restraint be derived from the conditions of a specific site rather than applied as a pared-back style. New partner Thomas Doyle's account of continuity after founder Shane Coen's death lands precisely on this distinction: "Shane was a presence, and he is missed. But from an operations and design-ethos standpoint, we are continuing the mission." He separates presence — what is irreplaceable about a person — from design ethos, a set of transmissible judgements.

Spatial strategy: one method, tested across three orders of magnitude.Lay the practice's key work out by scale and the range of the method becomes visible. At the small end is Peavey Plaza in Minneapolis, a rehabilitation of an existing plaza completed in 2019, where the number to remember is not an area but a process: more than twelve months of community engagement. This is a 1970s Modernist sunken plaza, so working on it means handling heritage protection, accessibility upgrades and public feeling simultaneously — very little design freedom, very high coordination cost. In the middle is the Crystal Bridges expansion in Bentonville, Arkansas, completed in 2025, adding 114,000 square feet and enlarging the campus footprint by roughly half — where the problem is keeping the addition from overwhelming a museum already known for its landscape. At the far end is Sports Boulevard in Riyadh: the conversion of an existing power corridor into a linear public space of roughly 124 kilometres (77 miles), part of Saudi Vision 2030, at a reported $23 billion. From one plaza to 124 kilometres is three orders of magnitude, and the genuinely useful question for students is whether a method founded on deriving form from site conditions still holds along a corridor that crosses an entire city — because linear infrastructure is precisely what does not have a single site.

Organisational innovation: stretch the handover across four years, and split the office by type of work.The most instructive thing about this transition is its time structure rather than its outcome. The ownership transition began in 2022, the three partners became equal owners in 2023, and the founder died in 2025 — which is to say the transfer of ownership was complete while the founder was alive, and his death followed rather than triggered it. That is the reverse of the usual script, in which a founder's death precipitates a succession crisis. The second move came in 2024, splitting the office into three studios by type of work, alongside a structure of three equal partners and seven directors. Read the size data against that: headcount has more than doubled since 2019 to around 45, across offices in Minneapolis and Riyadh. A 45-person practice with three studios, three partners and seven directors carries a high proportion of leadership — usually a deliberate choice so that each line of work has its own decision-maker, at the cost of coordination overhead. Practice governance is something students rarely encounter in school, yet it determines whether a firm can outlive its founder.

Context and consequence: two obituaries and one handover within a year.Read this against another item in today's issue: the academia section records the death of the Los Angeles architect and UCLA professor Heather Roberge at 55, and that she and Orhan Ayyüce died in succession shortly after being named AIA Los Angeles Educators of the Year for 2026. Shane Coen was 61. The cohort that founded practices in the late 1990s and early 2000s while teaching in schools is entering its handover period, and the profession has almost no institutional preparation for it — the value of most small and mid-sized design practices is bound tightly to the founder's personal reputation and client relationships, with ownership, decision-making and brand never having been separated. What makes the Coen+Partners case worth recording is that it treated those three as separate problems: ownership transferred in 2022–23, the decision structure was reorganised in 2024, and the brand and position were articulated as an inheritable "contextual minimalism." To be explicit, the valuation and specific terms of the ownership transfer have not been made public and this briefing will not speculate; nor has published reporting examined the project governance and labour questions raised by operating an office in Riyadh, on which we likewise draw no inference.

Le Grand-Espace published in Sherbrooke, Quebec: 1,370 square metres of flexible performance space shared by theatre, dance, circus and young-audience work, with theatre and acoustic consultants engaged from the design stage
Sources: ArchDaily (24 Sept) · Atelier Paul Laurendeau + NEUF architect(e)s (architects) · Photography Maxime Brouillet, Félix Michaud
The venue is in Sherbrooke, Quebec, Canada, with a floor area of 1,370 square metres, completed in 2025, designed by Atelier Paul Laurendeau with NEUF architect(e)s, and Paul Laurendeau as design architect. It is conceived as a flexible performance space, described by the architects as "capable of hosting a wide range of artistic disciplines, from theatre and dance to circus arts and productions for young audiences," with the aim of strengthening cultural provision in the Eastern Townships. What students should notice is the consultant team and how early it was engaged: structural and civil by Latéral-DWB, mechanical and electrical by Dupras Ledoux, theatre consulting by Trizart Alliance, acoustics by MJM, lighting by Light Factor. A 1,370-square-metre building carrying three separate specialist consultants for theatre systems, acoustics and lighting tells you where the difficulty in a one-room-many-uses building actually lies — not in the massing but in the equipment and the acoustics. A single volume has to deliver speech intelligibility for theatre, floor resilience for dance, and clear height plus rigging loads for circus, and those requirements frequently conflict. As of publication no construction cost or detailed structural materials have been released, and this briefing draws no inference about them.
Perkins&Will São Paulo publishes the Jeronimo Faria Lima Building: 20,500 square metres of offices and retail that decline to compete on height or silhouette, making the corner and the ground floor the subject instead
Sources: ArchDaily (25 Sept) · Perkins&Will São Paulo (lead architect Douglas Tolaine) · Photography Manuel Sá
The building stands in the Jardim Europa district of São Paulo, Brazil, at the significant intersection where Avenida Brigadeiro Faria Lima meets Jeronimo da Veiga Street. It was designed by the São Paulo office of Perkins&Will under lead architect Douglas Tolaine, with a total area of 20,500 square metres, completed in 2026, mixing office and commercial use. The architects explicitly reject the usual move for a site like this — pursuing urban prominence through height or a distinctive silhouette — and put the design effort into the surface where building meets city, stating an ambition "to make the encounter between architecture and the city a fundamental part of the experience." In practice that means activation at ground level and careful handling of pedestrian scale, supported by landscape and a dedicated lighting design; the building runs to seventeen floors and above, with basement levels below. Facade and interior materials come from suppliers including Guardian Glass, RODECA, Trespa Pura and Portobello, with Lucio Engenharia as contractor and Manuel Sá as photographer. The transferable judgement here is a pragmatic one: on a dense commercial corridor, a building's public character is usually settled by its lowest two floors, not by its skyline.
KUSKA Residence in the Peruvian Andes: 400 square metres built for a writer and an artisan while also hosting artists in residence; the name comes from the Quechua word for "together"
Sources: ArchDaily (25 Sept) · Taller MACAA (lead architect and photographer Rafael Ortiz Santos)
The project is in the Peruvian Andes, with a floor area of 400 square metres, completed in 2026, by Taller MACAA — in full, Misión de Arquitectura, Construcción y Arte en los Andes — with Rafael Ortiz Santos as both lead architect and photographer. The clients are a writer and an artisan, and the house is set up as their primary residence while also being "designed to host friends, family, and artists-in-residence," making it mixed-use rather than purely domestic. The practice's own name states its position — placing architecture, construction and art side by side and calling the whole a "mission" points to a way of working grounded in local building practice in the Andes. One caveat: as of publication the ArchDaily project page gives no account of the structural system, building materials or construction methods, and says nothing about the involvement of local craftspeople — which, for a practice with "construction" in its name, is exactly what one would most want to know. This briefing will not infer those details, and readers are directed to the plans, roof plans, axonometrics and sections published with the project.

Urbanism & Planning URBANISM
In Depth
Turn the school into housing, but don't let it stop being the town's public building: 42 apartments, 200 shared square metres, and 850 square metres of asphalt taken up in Stavyshche
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (25 Sept) · Office for Future (project documentation) · Charity Foundation Housing for IDP · European Commission civil protection and humanitarian aid reporting on housing for displaced Ukrainians · ArchDaily's earlier reporting on Drozdov & Partners converting schools into temporary shelters (comparison case)

Design concept: the difference between a shelter and a home.Converting Ukrainian schools into accommodation for displaced people is not new — Drozdov & Partners were doing exactly that, as temporary shelters, in 2022. What separates the Stavyshche project is precisely this: it is not a shelter, it is housing. A 1,900-square-metre school building becomes 42 permanent apartments in four types, from studios to three-bedroom units, including fully accessible units for people with disabilities. Those two facts — a range of unit types, and accessible provision — are the most concrete distinction between long-term dwelling and temporary refuge. Shelters are typically organised around beds and large undivided rooms; they rarely distinguish between household structures, and rarely accommodate disabled residents at all. "Dvir" is Ukrainian for courtyard, and the architects' full title, The Shared Courtyard, puts the central intention in the name: once it becomes housing, the building should not stop being the place the town gathers.

Spatial strategy: hang a timber walkway outside, and move access out of the building.The hardest technical problem in converting a school to housing is circulation. A school plan is an internal corridor threading large classrooms — the corridor consumes real area that cannot be counted as living space, and sharing one internal corridor among forty-odd households is difficult on both fire safety and privacy grounds. This scheme moves the access system outdoors entirely: selective internal demolition reorganises the plan, and a freestanding timber walkway added outside serves every apartment. "Freestanding" is the operative word — the walkway carries its own structure, so no new load is imposed on the existing masonry, and each household gains a semi-outdoor threshold facing the courtyard. Of the internal area this frees up, roughly 200 square metres is kept as shared indoor space: children's play areas, meeting rooms and space for cultural activities, open to local residents rather than tenants alone. That decision is what makes this an urban project. The 42 units are housing; those 200 square metres are the warrant for its continuing existence as public infrastructure.

Structure and materials: new timber, about 800 square metres of photovoltaics, 850 square metres of asphalt removed.Three quantifiable moves form the spine of the retrofit. First, a new timber roof carrying roughly 800 square metres of photovoltaic panels — in a country whose grid is under sustained wartime attack, roof-mounted generation is not a green badge, it is supply redundancy. Second, a full energy retrofit of the envelope: winters in Kyiv Oblast are severe and district heating has been repeatedly hit, so envelope performance directly determines whether these 42 households can get through the cold. Third, the desealing of roughly 850 square metres of asphalt on site, with a portion of the existing pavement deliberately retained as pedestrian routes through the new landscaped courtyard — which reduces both cost and demolition waste while preserving the paths people already walked across the schoolyard. All three sit firmly in the low-technology, countable category. None depends on materials or equipment that would be hard to source through a wartime supply chain.

Context and consequence: the third project is what makes it a method.The funder is the Charity Foundation Housing for IDP and the architects are Office for Future, based in Florence and Lausanne. ArchDaily notes specifically that this is the third such intervention the foundation has promoted in Ukraine. That "third" matters more than the building does. One conversion is a design outcome; a third means a method is being tested repeatedly — find a disused public building (schools and culture houses have structural redundancy, generous floor-to-floor heights, and central locations), solve access and roof with added timber, solve winter with an envelope retrofit, and protect the public role with desealing and shared space. What students can extract here is not a formal language but an order of priorities under severe constraint: unit diversity and accessibility first, then energy and overwintering, and only then the landscape of the courtyard. The project also leaves an open question: whether, after the war, these buildings will or should revert to being schools. No public source addresses this, and this briefing will not speculate.

Hangzhou's Yuelang Road block renewal completes: Original Design turns a 330-metre back-of-house route into a walkable street, narrowing the carriageway from 10–12 metres to two lanes in 7, putting long-stay parking underground across a 69,900-square-metre block
Sources: gooood (24 Sept) · Original Design Studio, Tongji Architectural Design (Group) (lead architects Zhang Ming, Zhang Zi, Shen Jie, Wang Xunan)
The project is in the core of Qianjiang Century City, Hangzhou, on a 69,900-square-metre site, designed in March 2024 and completed in November 2025 by Original Design Studio of Tongji Architectural Design (Group) Co., Ltd. The subject is a roughly 330-metre commercial street inside the block — one that in practice had been serving commuting and servicing functions, dominated by surface parking, vehicle movement and loading, with almost no space to linger. The strategy operates on three levels. First, the street section is reorganised: the carriageway is compressed from 10–12 metres to two-way 7 metres, all long-stay parking moves underground, and the reclaimed width is returned to pedestrians and public activity. Second, building frontages are opened: physical barriers are removed and entrances connected to street level through landscape transitions, steps and ramps, with a multi-level gallery at one entrance linked vertically by a spiral stair. Third, a unifying image under the theme "Yuelang Never Sleeps" — drawing on the district's existing nightscape and on the street name itself, lunar-phase motifs run through a suspended 16-metre moon-phase ring, pavilions, lighting and street furniture. Materials are decorative panels, metal, ecological elements and planting. The result turns a purely functional service route into what the architects call "a walkable environment for everyday use," retaining necessary traffic function while supporting daytime rest, evening dining and night-time socialising. Note the discipline of scale: every move happens within the existing block's section and frontages, with no demolition and rebuilding.
A long ArchDaily essay attacks the polarisation of climate narratives: designers are fluent in catastrophe and in technological salvation, but skip "the underappreciated, undervalued middle" — decades of parallel retrofitting, restoration and adaptation
Sources: ArchDaily (23 Sept, by Olivia Poston) · ArchDaily monthly topic "The Lure of Escapism in Architecture and Alternative Worlds"
The piece, titled "Between Burning Forests and Utopian Cities: Designing the Climate Transition," belongs to ArchDaily's editorial topic for the month. Its argument is that architecture and design have become highly skilled at imagining two extremes — climate catastrophe, and a future rescued by technology — while broadly neglecting the "underappreciated, undervalued middle" between them, which is precisely where the climate transition will actually take place. Poston sets out three problematic framings: climate denial, which refuses the necessity of transition; climate doom, which treats transformation as impossible; and technological salvation, which implies innovation lets us bypass the difficult phase. Named for discussion are THE LINE at NEOM, the smart-city framework as efficiency-focused but incomplete, and post-apocalyptic screen narratives such as The Last of Us. The core recommendation is that designers redirect their "ambition and creativity" toward imagining the process of transformation itself, using tools like lifecycle thinking, scenario planning and systems analysis to make adaptation culturally compelling. This is an opinion piece rather than reporting, and is flagged as such here.
A designboom column rereads the flâneur: from Baudelaire's observer of modern life to Benjamin's arcades research, a thread about how cities produce ways of looking
Sources: designboom (25 Sept, by Thomai Tsimpou) · "Spectator and Spectacle" series, in partnership with SolidNature
The essay traces how nineteenth-century Paris produced a new kind of urban observer — the flâneur — who walks the streets not to arrive anywhere but to watch and interpret modern life. Charles Baudelaire, in The Painter of Modern Life in the 1860s, described this figure as someone who finds significance in "its changing appearances, its fashions, crowds, gestures, and fleeting encounters," which the author reads as a shift of attention toward "what is temporary and unstable." Walter Benjamin's unfinished Arcades Project then examined nineteenth-century Paris as a place where "architecture, commodities, advertising, fashion, literature, and urban experience are brought together," exposing the tension between "the detached pedestrian and the commodity world surrounding him" and complicating romanticised accounts of urban freedom. Two kinds of space are discussed concretely: covered passages such as the Passage des Panoramas and the Galerie Vivienne, which the author calls spaces "between the street and the interior," where pedestrians could wander without a destination; and Haussmann's mid-century transformation of Paris, whose wider streets and longer views changed how people observed the city. The essay points finally at the present — urban spectatorship mediated by digital technology, whose prototype was formed here.
ArchDaily's community call "Designed for Walking": six unbuilt proposals all addressing how to let people walk in the city again, from street reconstruction to pedestrian links
Sources: ArchDaily (25 Sept, Unbuilt community feature) · Project material supplied by the submitting practices
ArchDaily's Unbuilt section has selected six as-yet-unbuilt proposals from its community submissions under the title "Designed for Walking," united by the theme of pedestrian movement — how to create the conditions for walking in cities built on the assumption of driving. The nature of the format should be stated first: the work is self-submitted by architects rather than selected by an editorial jury, so it reflects what practitioners and students are currently thinking about rather than constituting a ranking. That is also where its value lies — it is a sample of where the profession's attention is going. It is more interesting read against two other items in today's issue: Hangzhou's Yuelang Road, compressing a carriageway from 10–12 metres to 7, is the built version of the same proposition; while designboom's column on the flâneur supplies the intellectual history behind it — the arcades and boulevards of nineteenth-century Paris once produced a way of seeing the city premised on walking, which twentieth-century road engineering then removed. This is a compilation of unbuilt proposals and carries no cost, programme or client information.

Materials & Technology MATERIALS
In Depth
An unfixed column base is also base isolation: ishiba-date and niho-korobi inside 47.1 square metres, and why every beam had to be located in three dimensions first
Cross-checked: designboom (24 Sept) · ArchDaily project page · Archello · Arch2O · Sobokuya Inc. (designer and builder) · Lesprom Network company record

Design concept: make the ageing of the material the subject.The Shrine Office stands within the precincts of Fujitake Shrine in Hokuto, Yamanashi Prefecture, in a valley below the Yatsugatake range. Total floor area is 47.1 square metres, and the programme is the shrine office — the working space for the shrine's daily administration. Sobokuya Inc. both designed and built it, in collaboration with PRIVATE Architect Studio, led by Hisashi Imai and Yuko Sano, completed in 2025. The project's position can be read directly off its material choices. The roof is Corten steel, chosen not for its weathering performance as such but so that it will develop a patina; the marks left by hand-chiselling the joinery are deliberately kept on the surface rather than sanded away. The architects describe their concern as "the way natural materials change and deepen with age." In a religious building this is more than a preference — it aligns the building's temporality with the continuity of the shrine itself.

Spatial strategy: two large openings, a ring of deep eaves, and a sumo ring as reference.Forty-seven square metres leaves no room for elaborate subdivision, so everything happens at the openings and under the eaves. Two large openings cast seasonally shifting light across the floor, making the variation of a day and a year the room's only ornament. Outside are deep eaves and lime plaster walls, which together produce what the architects call a restrained, ceremonial character. In the wet, snowy foothills of the Yatsugatake the eaves are also a constructional necessity: they protect the timber column feet and the wall surfaces, and that protection is essential to the ishiba-date technique discussed below. The stated spatial reference is precise — the sumo ring at Mizu-waka-su Shrine on Oki Island. It explains the emptiness of the interior: a dohyō is a place that is sharply defined and contains nothing, its meaning given by its boundary rather than its contents.

Structural and material innovation: two historic techniques stacked, yielding an early base isolation system.This is the part worth remembering. The structure combines two traditional Japanese carpentry techniques. The first is ishiba-date: the column feet are completely unanchored, resting directly on stone footings. The second is niho-korobi: every column leans uniformly outward in two directions. Neither is rare on its own; stacked, the effect becomes structural. The lean sends loads along diagonal vectors, so the frame forms a self-stabilising geometry under its own weight, while the unanchored feet allow the structure to displace slightly against the stone during an earthquake and return — a mechanism designboom calls "an early form of base isolation." In contemporary terms, it dissipates seismic energy through friction and sliding instead of rigid connection, pointing in the same direction as a modern isolation bearing but realised in stone and wood. The cost falls on fabrication: because the columns lean in two directions, no beam meets its support at a standard angle, so every beam had to be located, set out and marked in three dimensions before it could be cut. All the timber is sourced nearby — the exposed larch roof frame was felled on the slopes of the Yatsugatake, with red pine taiko beams (beams that retain the log's curved face) in the main frame, and the joinery was designed and hand-made in Sobokuya's own workshop.

Context and consequence: a company that designs and builds, and one gap we will leave open.Sobokuya's status deserves separate comment: it is simultaneously the designer and the builder, with the carpentry and joinery produced in its own workshop. That explains how a method requiring three-dimensional setting-out of every member is viable on a 47-square-metre building with an obviously modest budget — there is no loss in translation between drawing and fabrication, and no tendering or variation game in between. This design-build organisation is normal in the Japanese tradition of shrine and temple construction, and has all but vanished from the contemporary division of labour. One thing must be stated honestly: the published sources do not disclose how the structure was calculated, nor how it satisfies the seismic provisions of Japan's current Building Standards Act — there is a real tension between unanchored ishiba-date feet and the column-base anchorage that modern codes generally require, and this briefing has not obtained the specific approval basis for this project, so it will not speculate. For students, this small building offers a counterintuitive reminder: seismic performance need not be achieved by adding restraint. It can also be achieved by removing restraint in a controlled way — provided geometry, detailing and fabrication accuracy all arrive together.

A century-old Flarz house transformed in Uster, Switzerland: a 52-millimetre folded concrete stair works simultaneously as reinforcement and services void, with risers acting as beams and no horizontal span over 1.20 metres
Sources: gooood (25 Sept) · Balissat Kaçani + Stefan Wülser (architects)
The project is in Uster, Switzerland, and the subject is a century-old Flarz house — a type of longitudinally extended row farmhouse common in eastern Switzerland. The architects are Balissat Kaçani and Stefan Wülser. The whole transformation is organised around a single concrete stair, which is both the structural spine and the organising device for the house. The structural solution rewards attention: the stair slab is just 52 millimetres thick, to avoid adding excessive weight to the existing structure; every fold is structurally activated, with the risers acting as beams, so that no horizontal span exceeds 1.20 metres; and a central vertical wall carries all forces to the ground, which the architects compare to a tree-like foundation system. The stair performs three jobs at once: reinforcing the deteriorating original timber beams, housing sustainable building services within its hollow structure, and reorganising circulation through the whole house. An architect who has barely built, with a show made only of drawings: Peter Salter at the AA, and the full-scale construction detail as a form of knowledge
Cross-checked: Architectural Record listings · Architectural Association Public Programme · AEX / architecture-exhibitions.com · Wikipedia entry on Peter Salter · Piano Nobile, Peter Salter: Drawing Walmer Yard, exhibition and publication (2016) · Divisare archive (photography Hélène Binet)

Design concept: the drawing as the exhibit, not as a stand-in for the building."Peter Salter: Drawings" opened on 25 September 2026 at the Architectural Association (AA) Gallery in London and runs to 5 December, presenting drawings by Peter Salter, a substantial portion of them made between 1985 and 2020 and never previously shown. Architecture exhibitions normally face an awkwardness: the building itself cannot attend, so what goes on display is a proxy — a model, a photograph, a drawing. This exhibition starts from different premises. Salter is an architect with very few built works who nonetheless holds a position in the discipline on the strength of his drawings; for him the drawing is not a substitute for the building but the place where the work happens. The organisers' framing catches this: the drawings balance architectural poetics with technical expertise — two things architectural education usually treats as opposite ends, which these sheets insist on completing at once.

Spatial strategy: three kinds of drawing, three scales, and especially the full-scale one.Per the organisers, the work on show spans three registers: architectural construction details, atmospheric perspectives, and large-scale plans and sections. Each answers a different cognitive task — the detail answers "how does it join," the perspective answers "what does it feel like to be inside," and the plan and section answer "how is the whole organised." The register that deserves particular attention is the full-scale: the exhibits include 1:1 drawings of construction details and of pieces of furniture. Full-scale means there is no longer any reduction between drawing and object, so the act of drawing becomes a direct working-out of material dimensions, tolerances and methods of connection — which is why, in the organisers' account, these sheets reveal a deep knowledge of materials and the way they behave, along with care for how buildings come together. For students this is a method available immediately: the 1:1 detail is not a construction-stage by-product, it can be a design-stage instrument of thought.

Structure and material: Walmer Yard, and a reputation built on drawings.Salter was born in 1947 and began in 1962 at the Shoreditch College of Furnishing Trades, training first in furniture design before moving into architecture — an origin not unrelated to his later attention to furniture drawings and full-scale joints. He took his Diploma at the AA in 1980, worked with Alison and Peter Smithson in the early 1980s, and through that decade collaborated with Christopher Macdonald as Macdonald and Salter. The output of that period was mainly unbuilt speculative work, characterised in the public record by "highly developed and evocative drawings, which are concerned with qualities of construction and spatial atmosphere." He now practises as Salter+Collingridge with Fenella Collingridge, and their principal built work is Walmer Yard in London — four bespoke houses, his first residential building in Britain, and an award winner. In 2016 Piano Nobile exhibited and published the drawings for that project as Peter Salter: Drawing Walmer Yard; the houses have been photographed by Hélène Binet. Per Architectural Record, the present AA exhibition coincides with the release of the book Peter Salter: Drawings.

Context and consequence: a thread about architectural education.Salter's other identity is as an educator, and a weighty one: he taught at the AA through the 1980s and 1990s, became Head of School at the University of East London in 1995 succeeding Ron Herron, and since 2006 has been Professor of Architectural Design at Cardiff University's Welsh School of Architecture. In 2004 he received the RIBA Annie Spink Award, given specifically for outstanding contribution to architectural education. Set that record beside "very few built works" and you have what makes this exhibition most interesting for students: in a profession that measures architects by built output, there exists another route the discipline itself recognises, whose credentials are the density of knowledge carried in drawings and the influence exercised in teaching. The venue is the AA Gallery at 36 Bedford Square, London, and the exhibition runs from 25 September to 5 December.
