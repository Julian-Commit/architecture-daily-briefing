ACADEMIA
The 2026 Soane Medal goes to Farshid Moussavi: selection committee chair Amin Taha writes that she charts a third path beyond spectacle and resignation — attentiveness. She receives the medal and gives the Soane Medal Lecture at the Royal Academy in London on 24 November
Sources: ArchDaily (29 Sep) · Dezeen (28 Sep) · The Architect's Newspaper (28 Sep) · Harvard GSD announcement · Architects' Journal · world-architects
Sir John Soane's Museum in London awards the Soane Medal once a year, with the stated aim of encouraging a better understanding of the central importance of architecture in people's lives. The 2026 recipient is the British-Iranian architect Farshid Moussavi, professor in practice of architecture at the Harvard Graduate School of Design. The selection committee was chaired by the architect Amin Taha and included the curator Vicky Richardson; the citation describes a sustained and rigorous contribution to architecture through building, teaching and writing, and a career that has kept practice and the academy connected. Taha's line is the one to keep: a profession that tends to choose between spectacle and resignation, and an architect who instead charts a third path — attentiveness. Richardson praised her intellectual curiosity and singled out her research on social housing, which brought the long-overlooked French architect Renée Gailhoustet back into view. Moussavi co-founded Foreign Office Architects with Alejandro Zaera-Polo in 1993 and established Farshid Moussavi Architecture (FMA) in 2011. Recent work includes the Museum of Contemporary Art Cleveland, the La Folie Divine apartments in Montpellier, the Îlot 19 housing at La Défense-Nanterre in Paris, the Harrods Toy Department in London, and the recently completed Ismaili Center Houston. She has taught at the Architectural Association, UCLA and Princeton among others, and has previously received the Jane Drew Prize, the Ada Louise Huxtable Prize and the American Academy of Arts and Letters Architecture Award. Her response reframes the museum itself as the argument: the Soane represents her own values, that a building can be simultaneously a home, a practice and a way of thinking, without separating the personal from the professional. The 2025 medal went to Madelon Vriesendorp. The presentation and the Soane Medal Lecture take place at the Royal Academy in London on 24 November 2026.

The Advisory Council on Historic Preservation wrote to the National Park Service on 24 September terminating Section 106 consultation for Washington's 250-foot triumphal arch; the Cultural Landscape Foundation calls the move singularly audacious, having attended a single consulting party meeting
Sources: The Architect's Newspaper (29 Sep) · Dezeen (7 Sep, 21 Sep) · Dezeen (22 May, CFA approval) · Dezeen (10 Apr, design unveiled) · WJLA · TIME
Travis Voyles, vice chairman of the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation (ACHP), wrote on 24 September to Tammy Stidham, associate regional director at the National Park Service (NPS), terminating the Section 106 consultation process for the Washington triumphal arch. Section 106, established by the National Historic Preservation Act, requires federal agencies to consider the effects of their undertakings on historic properties and to consult with interested parties before proceeding. Voyles cited significant public concerns regarding adverse effects and concluded that additional consultation on the existing proposal was unlikely to result in agreement; he also noted the NPS position that alternatives would alter the fundamental nature of the proposed undertaking. With consultation terminated, the ACHP must issue formal comment by 7 October. Charles Birnbaum, president of The Cultural Landscape Foundation (TCLF), objected in plain terms, calling the letter singularly audacious: TCLF had attended only one consulting party meeting, and the arch would be the second-largest monument in Washington, D.C. He further argued that the ACHP was proceeding as if the new regulations are already in effect, when current law is unchanged. The American Institute of Architects has separately opposed the concurrent revisions to Section 106, stating that they place approximately 1.4 million historic sites at greater risk. On the project itself: the design is by the Atlanta firm Harrison Design, with Nicolas Charbonneau as designer; the arch would rise 250 feet (about 76 metres), nearly 100 feet (30 metres) taller than its Parisian counterpart; it is neoclassical in language and topped by three gilded angels. The site is the currently empty Memorial Circle on Columbia Island in the Potomac, and the structure would block the view between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington National Cemetery. The cost is estimated at around $400 million, financed by a mix of private donations and public funds. A National Capital Planning Commission staff report in July put construction at up to three years, with excavation scheduled for September. Reporting on 21 September indicated an additional military function for the project.

Rice husk becomes a 23-centimetre exterior wall: SUMMARY combines U-shaped precast concrete modules with a rice husk cement composite in Portugal, thermal resistance 1.877 m²K/W, and assembles the 146-square-metre house on site in three days
Sources: ArchDaily (28 Sep) · Dwell · Azure Magazine · Archinect firm project page · SUMMARY (architects) · Photography: Alexander Bogorodskiy, Joaquin Mora
Rice Husk House stands in Esmoriz, Portugal, some 25 kilometres south of Porto. It covers 146 square metres, was completed in 2024, and is the work of the Porto studio SUMMARY — Samuel Gonçalves leading, Pedro Penalva on the design team — with structural engineering by FTS - Technical Solutions and construction by Arouconstroi SA. SUMMARY has been developing the same technical line for over a decade: a three-dimensional prefabricated modular system built from U-shaped reinforced concrete components, grouped according to the needs of the housing programme. What is new here sits on the outside. Each module carries an outer layer of cement-based composite incorporating rice husk, 23 centimetres thick, serving as both exterior cladding and insulation, with a published thermal resistance of 1.877 m²K/W. Rice husk is an agro-industrial byproduct normally discarded or burned; this batch was sourced locally from the rice fields of the Lower Mondego region. On programme, site preparation, earthworks, excavation and foundations ran in parallel with off-site production of the modules, and the single-family house was assembled on site in three days. The studio frames the gains as a chain: less concrete work on site, less waste, and a smaller footprint in noise and debris on the surrounding environment. The plot is level and square, oriented north-south, near a coastal pine forest, in a low-density setting of similarly scaled detached houses — a context the single-storey volume matches. For students, the value of the project lies in moving a bio-based material off the sample wall and into a layer that genuinely carries the enclosure and insulation, with a thermal number that can be checked.

Snøhetta completes all four clubhouses at Tai Wai, Hong Kong: 5,650 square metres split into a Tea House, a Well House, a Farm House and a Sky House, distributed around an urban farm over a nine-year run from 2017 to 2026
Sources: ArchDaily (28 Sep) · gooood (28 Sep) · Snøhetta project data · Photography: Harold de Puymorin / HDP Photography
The project sits in Tai Wai in Hong Kong's New Territories. The client is New World Development Ltd, the architect is Snøhetta, the local collaborating architect is Ronald Lu & Partners, landscape design is by Ohtori Consultants Environmental Design Institute, and the total area is 5,650 square metres, designed and built between 2017 and 2026. Rather than packing every amenity into one large clubhouse, the 5,650 square metres are split into four separate buildings distributed between the residential blocks and an urban farm, forming a route that has to be walked. The Tea House marks the threshold between urban life and home; water dominates it, with an indoor pool and a cascading waterfall, and a cantilevered pavilion frames views for contemplation and retreat. The Well House gathers gym, spa, sauna, badminton court and swimming pool, using generous glazing to thin the line between inside and out as far as it will go. The Farm House sits at the core of the development, surrounded by urban farming areas, and holds three kitchens, flexible event spaces and a custom rail-bound dining table system that adapts from a family dinner to a community gathering by moving one table rather than swapping several. The Sky House, the last of the four, is about intergenerational connection: outdoor playgrounds, children's play areas, classrooms and music rooms below, senior lounges above, tied together by a dramatic domed skylight and a rope-climbing tower. The architects describe the four not as isolated amenities but as a connected network of experiences distributed throughout the development. In one of the densest cities on earth, breaking the clubhouse apart and making residents cross a farm to get from one activity to the next is worth setting against the single consolidated clubhouse standard in mainland high-rise developments.

Jan Gehl turned 90 on 17 September: the Danish Architecture Center opens a new main exhibition, Cities for People, on World Architecture Day, 5 October, structured around his 12 quality criteria for public urban space
Sources: Danish Architecture Center press release · ArchDaily (28 Sep)
The Danish architect and urbanist Jan Gehl was born on 17 September 1936 and turned 90 this month. The Danish Architecture Center (DAC) has built a calendar around it: a professional celebration for invited guests took place on 18 September, and a new main exhibition, Cities for People, opens on World Architecture Day, 5 October, developed by DAC in collaboration with the practice Gehl and with Gehl himself. The exhibition draws on his life's work and is structured around his 12 quality criteria for public urban spaces, examining through both historical and contemporary perspectives how urban design shapes our lives and how it can improve health, well-being and community. DAC chief executive Kent Martinussen puts it this way: Jan Gehl has shown the world that perceived city quality stems from understanding people's needs and caring about how people live. Three of Gehl's own lines appear in the release: first life, then spaces, then buildings — the other way around never works; cultures and climates differ worldwide, but people are the same; and a good city resembles a good party, where people stay longer than necessary. Gehl founded Gehl Architects, now Gehl / Gehl Studio, in 2000; his books have been translated into more than 40 languages, and the cities he has worked in include Melbourne, Sydney, London, New York, Amman, Cape Town, Copenhagen and Moscow. We have not found a closing date for the exhibition — the DAC release does not state one, and we will not guess.

IN DEPTH
Break the clubhouse into four and make residents walk through a farm to use it: Snøhetta completes 5,650 square metres of clubhouses at Tai Wai, Hong Kong, nine years in the making
Cross-checked against: ArchDaily project page (28 Sep) · gooood (28 Sep) · Snøhetta project data · Ronald Lu & Partners (local collaborator) · Ohtori Consultants Environmental Design Institute (landscape) · Photography: Harold de Puymorin / HDP Photography

Concept: not one big clubhouse, but a distributed network of experiences.The project is in Tai Wai, in Hong Kong's New Territories. The client is New World Development Ltd, the architect is Snøhetta, the local collaborating architect is Ronald Lu & Partners, landscape design is by Ohtori Consultants Environmental Design Institute, and the total area is 5,650 square metres, designed and built between 2017 and 2026. Amenity buildings in dense residential developments are usually handled as a single consolidated volume: pool, gym, banquet hall and children's room stacked in one building. It is the most efficient arrangement, it produces the shortest routes, and it is also the easiest way to end up with a sealed box that has nothing to do with the rest of the development. Snøhetta went the other way, splitting the 5,650 square metres into four buildings distributed between the residential blocks and an urban farm. The architects describe the four not as isolated amenities but as a connected network of experiences distributed throughout the development, intended to encourage discovery, interaction and participation across ages and lifestyles. The cost of that decision is obvious: longer routes, more duplicated service space. What it buys is a specific behavioural outcome — to move from one activity to another, residents have to go outdoors, and they have to pass the farm.

Spatial strategy: four buildings, four social scales.The Tea House is placed at the junction between city and home, and functions as a threshold. Water is its dominant material: an indoor pool with a cascading waterfall, and a cantilevered pavilion framing the middle distance, programmed for contemplation and retreat — slowing the body down from urban tempo before it enters the residential realm. The Well House takes on movement and physical wellbeing: gym, spa, sauna, badminton court and swimming pool, worked with generous glazing and strong visual connections to nature that blur the boundary between architecture and landscape. The Farm House sits at the geographic core, ringed by the urban farming areas, with three kitchens and a set of flexible event spaces. Its most memorable element is a piece of furniture: a custom rail-bound dining table system that moves and recombines along its track to suit anything from one family's dinner to a full community gathering, rather than requiring several sets of tables. The Sky House, completed last, is about intergenerational connection — outdoor playgrounds, children's play areas, classrooms and music rooms at the base, senior lounges on the upper level, stitched vertically by a domed skylight and a rope-climbing tower. Each building answers to a different social scale: solitude, the body, family and collective, and across generations.

Materials and construction: wood, metal, concrete, glass — plus ground that actually has to grow things.The material list is short: wood, metal, concrete and glass. Published images show interiors led by timber detailing, with generous glazing and openings onto gardens. That combination carries a cost in Hong Kong's climate — humidity, typhoons, salt spray — so the timber is more likely concentrated where eaves and shading protect it; the published material does not disclose species or assembly details, and we will not infer them. The real technical difficulty is not in the clubhouses but in whether the phrase urban farm can hold: cultivation needs soil depth, drainage, sun hours and year-round irrigation, none of which follow automatically from the structural slabs and services of a high-rise residential development. Landscape was handled by Ohtori Consultants Environmental Design Institute in parallel with the architecture, and the decision to distribute the four buildings around the farm is itself what turns the farm from landscape decoration into an everyday place that must be crossed and seen. The architects describe the whole as a vibrant green lung and urban farm within the development.

Context and consequence: a contrarian answer to dense-residential amenity.Hong Kong is among the most densely populated cities in the world, and the prevailing logic for residential amenity is to stack public space vertically and manage it centrally. The Pavilia Farm clubhouses offer the opposite bargain: sacrifice some compactness in exchange for making outdoor walking unavoidable and agricultural activity visible. The nine-year run from 2017 to 2026 is itself information — a distributed layout of this kind has to be coordinated with the phasing, structure and landscape systems of the residential development over the long haul, and it is not something that can be retrofitted at the end. For students at Syracuse Architecture, the case is most useful as a comparison: set it beside the consolidated clubhouse typical of mainland high-rise developments and the same resource produces entirely different patterns of use. Note that the published material does not disclose cost, the area or crop programme of the urban farm, or the individual areas of the four buildings, and we will not infer them.

Sources: ArchDaily project page (28 Sep) · Bastidas Architecture · Photography: Medsea Studio
The house sits on a Mediterranean hillside in Spain, covers 680 square metres, and was completed in 2023 though published this week. Bastidas Architecture led the work, with Wolfgang Wagner as lead architect and Ignasi Fuster on the technical team, Melchor Mascaro as general contractor and Iona Group as engineering consultant. The architects describe the house as semi-buried and covered by a layer of native vegetation that renders it invisible — it is not a volume added to the topography so much as a continuation of it. Burying and planting a hillside house is an old move; what is worth noting here is how far it has been pushed towards outright invisibility.
Aranya Youyi Bay Community Regeneration / genarchitects, Qinhuangdao: a 1,046-square-metre mixed-use micro-community that grades its public space through a chain of interconnected plazas, from lively to quiet

ANDERSSON BELL Tokyo flagship store / I IN: rather than covering the raw concrete and three decades of patina in a 1993 brutalist building, the design layers saturated colour, glossy metal pipes and embedded lighting on top of it
Sources: gooood (29 Sep) · I IN Inc.
The store is in Tokyo, inside a brutalist structure built in 1993, and was completed in 2026 by the Tokyo studio I IN across three levels. The architects did not wrap the old structure; they kept the exposed concrete surfaces and the traces accumulated over more than three decades, then layered new elements on top: saturated colour, glossy metal pipes piercing through the architecture, mirrored ceiling treatments, glass display elements, and lighting embedded in floors and ceilings. The strongest gesture on the street is a single block of red in the display window — the façade asserts itself through colour rather than mass. The studio's framing is to bring the cultural energy of 1990s Tokyo, when fashion, music, art and architecture flourished together, into the same room as the free-spirited, globally minded sensibility emerging from contemporary Seoul, and to look for a new equilibrium in the contrasts between architecture, colour, material and light. It is one answer to a recurring problem in retail inside existing buildings: do not compete with the original structure for dominance — acknowledge it and add.

A federal preservation process closed early: the ACHP terminates Section 106 consultation for Washington's triumphal arch, a 250-foot monument that would block the view between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington
Cross-checked against: The Architect's Newspaper (29 Sep) · Dezeen (7 Sep, construction start) · Dezeen (21 Sep, added function) · Dezeen (22 May, CFA approval) · Dezeen (10 Apr, design unveiled) · WJLA · TIME · The Cultural Landscape Foundation statement

The event: a letter dated 24 September shuts the consultation down.Travis Voyles, vice chairman of the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation (ACHP), wrote on 24 September to Tammy Stidham, associate regional director at the National Park Service (NPS), terminating the Section 106 consultation process for the Washington triumphal arch. The letter gives two reasons: significant public concerns regarding adverse effects, and a conclusion that additional consultation on the existing proposal is unlikely to result in agreement. Voyles also cites the NPS position that alternatives would alter the fundamental nature of the proposed undertaking. Termination is not approval; it moves the process to its next stage. The ACHP must issue formal comment by 7 October, after which the responsible agency may decide whether to proceed, provided it has taken that comment into account. The distinction worth holding onto is between process and substance: what has been closed is the consultation, not the permit. But in a federal undertaking in the United States, consultation is often the only formal channel through which the public and professional bodies can intervene in a design at all.

Site and design: 250 feet, three gilded angels, and a sightline that would be cut.The design is by the Atlanta firm Harrison Design, with Nicolas Charbonneau as designer; it was unveiled in April and approved by the Commission of Fine Arts (CFA) in May. The arch would rise 250 feet, about 76 metres, nearly 100 feet — 30 metres — taller than its Parisian counterpart. The language is neoclassical, and three gilded angels would top it. The site is the currently empty Memorial Circle on Columbia Island in the Potomac. That location is the substantive urban-design argument in the whole affair: Washington's monumental order rests on a specific set of axes and sightlines, and Memorial Circle sits in the visual corridor between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington National Cemetery. A 76-metre structure placed there severs that relationship. Cost is put at around $400 million, financed by a mix of private donations and public funds. A National Capital Planning Commission (NCPC) staff report in July estimated construction at up to three years, with excavation scheduled for September.

Process and law: what Section 106 is, and why closing it drew professional objection.Section 106 comes from the National Historic Preservation Act of 1966. It requires federal agencies to consider the effects of their undertakings on historic properties and to consult with state historic preservation officers, tribes and other interested parties, seeking ways to avoid, minimise or mitigate adverse effects. Charles Birnbaum, president of The Cultural Landscape Foundation (TCLF), objects on two counts: the density of the process, and the timing of the law. He notes that TCLF attended only one consulting party meeting, and that the arch would be the second-largest monument in Washington, D.C. — to declare, on that basis, that consultation is unlikely to produce agreement is what he calls singularly audacious. The sharper point is his second: in his reading, the ACHP is proceeding as if the new regulations are already in effect, when current law is unchanged. That points to the concurrent revision of the Section 106 regulations, which the American Institute of Architects has publicly opposed on the grounds that it would place approximately 1.4 million historic sites at greater risk.

Context and consequence: one monument's approval path is becoming a stress test of the preservation system itself.Laid out as a timeline: design unveiled in April, CFA approval in May, NCPC staff report in July, excavation scheduled for September, consultation terminated on 24 September, formal comment due 7 October. Reporting on 21 September separately indicated an additional military function for the project. The density of that timeline is itself the story — a project whose scale and position sit in the monumental core of Washington has gone from public unveiling to preparation for groundbreaking in under six months. For readers working in urbanism and planning, the value of the case is not in judging whether the building is handsome (this briefing does not make that judgement) but in how clearly it exposes the real strength of a preservation framework: when the review body, the planning commission and the preservation council all compress their timetables into a single quarter, the formal window for professional and public comment shrinks accordingly. Note that the ACHP's formal comment has not yet been issued and the project's final permitting status was undetermined as this issue went out; we do not predict the outcome.

How air-conditioning reshaped the Asian city: in Hong Kong, air-conditioning alone accounted for around 30 per cent of total electricity consumption in 2023, with waste heat equivalent to roughly 800,000 continuously running two-kilowatt heaters
Sources: ArchDaily (28 Sep, by Jonathan Yeung) · Urban Redevelopment Authority, Singapore — planning guidelines · Hong Kong electricity consumption data cited in the article
The argument is that air-conditioning infrastructure in Asian cities has moved from being a comfort technology to being a system that lets inhabitants bypass the outdoors entirely. Jonathan Yeung describes it as a feedback loop: investment keeps flowing towards climate-controlled interiors, so exterior streets get progressively more neglected and less worth walking, which deepens the dependence on interiors. The case studies cluster in Hong Kong (the MTR system, the Elements podium, the International Finance Centre, the Pacific Place podium mall, Lee Garden Eight, The Landmark Atrium, the MegaICE rink at MegaBox), Singapore (Urban Redevelopment Authority guidelines emphasising covered walkways and multi-level networks), Bangkok (One Bangkok), Guangzhou (Tianhe Vanke Plaza) and Shaoxing (CTC Mall). The figure given is that in Hong Kong air-conditioning alone accounted for around 30 per cent of total electricity consumption in 2023, producing waste heat roughly equivalent to 800,000 continuously running two-kilowatt heaters. The warning is about professional capacity: when mechanical cooling becomes the only definition of comfort, cities lose the knowledge needed to design inhabitable public space without total enclosure. Worth keeping for anyone designing in hot and humid climates.
Zando Central Market, Kinshasa: designed for 3,500 traders and now carrying more than 20,000, THINK TANK rebuilt it around five courtyards, patterned terracotta screen walls and rainwater harvesting — a 2025 Holcim Foundation Award winner
Sources: ArchDaily project page (24 Sep) · designboom · Holcim Foundation Awards 2025 · THINK TANK architecture · Photography: Martin Argyroglo and others
The market is in Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of the Congo, covers 81,500 square metres, and is the work of THINK TANK architecture for the contracting authority Sogema, with civil engineering by EGIS Bâtiment International, façade design by Ayo and graphic design by Novablok. The premise is a mismatch of numbers: the market was originally designed for 3,500 traders and now supports over 20,000 vendors, and years of neglect had left blocked streets, absent sanitation, uncontrolled sprawl and nightly violence. The architects set their own baseline — conceived above all as an African market, not a mall — remaining open to the city, its climate, its people and its everyday life while providing robust and dignified infrastructure. Three moves carry it. The plan is organised around five open courtyards that bring vegetation and daylight into a very deep footprint. Open concrete structures are combined with locally produced terracotta bricks, with patterned brick façades drawn from Congolese textiles acting as moucharabieh screens that deliver shading and ventilation through the envelope itself rather than through machinery. And rainwater is collected and stored in tanks, then reused for irrigation, cleaning and fire safety, reducing dependence on municipal infrastructure and improving resilience during heavy rains or service interruptions. The scheme won a 2025 Holcim Foundation Award for the Middle East and Africa region. The architects describe their own role as a reservoir of ideas, combining international expertise with local knowledge and craftsmanship, developed throughout with local authorities, vendors and other stakeholders.
Xicheng Canal Landscape: Opposite Parks / BAU, Jiangyin: two deliberately opposite things along 4 kilometres of canal — 101 pentagonal garden mosaics on the east bank, a rewilded ecological corridor with kilometres of concrete edge removed on the west, 169.7 hectares in all
Sources: gooood (29 Sep) · BAU (Brearley Architects + Urbanists) · Jiangyin Planning Bureau (client)
The project is in Jiangyin, Jiangsu province, covers 169.7 hectares along roughly 4 kilometres of the Xicheng Canal, and was delivered between 2017 and 2025 by BAU (Brearley Architects + Urbanists) for the Jiangyin Planning Bureau. The design rests on a deliberate dichotomy: one bank is a continuous indigenous ecological corridor, the other a highly flexible cultural park composed of 101 distinct park mosaics. On the east bank a pentagonal tessellating grid divides the parkland into 101 adaptable garden units, each functioning independently — the architects note that a single garden can be designed, re-designed, programmed and re-programmed without disrupting its neighbours, which lets cultural programme evolve over time. The west bank does the opposite: a rewilded post-industrial landscape that prioritises habitat continuity, removing kilometres of concrete edges and replacing them with vegetated banks that support fish spawning and amphibious life, and native species including Oriental Storks and Common Kingfishers. Techniques include native riparian vegetation for bank stabilisation, rain gardens and wetlands, low-saturation pavilions that recede into their settings, and adaptive reuse of industrial elements such as silos and gantries. What is transferable here is the refusal to cover the whole river with one method: ecological resilience and cultural adaptability are assigned to opposite banks and each is allowed to stay intact.

Turning agricultural waste into the layer that actually does the insulating: SUMMARY's Rice Husk House uses 23 centimetres of rice husk cement composite at 1.877 m²K/W, assembled in three days
Cross-checked against: ArchDaily project page (28 Sep) · Dwell · Azure Magazine · Archinect firm project page · SUMMARY — Architecture, Territory, [Pre]Fabrication · Photography: Alexander Bogorodskiy, Joaquin Mora

Concept: the prefabrication system stays; the outer layer changes.Rice Husk House is in Esmoriz, Portugal, about 25 kilometres south of Porto. It covers 146 square metres and was completed in 2024. The architect is the Porto studio SUMMARY, with Samuel Gonçalves leading and Pedro Penalva on the design team; structural engineering by FTS - Technical Solutions, construction by Arouconstroi SA. The studio carries a strapline that doubles as a research agenda — Architecture, Territory, [Pre]Fabrication — and has been pushing the same technical line for over a decade: a three-dimensional prefabricated modular system built from U-shaped reinforced concrete components, grouped according to the needs of the housing programme. Rice Husk House keeps that system intact. The new variable is the outer material. Each module carries an outer layer of cement-based composite incorporating rice husk, and that single layer serves as both exterior cladding and insulation — not a board of insulation added outside the concrete and then finished, but two jobs merged into one 23-centimetre assembly. For an industry that has largely kept bio-based materials in interior finishes and sample walls, the significance is the transfer of responsibility.

Spatial strategy: single storey, square plot, low-density fabric — complexity left to the assembly.The plot is level and square, oriented north-south, near a coastal pine forest, surrounded by similarly scaled detached houses in a low-density setting. The house is single-storey and matches that context; the architects describe the single-storey structure as fitting within a context of similarly scaled, detached homes. It is a trade worth noticing: when the innovation is concentrated in the assembly, volume and plan tend towards the conservative. The combinatorial logic of the U-shaped modules sets bay and depth to a module-based dimension, and room divisions run broadly along the module side walls. In other words this is not a form-driven house; its design intensity lives in the section and in the construction detail, not in the silhouette. For students, that is a useful reference point in itself: once a prefabrication system is fixed, design freedom migrates from outline to construction and material.

Material and construction innovation: 23 centimetres, 1.877 m²K/W, three days of assembly.This is the substantive part. Rice husk is an agro-industrial byproduct of rice processing, normally discarded or burned; here it was sourced locally from the rice fields of the Lower Mondego region, so the haul is short. Mixed into a cement-based composite, it forms a 23-centimetre outer layer that works as cladding and insulation together, with a published thermal resistance of 1.877 m²K/W. On sequencing, site preparation, earthworks, excavation and foundations were carried out in parallel with off-site production of the modular components — which is the real reason a modular system compresses a programme, not because assembly is fast but because two tracks run at once. The single-family house was then assembled on site in three days. The studio frames the benefits as a chain: less concrete work on site, less waste, and a reduced impact on the surrounding environment in both noise and debris. On a suburban plot with immediate neighbours, three days is not just a saving in money — it directly sets how long the neighbours have to live with a building site. Note that the published material does not disclose the composite's mix proportions, husk content, mechanical performance or durability test data, nor the cost, and we will not infer them.

Context and consequence: bio-based materials moving from demonstration into the assembly.Rice husk as a building material is not new: it is high in silica, low in bulk density and low in thermal conductivity, and there is a substantial research literature on rice husk-based insulation, with companies such as Italy's Ricehouse already producing prefabricated rice husk blocks at scale. The position Rice Husk House occupies is not inventing a material but placing one inside an already mature prefabrication system and letting it carry both enclosure and insulation. That integration is the necessary step for bio-based materials to enter mainstream construction — for as long as such a material remains an added insulation layer or a decorative finish, it stays subordinate to somebody else's assembly logic. For students at Syracuse Architecture, the project reads well against two kinds of precedent: bio-based materials used as infill (straw bale, hempcrete) and bio-based materials used as finish (the various recycled boards). Rice Husk House sits between the two, and it publishes a number you can check — in a conversation about sustainable materials, 1.877 m²K/W is worth more than any adjective.

The iLOOminate Public Toilet / Studio 10, Longmen, Huizhou: a 61.63-square-metre village toilet renovation that uses locally sourced hollow-brick screen walls, angled square skylights and light scoops to bring daylight into every stall
Sources: ArchDaily project page (28 Sep) · gooood (28 Sep) · Studio 10 (principal: Shi Zhou) · Photography: Chao Zhang
The building is in Zhongxin Village, Mazha Town, Longmen County, Huizhou, Guangdong province. It covers 61.63 square metres, was completed in 2025, and is by Studio 10 with Shi Zhou as principal for the Longmen County Mazha Town People's Government; it is a supporting facility within a local architectural arts programme and a renovation of the village's only public toilet. Preliminary field research produced the governing fact: most villagers have private bathrooms at home yet still prefer to use this one public toilet — meaning this 61-square-metre building carries a public-life function, not merely a sanitary one. The design theme is borrowing light through chiselled openings. First, the new single-slope roof opens northward towards the mountains. Second, a gallery-and-sink transition zone faces the village square. Third, locally sourced hollow bricks are laid as screening walls that hold privacy while drawing daylight and cross ventilation inside. Fourth, angled square skylights and tubular light scoops channel natural light into each individual stall. It is a case of the cheapest category of rural infrastructure being made into public cultural space, and its technical content sits almost entirely in two things: brick and light.
Comillas Waterfront Promenade / Héctor Navarro + arkhitekton, Cantabria, Spain: 5,400 square metres that turn sand from a nuisance into an active component of the paving system, alongside reclaimed coastal armour limestone and photocatalytic pavers
Sources: gooood (29 Sep) · Héctor Navarro + arkhitekton · Municipal government of Comillas (client) · Photography: Imagen Subliminal (Miguel de Guzmán & Rocío R. Rivas)
The project is in Comillas, Cantabria, Spain, covers 5,400 square metres and was completed in 2026. Héctor Navarro + arkhitekton led the design, with landscape collaboration from Diego Jardinería (Diego Cuartas, Adolfo Vila), Laura Fernández as project manager, and the municipal government of Comillas as client. What it replaced was a narrow sidewalk and a parking lot. The most interesting judgement is a material one: sand, usually treated in seaside towns as something to be swept away, is taken instead as an active component of the promenade itself, integrated into both its permeable paving and landscape system. Three paving materials do the work — permeable concrete pavers in varying tones and textures, reclaimed limestone blocks taken from coastal armour stones, and photocatalytic pavers that improve air quality. Spatially there are two complementary zones: an accessible waterfront promenade retaining the historic balustrades, and an inland green corridor planted with native vegetation drawn from Cantabrian dune ecosystems, with canopy developed in phases. Other moves include accessible seating, reinterpreted traditional ice cream kiosks, and a parking reorganisation with new pedestrian connections. The permeable surface system also improves the hydrological performance of the strip.

The feature starts from a geological fact: volcanic landscapes make geological change unusually visible, as lava hardens and vegetation gradually colonises the new terrain. One counterintuitive observation: the reason people keep building in volcanic regions is often precisely their remoteness and the oddity of the ground, since their isolation and unusual terrain are often part of the appeal. Don't skip to the end: an ArchDaily essay argues that design can picture catastrophe and can picture technological utopia, but lacks the tools to picture the transition itself
Sources: ArchDaily (26 Sep, by Olivia Poston) · ArchDaily September monthly topic: The Lure of Escapism in Architecture and Alternative Worlds
The argument identifies a gap in tools. Designers and architects have developed sophisticated vocabularies for imagining climate catastrophe and for imagining technologically advanced futures, but lack adequate means of envisioning the transition itself — the decades of gradual adaptation, repair and restoration that will actually constitute climate action. Olivia Poston names the three frameworks that currently dominate: climate denial, which rejects the necessity of transition; climate doom, which treats transformation as impossible; and technological salvation, which defers meaningful action to a future breakthrough. She stresses that real climate work involves adaptation, maintenance and resilience unfolding gradually and unevenly across existing infrastructure, communities and systems, so what designers need is not a single dramatic solution but the imaginative capacity for incremental, coordinated change. THE LINE at NEOM appears as the example of technological utopianism, set against a call to give equal creative attention to practical retrofitting, landscape restoration and material reuse. The essay is part of ArchDaily's September monthly topic, The Lure of Escapism in Architecture and Alternative Worlds.

Attentiveness recognised as a position: Moussavi takes the 2026 Soane Medal, and the committee also credits her with returning the overlooked French architect Renée Gailhoustet to view
Cross-checked against: ArchDaily (29 Sep) · Dezeen (28 Sep) · The Architect's Newspaper (28 Sep) · Harvard GSD announcement · Architects' Journal · world-architects · FAD Magazine

The award and the citation: one medal, two lines worth keeping.The Soane Medal is given once a year by Sir John Soane's Museum in London, with the stated purpose of encouraging a better understanding of the central importance of architecture in people's lives. The 2026 recipient is the British-Iranian architect Farshid Moussavi, professor in practice of architecture at the Harvard Graduate School of Design. The selection committee was chaired by the architect Amin Taha and included the curator Vicky Richardson, and the citation describes a sustained and rigorous contribution to architecture through building, teaching and writing. Two lines from the committee are worth copying down. Taha observes that the profession tends to choose between spectacle and resignation, and that Moussavi instead charts a third path: attentiveness — which elevates attentiveness into a professional position that can be recognised, rather than a description of temperament. Richardson praised her intellectual curiosity and singled out her research on social housing, which brought the long-overlooked French architect Renée Gailhoustet back into view. The second line carries as much weight as the first: it locates the value of scholarship in the question of who gets returned to the historical record.

Practice and the academy: from FOA to FMA, two tracks feeding each other.Moussavi co-founded Foreign Office Architects (FOA) with Alejandro Zaera-Polo in 1993, the practice whose Yokohama International Passenger Terminal established its international standing, and set up Farshid Moussavi Architecture (FMA) in 2011. Recent work includes the Museum of Contemporary Art Cleveland, the La Folie Divine apartments in Montpellier, the Îlot 19 housing at La Défense-Nanterre in Paris, the Harrods Toy Department in London, and the recently completed Ismaili Center Houston. On the teaching side she has held posts at the Architectural Association, UCLA and Princeton among others, and is now professor in practice at Harvard GSD. She has previously received the Jane Drew Prize, the Ada Louise Huxtable Prize and the American Academy of Arts and Letters Architecture Award. What characterises this record is not two parallel lines but two that feed each other: her writing on form and effect comes directly out of the office's projects, and the office's choice of project types is pulled by her research questions — social housing being the clearest of them.

Her own response: the museum itself is the argument.Moussavi's response to the award is not a courtesy but a statement that can be read as method. She takes the Soane as an expression of her own values — that a building can be simultaneously a home, a practice, and a way of thinking, without separating personal from professional concerns. The line fits its source precisely: Sir John Soane's Museum is itself the product of merging Soane's house, his office and his collection, and the terraced houses on Lincoln's Inn Fields were both his residence and the teaching apparatus he used with his students. Read back into her practice, it explains why she both writes books and builds buildings, and it explains why the committee chose attentiveness rather than innovation to define her: attentiveness means refusing to let go of any layer of a thing, rather than making one layer louder.

Context and consequence: what this medal is calibrating.The list of Soane Medal recipients is itself a set of judgements about where architecture matters. The 2025 medal went to Madelon Vriesendorp — an artist who shaped a generation of architects through drawings and objects rather than buildings. Giving the 2026 medal to Moussavi continues the same thread: what is being recognised is the continuity of a body of thought and a way of working, not the finish of any single building. The presentation and the Soane Medal Lecture take place at the Royal Academy in London on 24 November 2026, and the public lecture is part of the award's mechanism — the medal is not only conferred, it requires the recipient to explain their thinking to a public audience. For students, the thing to keep from this year is not who won but the word written into the citation: in an education system that rewards making statements and making shapes, attentiveness has been named explicitly as a capacity worth learning.
