EDITOR'S NOTE

In the first week of September, architecture's "center" shifted in several directions at once. On 3 September the Suzhou Museum of Contemporary Art opened to the public on the shore of Jinji Lake — BIG's first completed art museum, in which twelve pavilions are gathered under a single continuous ribbon roof. The same week, MAD's Lucas Museum of Narrative Art in Los Angeles was declared complete: 1,271 double-curved fibre-reinforced polymer panels, no two alike, will be unveiled on 22 September. On the awards side, the 2026 OBEL Award went to no building at all, but to the public housing model run in Spain's Balearic Islands between 2019 and 2023 — as jury chair Nathalie de Vries put it, innovative architecture can be found not only in design and material choice but in procurement, policy and process. On 7 September the inaugural Pan-African Biennale opens in Nairobi, where artistic director Omar Degan's one-line thesis is "the center has always been here." And in Barcelona, Zhang Li, dean of Tsinghua University's School of Architecture, was elected President of the International Union of Architects for 2026–2029 — the first Chinese architect to hold the post.

Dissolving a museum into a garden: how BIG's Suzhou MoCA handles 60,000 square metres with twelve pavilions
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · Archinect · Wallpaper* · The Plan · China Daily · art4d

The Suzhou Museum of Contemporary Art opened to the public on 3 September on the shore of Jinji Lake, having soft-opened with preview exhibitions on 30 August. The project was delivered by BIG – Bjarke Ingels Group with ARTS Group, VIA.inc and SHUISHI for the Suzhou Industrial Park Culture, Sports and Tourism Bureau, and covers 60,000 square metres. For a practice founded in 2005, it is the first completed art museum.

Concept: a village rather than a building. Museums of this size normally compress every function into one legible mass. Suzhou MoCA does the opposite: twelve discrete pavilions are distributed across the site, linked by covered corridors and gathered into a whole by a single continuous ribbon roof. Bjarke Ingels describes it this way: "By dissolving the museum into a Chinese garden of interconnected galleries, distributed across the site like pavilions in a park, the museum becomes a connection between the city and the lake." The reference is direct — Suzhou's UNESCO-listed classical gardens are not organised around a single dominant object but around the relationships between pavilion, corridor, water and rock.

Spatial strategy: the route before the room. Circulation here is not enter–proceed–exit but a series of choices made while moving between pavilions. The corridors are simultaneously infrastructure and semi-outdoor space for display and pause; the gaps between the twelve volumes admit lake views, courtyards and daylight into the intervals of a visit. Prioritising the interval this way breaks 60,000 square metres into a sequence of graspable fragments, which is the most reliable defence against the fatigue large museums usually induce.

Structure and form: the roof as the only unifying device. What makes twelve volumes read as one building is the continuous undulating roof. Seen from above, its loop approaches the infinity knot of Chinese paper-cutting — a figure that only resolves from the air. At ground level nobody reads the whole shape; visitors experience it instead as a continuously varying eave height and a shifting depth of shadow. At the opening, Ingels called the result "a manifestation of the ancient future of Suzhou and a testament to construction quality and technical sophistication in contemporary Chinese architecture." That points at something concrete: landing an irregular curved roof accurately across twelve supporting volumes at differing levels is a construction problem far more than a formal one.

Context and consequence: from industrial park to cultural destination. The museum sits on the east bank of Jinji Lake in the Suzhou Industrial Park, and its client is the park's culture, sports and tourism bureau — a textbook case of locally-led cultural infrastructure investment. The opening phase presents four exhibitions: "Picasso and the World of His Influence," "Gèn: A Journey Through Art's Transcendence," "Codes & Canopies: Art Beyond the Algorithmic Age," and "BIG: MATERIALISM," curated by the architects themselves. That combination is itself a positioning statement: an internationally legible master show to open the doors, held alongside local contemporary art scholarship and a technical, material-driven argument.

One comparison is worth drawing. Suzhou already has I.M. Pei's Suzhou Museum extension, completed in 2006, which answered the garden tradition head-on through white walls, grey tile and a geometricised landscape. Twenty years later BIG offers a different translation — not reproducing the garden's formal vocabulary but extracting its organisational logic (dispersal, corridor, route, borrowed scenery) and rebuilding it with contemporary structural means. The two buildings now sit in the same city as a pair, and between them they move the question of how tradition enters contemporary architecture a step further.

When the prize goes to the process: the Balearic Social Housing Model and the 2026 OBEL Award
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · Dezeen · METALOCUS · Wallpaper* · world-architects · The Plan

On 1 September the OBEL Foundation announced the 2026 OBEL Award for the Balearic Social Housing Model. What makes this edition unusual is that the recipient is neither a building nor an architect, but a method of public administration operated between 2019 and 2023.

Who did it: an economist and an architect. The model was driven from within IBAVI (Institut Balear de l'Habitatge) by a pairing — Cris Ballester Parets, an economist and then managing director, and Carles Oliver Barceló, the architect heading the technical department. The composition of that partnership already describes the model: half institutional design, half building practice.

Institutional strategy: from passive client to active developer. Spain faces an acute housing shortage and very thin public rental stock. The default arrangement outsources projects and, through rigid tendering, selects the cheapest code-compliant scheme. The Balearic approach rewrote that chain: the administration rebuilt technical capacity in-house and became an active developer; procurement rules and competition formats were redesigned so that lowest price no longer governed the outcome; and innovation was coordinated across projects so that what one experiment learned could be carried into the next. Jury chair Nathalie de Vries stated the reasoning plainly: "What makes the Balearic Social Housing Model remarkable is how innovative architecture can be found not just in design and material choice but also in procurement, policy and process."

Material and climate strategy: a Map of Resources. On the construction side the model relies on a "Map of Resources" that assesses territorial materials against five criteria — carbon impact, availability, performance, cost and scalability — favouring local supply including marés, the honey-coloured local sandstone, and timber. Climate response leans on passive design: orientation, thermal inertia, cross-ventilation and solar protection, which cut embodied and operational emissions and household energy bills at the same time. In social housing that dual effect matters more than usual: low-income tenants are far more exposed to energy costs than average households, so passive strategy is a social instrument as much as an environmental one.

Scale and output. The portfolio includes 24 units at Pere Matutes 72 in Eivissa, 25 temporary cohousing units at Lope de Vega 3 in Palma, 19 units at Salvador Espriu 37 in Palma, and developments of 42 to 43 units elsewhere across the islands. No individual project is large. The value of the model lies in its repeatability rather than in the size of any one scheme.

"Systems' Hack" and one unusual decision. The 2026 theme, "Systems' Hack," treats systems as interconnected operating mechanisms and hacking as strategic intervention to alter how they run. The jury then did something the award has never done before: it extended the theme into spring 2027 with a second award, to recognise another practice engaging systems at a different scale. That decision is itself the signal worth reading — having moved the frame from object to mechanism, the jury evidently thinks the argument is unfinished.

In depth
1,271 panels, none identical: the Lucas Museum's double-curved FRP envelope and a building that has to rock in an earthquake
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · Dezeen · The Architect's Newspaper / Facades+ · CompositesWorld · designboom · Facade Tectonics Institute

The Lucas Museum of Narrative Art, designed by Ma Yansong of MAD Architects, is complete and opens on 22 September. It sits on an 11-acre former surface parking lot in Los Angeles's Exposition Park, measures 300,000 square feet (about 27,900 square metres), and has landscape by Studio-MLA (Mia Lehrer) with Stantec, which also acted as Architect of Record. For students, the part of this building most worth studying is not the shape but how the shape was made buildable.

Material choice: why GFRP rather than GFRC. The envelope comprises 1,271 uniquely shaped fibre-reinforced polymer (FRP/GFRP, Glass Fiber Reinforced Polymer) panels averaging roughly 8 by 32 feet. The design team, including façade consultant Walter P Moore, passed over glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) — the more common choice for curved cladding — in favour of GFRP. The governing reason is weight: GFRP weighs a fraction of GFRC, and on this building reducing mass was not an economic preference but a structural precondition.

Structural logic: base isolation demands a light skin. Los Angeles is high-seismic territory, and the building uses a base isolation system: in an earthquake the superstructure rocks on the isolation layer, cutting the seismic force delivered into the primary structure. The efficiency of that system is directly tied to the mass above it — a heavier envelope means larger loads and displacement demands at the isolators. The 1,271 lightweight GFRP panels are therefore not merely a façade decision; they are what allows the isolation strategy to work at all. It is an unusually clean example of material selection and structural system being mutually conditional.

Fabrication: every panel gets its own mould. The panels are produced by Kreysler & Associates in California, each formed on an individually shaped mould, because no two curvatures repeat across a double-curved surface. The workflow is a digital process developed specifically for FRP's material behaviour: panelising from the three-dimensional model, generating mould geometry, CNC-machining the moulds, then forming, trimming and checking tolerances. Complementing it is a glass fibre reinforced concrete (GFRC) ribbon used to resolve the continuous curve at the building's edge.

Structure and space: a 185-foot steel arch holding up a floating mass. Arched steel beams spanning 185 feet carry the lifted public plaza at the centre of the building; an elliptical oculus opens through it, and three of the five public elevators run in cylindrical glass shafts. Long span and cantilever together produce the impression of a volume hovering above the ground — a familiar MAD motif, but one delivered here by explicit structural means rather than implied by form alone.

Context and consequence. The project was approved in 2017 and broke ground in 2018, with an opening originally planned for 2025. Inside are two 299-seat theatres (the Robert Flaherty and Slavko Vorkapich theatres), roughly 100,000 square feet of gallery space (about 82,000 on the fourth floor and 8,000 on the fifth), a research library, and ten studios and classrooms. The collection runs to more than 40,000 works assembled by George Lucas over five decades, including Norman Rockwell, Frida Kahlo, Jack Kirby, Alison Bechdel, Gordon Parks and Dorothea Lange. Over 300 new trees — jacarandas, oaks, pines — have been planted alongside an amphitheatre, a hanging garden and a waterfall fountain. A freely shaped building that must stand in a seismic zone usually pays for that freedom somewhere in the cladding schedule; this one shows the arithmetic clearly.

"The center has always been here": the first Pan-African Biennale opens tomorrow in Nairobi
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · Wallpaper* · The Architect's Newspaper · Pan-African Biennale · Apsaidal · Empowers Africa

The inaugural Pan-African Biennale (PAB) runs from 7 to 11 September at the Kenyatta International Convention Centre (KICC) in Nairobi. It is the first architecture biennale devoted to spatial practices from and within the African continent and organised entirely under African leadership. The artistic director is the Somali-Italian architect Omar Degan; the biennale has been in public preparation since 2025, and this edition's theme is "Shifting the Center: From Fragility to Resilience."

The curatorial claim: taking back the word "center." Degan compresses the theme into a single sentence: "The center has always been here." The target is a long-standing structural fact — African architecture has tended to appear in international discourse as case study, field site or aid recipient, while agenda-setting happened elsewhere. PAB positions itself to foreground African cities, cultural memory and design agency on a global stage rather than as background annotation.

Structure: three strands. The exhibition is organised through three thematic sections. Land Under Pressure addresses climate change and its impact on settlements and territorial networks; Inherited Knowledge takes up vernacular intelligence and local building know-how; Worlds to Come turns toward African futures and new spatial imaginaries. The sequence itself is an argument: acknowledge the pressure first, return to the existing knowledge base second, and only then propose a horizon — rather than jumping straight to "the future of Africa."

Participation: thirty-plus countries, across disciplines. The announced selection spans more than thirty African nations, among them Algeria, Angola, Benin, Comoros, Djibouti, Equatorial Guinea, Eritrea, Gabon, the Gambia, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Ivory Coast, Lesotho, Libya, Madagascar, Malawi, Mauritania, Mauritius, Morocco, Namibia, Nigeria, Senegal, Seychelles, South Sudan, Sudan, Togo, Tunisia, Zambia and Zimbabwe. Participants are not limited to architecture offices; they include urban researchers, material research collectives, conservation bodies and community-based design groups. Confirmed practices include Djamel Klouche (Algeria), Banga Coletivo (Angola), Ramos Castellano Arquitectos, MASS Design Group and Raas Architects. The organisers' stated ambition is to expand toward all 54 African nations, with this edition as the first attempt.

Why it is worth following from Syracuse. The central question PAB raises — how architecture establishes its own criteria from local materials and inherited building knowledge under conditions of constrained resources, acute climate pressure and rapid urbanisation — is legible from a distance and directly comparable to debates about regionalism and situated construction elsewhere. What distinguishes PAB is that it puts the question at the institutional level: not competing for a booth inside somebody else's biennale, but building one. For architecture students, a first edition is also the most transparent edition — curatorial framework, selection mechanism and funding structure are all still visible, which makes this a live case study in how an academic institution gets constructed.

The opening week unfolds through exhibitions, installations, keynote dialogues and public events across Nairobi and a number of satellite locations.
