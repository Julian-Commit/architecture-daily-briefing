EDITOR'S NOTE

Today's threads all answer the same question: which way should a building grow? Frida Escobedo's answer is downward — MALBA's expansion in Buenos Aires tucks 3,840 square metres beneath Plaza Perú without giving up a single square metre of the public square above. Design Museum Gent's answer is to push into the old city: its new DING wing, five storeys of timber clad in 82,000 bricks pressed from the city's own demolition waste, reopens on 3 October. LTL Architects' answer is to start over with a different material — straw boards compressed to roughly 370 kg/m³ that serve at once as structure, insulation and interior finish, with 111 stacked courses left exposed as the wall surface. And the colour guideline released in Shenzhen moves the question to the opposite end of the scale: when construction shifts from new build to existing stock, colour becomes one of the few variables that can still change a city's face at scale. Anna Puigjaner, meanwhile, takes this year's RIBA Charles Jencks Award — her decade-long Kitchenless City research is about the same thing: redistributing space rather than adding it.

Frida Escobedo buries MALBA's expansion under Plaza Perú: 3,840 m² doubles the exhibition space, construction from 2027, opening late 2029 or early 2030 — alongside a USD 50m trust
Sources: designboom (Sept 18) · The Art Newspaper (Sept 17) · Infobae (Sept 18) · MALBA · Frida Escobedo Studio
The Museo de Arte Latinoamericano de Buenos Aires (MALBA) has selected Mexican architect Frida Escobedo to design an expansion the museum has been pursuing for more than a decade. The scheme places all 3,840 m² of new space beneath the adjacent Plaza Perú, doubling the exhibition area of the existing 7,455 m² building while the square above remains in use as public space. The new programme includes temporary exhibition galleries, an auditorium that can operate independently or as part of the visitor circuit, flexible spaces for public programming, workshops and storage, plus a sculpture garden, new entrances, a landscaped courtyard of roughly 600 m², and an open garden leading to a staircase on Salguero Street. According to The Art Newspaper, construction is expected to begin in 2027, with opening targeted for late 2029 or early 2030. The idea dates back to 2011; Buenos Aires approved it in 2012 on the basis of an early scheme by Uruguayan architect Carlos Ott, with a thirty-year window for construction — the present design is being advanced inside that window. Three further announcements came with the design: founder Eduardo Costantini has established a USD 50 million trust (he currently covers an annual operating deficit of USD 3–4 million and says he is prepared to fund construction himself if needed); he has donated the Daros Latinamerica Collection — 1,232 works by 117 artists, mostly from the 1950s to the 2010s — plus 17 works from his personal collection including Frida Kahlo's Diego y yo (1949); and the Costantini family will become a minority on the board, shifting the institution toward a US-style endowment model in order to recruit new donors.

LTL Architects completes the All Straw House: straw compressed to about 370 kg/m³ serves as structure, insulation and finish at once, with 111 exposed courses forming the interior wall
Sources: designboom (Sept 15) · Architectural Record (September 2026 issue) · Archinect · MaterialDistrict · LTL Architects
The All Straw House stands in New York's Hudson Valley, designed by LTL Architects (principals Paul Lewis, Marc Tsurumaki and David J. Lewis) with Guy Nordenson and Associates as structural engineer, prefabricated over two months at the Princeton University Architecture Lab and assembled on site. The house is built from 16 large prefabricated straw units. The compressed straw board reaches roughly 370 kilograms per cubic metre — more than three times the density of a conventional straw bale — and it is precisely that density that lets a single material carry structure, insulation and interior finish simultaneously, without the separate load-bearing frame and render coat that conventional straw-bale construction requires. Inside, 111 courses of straw are left exposed as the finished surface, with a timber ring beam inserted every 22 courses for lateral stability. The exterior rainscreen is formed of 33 prefabricated thatch panels, six to eight inches thick, each overlapping the one below by 21 inches. The straw boards were manufactured by Durra Panel, fastenings by Beck Fastening, thatching by McGhee + Co. Roof Thatchers; photography by Michael Moran. The prototype was supported by Princeton University's Sustainability of our Planet fund. In the architects' words: "The density of the material, its ability to be compressed and stacked, and its limits in forming the building all become design parameters" — the building's tapered profile is what the corbelling limit of straw and the required roof pitch, taken together, leave behind.

Design Museum Gent's DING wing is finished and reopens 3 October: 8,600 m², five storeys of timber, and a façade of 82,000 unfired bricks pressed from the city's own demolition waste
Sources: Carmody Groarke · Design Museum Gent · BC Materials (material development) · The Architect's Newspaper (Fall 2026 openings preview) · Dezeen · architectura.be
Design Museum Gent reopens on 3 October 2026 with its new wing, DING. The project sits in the historic core of Ghent, a UNESCO World Heritage city, with a total floor area of 8,600 m², designed by London practice Carmody Groarke with ATAMA and RE-ST — the competition-winning scheme was titled "Museum in the Width" — with structural engineering by Ney & Partners, services by Boydens Engineering and acoustics by Daidalos Peutz, for clients Sogent and the museum. The new wing is five storeys and built principally in timber, part of an effort to make the museum fossil-free and highly flexible. The technical interest is the façade: the Gent Waste Brick was developed by Carmody Groarke with materials designers BC Materials and Local Works Studio, and contains 63% recycled aggregate from the city of Ghent itself — mainly crushed concrete, masonry and glass from demolished buildings. The bricks are never kiln-fired; they are lime-bound and cure by carbonation at ambient conditions. The product was certified in September 2022; between February and July 2024, 82,000 blocks were produced over 104 working days at the Arsenaal site less than 5 kilometres from the museum, at 1,200–1,320 a day. BC Materials' comparison: a Gent Waste Brick carries roughly one-third the embodied carbon of a typical Belgian fired clay brick. The project is worth reading against this issue's other lead, the All Straw House — in neither case did the architects select from an existing catalogue; they made the material first and derived the construction from it.

China releases its first colour standard for urban renewal: the Design Guidelines for Exterior Colour Renewal of Existing Buildings, launched in Shenzhen on 17 September at the 4th China Architectural Culture Forum
Sources: PR Newswire Asia (Sept 17) · Nippon Paint China · ifeng Shenzhen · China Architectural Culture Centre (organiser)
On 17 September 2026 the 4th China Architectural Culture Forum — themed "Deepening the Substance of Renewal, Shaping Urban Character" and hosted by the China Architectural Culture Centre — convened in Shenzhen, where the Design Guidelines for Exterior Colour Renewal of Existing Buildings were formally released. According to the lead editors, this is China's first group standard devoted specifically to colour design within urban renewal. It was co-edited by Nippon Paint China and the China Academy of Building Research, initiated in 2024 by the China Architectural Culture Centre, the China Academy of Building Research and Nippon Paint, and developed over roughly three years with the Academy of Arts & Design at Tsinghua University among other contributors. The logic of the document is to move colour from "a decorative choice" to "a checkable design variable": it weighs the surrounding natural environment, regional culture, building function and material performance, sets out differentiated colour strategies for residential, commercial, industrial and educational building types, and establishes quantitative criteria for colour combination, brightness and saturation so that designers, contractors and administrators share a single reference. The problem it names directly is the sameness of Chinese cities — qiān chéng yī miàn, "a thousand cities with one face." For architecture and planning students the significance is not the colour chips: once renewal of existing stock becomes the main arena, and most existing buildings cannot have their structure or massing touched, exterior colour is one of the few variables still open to large-scale, low-cost redesign — and until now it had almost no citable technical basis.

Putting 3,840 m² under a public square without taking a metre of it: Escobedo's MALBA scheme is less an addition than a re-sorting of the whole museum
Cross-checked: designboom (Sept 18) · The Art Newspaper (Sept 17) · Infobae (Sept 18) · Cadena Láser · MALBA · Frida Escobedo Studio

Concept: replace the word "expansion" with "continuity." Urban museums usually grow in one of two ways — add storeys, or build a neighbouring block and connect it with a bridge. Both pay the same price: a piece of ground. MALBA's existing building is 7,455 m², sitting in Buenos Aires's Palermo district next to Plaza Perú; a conventional expansion would have consumed that square. Escobedo's scheme goes under instead: all 3,840 m² of new floor area sits beneath the plaza, and the plaza above remains a working public square. designboom summarises the scheme with a single word — continuity — and it is worth being precise about what is continuous. Not the building mass, but the urban ground. That distinction matters for students: going under and lifting up both avoid occupying a site, but they are opposite urban gestures. The first surrenders the building's external image and buys, in exchange, a piece of ground whose ownership does not change.

Spatial strategy: not a longer circuit, but the end of the single circuit. The new programme covers temporary galleries, an auditorium, flexible spaces for public programming, workshops and storage, plus a sculpture garden, new entrances, a landscaped courtyard of roughly 600 m² and an open garden leading down to a staircase on Salguero Street. The decisive item is how the auditorium is defined: it can operate independently or as part of the visitor circuit. This, designboom writes, gives the expansion "a second scale beyond its added square footage, stretching Malba's activity across more hours and allowing several parts of the institution to function at once." A conventional museum is a line — ticket, entrance, galleries, exit — and when it closes, everything stops. What Escobedo offers is a plan that can be split into several independent loops: the galleries can be shut while the auditorium hosts an evening lecture; the sculpture garden can stay open to the street while a workshop is in session. That is why designboom calls it "a reorganization of Malba as much as an addition." The 600 m² sunken landscaped courtyard and the new entrances handle the other hard problem: orientation and daylight are what underground space loses first, and a planted opening with sky in it works better than any wayfinding system.

Structure and technique: we know the question, not yet the answer. The project is in design; neither the museum nor any of the published reports has released the structural system, excavation and dewatering approach, waterproofing detail or services strategy, and this paper will not speculate. But the brief is already legible. Excavating nearly 4,000 m² beneath a city square that stays open to the public — without interrupting its use, and without disturbing mature trees or the existing museum's foundations — puts far heavier demands on shoring and construction sequencing than an above-ground building of the same area. Add the need to bring daylight into underground galleries and to hold the temperature, humidity and vibration tolerances that artworks require, and those three items — shoring, daylighting, environmental control — are typically where the cost and the programme of such a project are decided. The Art Newspaper gives the milestones: construction from 2027, opening late 2029 or early 2030, roughly three years on site.

Background and consequence: a fourteen-year-old permit, and a USD 50m trust. The expansion was first proposed in 2011 at roughly 3,840 m² — the same figure announced now. Buenos Aires approved it in 2012 on the basis of an early scheme by Uruguayan architect Carlos Ott, granting a thirty-year construction window. Escobedo therefore did not inherit a blank sheet but a permit with a legally fixed envelope; her task was to reorganise the contents within a volume already agreed. The governance changes announced alongside the design deserve equal attention. Founder Eduardo Costantini has set up a USD 50 million trust (he currently covers an annual operating deficit of USD 3–4 million); he has donated the Daros Latinamerica Collection — 1,232 works by 117 artists — plus 17 works from his own holdings including Frida Kahlo's Diego y yo (1949); and the Costantini family will step back to a minority position on the board, moving toward a US-style endowment model. Costantini was direct about why this scheme won: "One of the things we liked about her project was how it brings the two buildings together." Read together, what was announced this week is less an expansion design than the full choreography of a privately founded museum converting itself into a long-term public institution — the architecture is one movement in it. For Escobedo personally, this is the second international museum commission in two years, after the Metropolitan Museum's Tang Wing, which broke ground on 15 September (reported in our No. 012).

In Seoul's Yeonhui-dong, a 1968 single-storey house was kept because its ceilings were higher than the neighbours': a.co.lab's Saekdong House, 126 m²
Sources: ArchDaily (Sept 18) · a.co.lab · Choi Yongjoon and Kyung Roh (photography)
Saekdong House is in Yeonhui-dong, Seoul, designed by a.co.lab (lead architects Isak Chung and Jinpyo Hong, lead team Jihyeong Lee, design team Juyoun Lee), 126 m², completed in 2026. The name comes from saekdong, the traditional Korean pattern of coloured stripes. The subject of the renovation is a single-storey residence built in 1968, and what made it worth keeping is specific: both its ground-floor level and its ceiling heights are noticeably greater than those of neighbouring houses of the same period. In old-house renovation that is a scarce asset, because once ceiling height is demolished it cannot be recovered. The design therefore chose retention over replacement, extending within the existing spatial dimensions to meet contemporary living requirements. Exposed timber beams run through the interior, with concrete, glass and brick as secondary materials. For students working on adaptive reuse, the reading is this: first inventory which conditions on the site are non-renewable — ceiling height, orientation, mature trees, structural span — and only then decide where the renovation's boundary falls.
HCCH Studio builds a deliberately "low-tech" reception hall on Shanghai's Yangpu waterfront: 300 m², for an embodied-intelligence company
Sources: ArchDaily (Sept 19) · HCCH Studio · Qingyan Zhu (photography)
Bothaus Coffee – Sudo Developer Center occupies the lower floor of a tower on Shanghai's Yangpu waterfront, serving as the reception and exhibition space for Sudo Technology's offices and robotics laboratory. It covers 300 m² and was completed in 2026. HCCH Studio led the design, with a team of Hao Chen, Chenchen Hu, Qisen Yang, Puchun Zhang and Yazhou Ding. The client brief asked for a "low-tech feel" — the reasoning being that the deeper technology embeds itself in everyday life, the less conspicuous it ought to be. Materials are glass, concrete, brick and wood. That a company working on embodied intelligence chose to present itself through a coffee shop with no visible trace of technology says more about how tech firms now regard the sign of "futurity" than any parametric façade could.
A climate visitor centre in Oerlinghausen, Germany: 681 m², with DBCO using wood as structure and as finish, inside and out
Sources: ArchDaily (Sept 19) · DBCO · Patrick Johannsen (photography)
The Klimaerlebniswelt (Climate Experience World) visitor centre sits in Oerlinghausen, North Rhine-Westphalia, designed by DBCO (leads Martin Keßler and Gesa Robbert), 681 m², completed in 2024. It is the entry building for a public climate-education attraction and contains exhibition and foyer space. The approach is direct: wood runs through interior and exterior alike, acting as both structure and finish with no applied decorative layer. A building whose subject is climate makes its material choice part of the exhibition content — and small public buildings of this kind often make better teaching cases than large cultural projects, precisely because every construction junction is still visible.
Common Days, a three-storey gathering space in Maros, Indonesia: 269 m², perforated terracotta screens filter the morning sun and draw in hill breezes, with andesite laid in squares inside
Sources: designboom (Sept 19) · INS Studio
Common Days is located in Maros, South Sulawesi, on the outskirts of Makassar, designed by INS Studio under principal Wisnu Wardhana, with a built area of 269 m². Each level has a job: the ground floor is a coffee shop acting as the social hub; the second floor is workspace with meeting rooms and an outdoor terrace; the roof garden is left flexible for exhibitions and community gatherings. The materials are all locally sourced — andesite stone laid in a distinctive square pattern dominates the interiors, a perforated terracotta breeze-block screen forms the exterior envelope, and locally manufactured terracotta tiles finish the second-floor terrace. The perforated skin solves a specific climatic problem: filtering the intense morning sun on the front elevation while letting natural ventilation from the surrounding hills pass straight through. The third storey serves another purpose, lifting sightlines clear of the surroundings so that Bukit Cenrana, the local landmark, reads whole. A moss wall cultivated by local gardeners lines the workspace.

In Depth
4,300 buildings, 481 hectares, a 1,300-page nomination: how Asmara turned a colonial city into the modernist settlement inscribed on the World Heritage List in its entirety
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (feature, Sept 18) · UNESCO World Heritage Centre · RIBA Journal · EEAS (European External Action Service) · Shabait, Eritrean Ministry of Information · Dawan Africa

Concept: a modernist city inscribed whole. On 8 July 2017, at the 41st session of the World Heritage Committee in Poland, Asmara — the capital of Eritrea — was inscribed on the World Heritage List as "Asmara: A Modernist African City." The inscription carried two firsts: it was the first modernist urban site anywhere to be listed in its entirety, and Africa's first explicitly modernist World Heritage Site. UNESCO's citation describes it as an exceptional example of early modernist urbanism at the beginning of the twentieth century and of its adaptation to an African context. Note where the emphasis falls: what is being valued is not a handful of celebrated buildings but an entire planning logic and its mutation in a foreign setting — which is exactly why the case matters to architecture and planning students.

Spatial strategy: drawing a heritage boundary around a whole city. The inscribed property covers 481 hectares, with a buffer zone of 1,203 hectares — 1,684 hectares in total — encompassing the historic urban core of the capital. Within it are roughly 400 modernist buildings from the Italian colonial period (1893–1941). But the figure that conveys the labour involved is a different one: the complete three-volume UNESCO submission ran to 1,300 pages, accompanied by a survey of more than 4,300 buildings and public spaces and over 80,000 scanned archival documents and drawings. Set those numbers side by side and the argument becomes clear — 400 is the roll of masterpieces; 4,300 is the city. Heritage definition here is not a matter of selecting monuments but of conceding that the ordinary fabric of a city carries value as a whole. Asmara survived intact enough to be listed this way for an unromantic reason: a long period of relative isolation insulated it from the redevelopment that swept most cities in the second half of the twentieth century.

Form and type: Rationalism, Futurism, Art Deco — and a petrol station. The buildings cited in ArchDaily's feature include St Mary's Orthodox Cathedral, Our Lady of the Rosary Catholic Cathedral and the Fiat Tagliero building. Stylistically, Art Deco, Futurism and Rationalism all appear. Fiat Tagliero is the most-quoted case: a 1938 petrol station whose two wings cantilever more than thirty metres each without columns, in the shape of an aircraft. It recurs in architectural history not only for its silhouette but because landing the machine aesthetic of Italian Futurism directly on an everyday service facility was rare in Italy itself. That is Asmara's characteristic condition: modernism here is not the language of monuments but the language of petrol stations, cinemas, markets and apartment blocks. The other side must be stated plainly — this urban fabric was produced under colonial rule, and the title of ArchDaily's feature names the tension outright: a colonial project that became a national symbol. In Asmara the value of the heritage and the politics that produced it cannot be separated.

Conservation machinery, and what remains uncertain. Management is coordinated between the Central Region Administration and the Asmara Heritage Project, covering building permits, conservation approvals, maintenance standards and heritage planning. The planning instruments — an Urban Conservation Master Plan and accompanying technical regulations — set requirements for building heights, materials, façades and restoration practice. Supported intervention clusters, backed by UNESCO and the European Union, include completing the Conservation Master Plan, drafting planning norms and regulations, an advanced professional training course in conservation, training for artisans and skilled workers, and public engagement and awareness activities. Those last two matter more than they sound: for a city holding 4,300 historic buildings, whether it can keep supplying craftspeople who can repair render, steel windows and terrazzo will determine conservation outcomes more than any written code. For students at Syracuse Architecture, Asmara is worth reading as a control case. When modernist heritage conservation is discussed, the default scenario is the restoration of a single celebrated building in Europe or North America. Asmara poses a different problem: how to maintain a city that is itself the monument, under sharply limited resources.

When one material is structure, insulation and wall at once: LTL compresses straw to 370 kg/m³, and the material decides the shape of the house
Cross-checked: designboom (Sept 15) · Architectural Record (September 2026 issue) · Archinect · MaterialDistrict · LTL Architects · Guy Nordenson and Associates (structure)

Concept: not building with straw, but building in straw — a tectonics. Straw-bale construction has more than a century of history in North America, but it has stayed in the position of infill: a timber frame carries the load, bales are packed into it, and render protects the outside. What LTL Architects (principals Paul Lewis, Marc Tsurumaki and David J. Lewis) set out to test with the All Straw House in New York's Hudson Valley is a different proposition — if straw is compressed hard enough, can it stop being infill and become the structure itself? The answer lands on a number: the compressed straw board used here reaches roughly 370 kilograms per cubic metre, more than three times the density of a conventional bale. At that density the material acquires compressive capacity, thermal performance and a surface that can be left exposed — collapsing frame, insulation layer and interior finish into a single layer. Archinect describes the direction as a new straw tectonics, and the phrase is accurate: the shift happens at the level of construction logic, not material substitution.

Form: the geometry was not drawn, it was calculated from the material. The house is assembled from 16 large prefabricated straw units, produced over two months at the Princeton University Architecture Lab and trucked to site. Inside, 111 courses of straw are left exposed as the finished surface, with a timber ring beam inserted every 22 courses to provide lateral stability — the only non-straw structural intervention in the building, its position and spacing set directly by the course count. The tapered, inward-stepping massing is not a stylistic choice: straw corbelling has a limit on how far each course can project inward, and the roof must reach a certain pitch to shed water. Squeeze those two constraints together and one shape remains. In the architects' own words: "The density of the material, its ability to be compressed and stacked, and its limits in forming the building all become design parameters." That sentence is worth copying out. It describes a route to form that runs opposite to parametric modelling — constraints first, form afterwards.

Construction: straw fears water, so 33 thatch panels hang outside it. Moisture is the weak point of high-density straw, so the rain barrier is handled as a separate system: 33 prefabricated thatch panels, six to eight inches thick, each overlapping the one below by 21 inches, stacking into a tightly packed reed surface. This is a textbook rainscreen — the outer layer sheds water without sealing, allowing the cavity behind it to ventilate and dry, which decouples waterproofing from insulation. The division of labour is worth recording too: straw boards by Durra Panel, fastenings by Beck Fastening, thatching by McGhee + Co. Roof Thatchers, structure by Guy Nordenson and Associates, photography by Michael Moran. That a prototype this small required four specialist suppliers plus a top-tier structural office is itself the finding: the cost of such experiments lies not in the material but in the fact that, absent a mature supply chain, every junction has to be solved from scratch.

Background and consequence: a prototype house answering an industry-scale question. The project was supported by Princeton University's Sustainability of our Planet fund and is explicitly a prototype, not a market house. Its significance is that it pushes bio-based materials — a direction discussed at length in recent years — to the point where a structural engineer will sign off on them. Straw sequesters carbon as it grows, so the carbon case for replacing mineral- or fossil-based assemblies is straightforward; the obstacle was never the carbon arithmetic but load capacity, fire and moisture. The All Straw House does not claim all three are solved. What it does is advance two of them — structure and moisture — and leave behind reusable figures: 370 kg/m³, 111 courses, a ring beam every 22 courses, 33 thatch panels, 21-inch laps. Architectural Record called it, in its September 2026 issue, a new way of building. That is heavy praise for a small house; but given that it answers both how it is built and why it has this shape, the praise is not excessive.

Read the Gent Waste Brick as an industrial ledger: two permanent workers, 104 working days, 82,000 blocks, 1,200–1,320 a day
Sources: BC Materials (producer) · Carmody Groarke · Dezeen · Fast Company · Archinect
This issue's lead story covers what the Gent Waste Brick is made of; set out here is how it was produced, because that is what decides whether an experimental material can actually clad an 8,600 m² building. Production was sited at the Arsenaal, a former railway facility less than 5 kilometres from the museum — not for symbolism but to hold transport emissions down. Between February and July 2024, 82,000 blocks were made over 104 working days at 1,200–1,320 a day, by a crew of just two permanent workers plus rotating support. The process is mixing, pressing, setting and curing, using hydraulic presses and conveyor systems, all electrically driven. Not firing the bricks is the decisive carbon move: lime carbonates and binds at ambient conditions, cutting out the single most energy-intensive step in fired brick, and the result carries roughly one-third the embodied carbon of a standard Belgian clay brick. Community brick-making workshops ran alongside production. The figures give students a comparable scale: two people, five months, eighty thousand bricks — that is what "circular recycled material" looks like on a construction site when you write it into a design statement.

One more figure from the All Straw House: 16 prefabricated units, two months in the lab — keeping the uncertainty in the workshop rather than on site
Sources: designboom (Sept 15) · Archinect · MaterialDistrict · LTL Architects
This issue's lead story and in-depth piece cover the material logic of the All Straw House; here is the organisational decision that is easy to skip past. The entire house was broken into 16 large prefabricated straw units, produced over two months at the Princeton University Architecture Lab before being trucked to the Hudson Valley for assembly. For a material with no mature code and no crew that has handled it before, that decision is the critical one: compaction density, alignment between courses, the seating of ring beams — variables like these are heavily exposed to weather and to crew experience if resolved outdoors on a live site. Moved into a lab, they sit inside a controlled environment, and the site is left with lifting and connection only. Students working on material-experiment topics routinely underestimate this: whether a new material reaches a building usually depends less on its performance curve than on whether a matching prefabrication and assembly process can be designed for it.

From the Kitchenless City to a chair in Architecture and Care at ETH: Puigjaner's Jencks Award signals that housing research has changed its question
Cross-checked: e-flux (September) · Archinect / Bustler · World-Architects · RIBA · ETH Zurich Department of Architecture · Harvard GSD Wheelwright Prize (2016)

The prize: given only to those whose theory and practice both count. The RIBA Charles Jencks Award states its criterion precisely — a major recent international contribution to both the theory and the practice of architecture. That "both" is the operative word: neither pure theorists nor pure practitioners qualify. The endowment comes from the prize money Charles Jencks received with the 1992 Nara Gold Medal. Past honorees include Rem Koolhaas, Zaha Hadid, Steven Holl and Herzog & de Meuron, and more recently Dogma, Forensic Architecture and DAAR. Read those recent years in sequence and a tendency is clear: the award's centre of gravity has moved from individuals who advanced the discipline through formal language toward collectives and scholars who redefine what the discipline takes as its object. Puigjaner belongs to the second line. This year's jury comprised RIBA President Chris Williamson, The Cosmic House founder Lily Jencks, Dr Adrian Lahoud, Edwin Heathcote and Samaneh Moafi; the three other nominees were Arno Brandlhuber, Fernanda Canales and Mabel Wilson.

The research: from one kitchen to the economic structure of the dwelling. Puigjaner became widely known through the 2016 Wheelwright Prize at Harvard GSD — a USD 100,000 travelling research grant for an early-career architect — awarded for "Kitchenless City: Architectural Systems for Social Welfare." The entry point is tiny: why does the standard plan of the modern dwelling require a private kitchen in every unit? Following that question she spent two years studying collective housing models in China, Korea, India, Russia, Sweden and Brazil, starting from the historic type of the housing block with a collective kitchen and its associated dining rooms, lounges and service areas. The force of this line of work is that it converts an apparently technical question of plan configuration back into a question of economics and gendered labour: a private kitchen means domestic work is atomised inside each unit and cannot be socialised — and statistically that work falls mainly to women. Which is to say the housing plan is not neutral.

From research to a chair: care as a category in architecture. In January 2023 Puigjaner took up the professorship of Architecture and Care in the Department of Architecture at ETH Zurich, a cross-disciplinary chair endowed by the Stavros Niarchos Foundation as part of the ETH Rehab Initiative. The same year she launched the Care XYZ project within the department, investigating the spatiality of shared kinships and attempting to reimagine care systems through both the commons and the institutional. The disciplinary significance of this step exceeds the personal: when a leading technical university establishes an architecture chair named for care, the subject has moved out of critical discourse and into the teaching establishment. RIBA's citation — that her work with MAIO "has reframed the question of how to live together collectively," repositioning housing as collective infrastructure and social care — is a summary of exactly this trajectory.

What it offers students, and where to catch her. MAIO is the Barcelona practice Puigjaner co-founded, and its built work and its research are two sides of one thing: projects such as 110 Rooms make "rooms of equal size with no assigned function" a planning principle, letting residents decide which room is a bedroom and which a living room. That is not a stylistic preference; it is the research landing directly in plan. For students working on housing, the method is transferable: start by asking where a taken-for-granted planning convention came from and who bears its cost, then use the plan to revise it — rather than starting from massing studies.
