EDITOR'S NOTE

Four threads worth reading together today. In Toyama, a company that manufactures structural timber connectors turned its own headquarters into a public test of its product: 210 mm-thick, 3,000 mm-wide CLT panels slotted into one another to form a three-dimensional lattice in which every member is simultaneously column and beam. The architects call it going beyond the Domino system. In Shenzhen, URBANUS packed 39,752 square meters onto a 5,761-square-meter site and pushed the public plaza up to levels 5 and 9 — in a district this dense, public space has nowhere to go but up. In Lima, a citizens' network of archaeologists and historians is pursuing a collective World Heritage nomination for the capital's 600-plus pre-Hispanic sites, timed deliberately to land before the October 4 local elections. And in Venice, the Luxembourg Pavilion announced Excess!, a 2027 Biennale proposal about the overconsumption of living space, argued through a pairing of Europe's wealthiest country with Vietnam. What these four have in common: none of them is an argument about form.

A timber-connector manufacturer turns its own headquarters into a proof of its product: MOUNT FUJI ARCHITECTS STUDIO slots 210 mm-thick, 3,000 mm-wide CLT panels into a three-dimensional lattice across 499.64 sqm — winner of a 2026 Architectural Institute of Japan Works Award
Sources: gooood (Sept 30) · ArchDaily project page (Sept 24) · STROOG Co., Ltd. official site · Architectural Institute of Japan, 2026 Works Award announcement · MOUNT FUJI ARCHITECTS STUDIO
The building sits in Toyama Prefecture, Japan: 905.04 sqm site, 494.02 sqm building area, 499.64 sqm total floor area, structural engineering by KMC (Ken Kamachi), photography by Ryota Atarashi. The client, STROOG, manufactures structural timber connectors — hardware for pinned, rigid, truss and hybrid joints. The architects reduced the entire primary structure to a single component family: CLT (cross-laminated timber) panels, all 3,000 mm wide and 210 mm thick, varying in length from 4,500 to 12,000 mm. Slots are cut wherever members need to meet, and the panels are assembled into a lattice by knock-down construction. Because CLT has strong axes in two perpendicular directions, each panel works both as a column carrying vertical load and as a beam resisting horizontal force; because it is solid laminated timber, slots can be positioned anywhere to the nearest millimetre, and the engagement depth of the slot itself sets the structural span of the joint. The result is a three-dimensional system free of grid-module constraints — what the architects describe as moving beyond the Domino system.

When there is no ground left for a plaza: URBANUS stands a 39,752-square-meter civic center on end in Shenzhen, then hangs three plazas off it — at grade, at level 5 and at level 9
Cross-checked: gooood (Sept 30, completed-project release) · URBANUS project page · gooood (August 2019, design-stage release) · archina (URBANUS practice profile) · URBANUS Shenzhen project map

Design concept: dismantling the assumption that public space means a plaza at grade. The Yuehai Culture and Sports Center occupies a site in Yuehai Subdistrict, Nanshan District, Shenzhen: 5,761 sqm of land carrying 39,752 sqm of building, 12 storeys above ground and 3 below, 69.5 m tall. Lead architect Meng Yan of URBANUS; design 2016–2024, construction 2018–2025, completed at the end of 2025. Start with the relationship between those two numbers — 5,761 and 39,752. The site is almost entirely built over, at a floor area ratio approaching 7. Under that constraint, the default move for a district-scale civic center is to seal library, theater, pools and courts into separate boxes, link them by corridors, and leave a token patch of paving at grade to satisfy the word "plaza." URBANUS states the intent differently: to break the notion that public open space is confined to ground-level plazas. The underlying judgment is blunt — in a district as dense as Yuehai there is no ground left for a plaza, so the plaza has to go up.

Spatial strategy: three platforms, three concentrations of publicness. The completed scheme sets three principal public platforms in section. At grade is the Community Life Plaza, the most open of the three, meshed directly with the street; the library entrance and a 24-hour self-service book return station sit on this level, carrying the kind of publicness you use in passing. Level 5 holds the Elevated Urban Living Room, positioned exactly at the seam between the cultural and the sports programs, functioning as the building's interchange and lingering floor. Level 9 is the Sky Sports Garden, the quietest and the most panoramic, with an outdoor tennis court on the roof above. Outdoor escalators, landscape stairs and public corridors connect all three, and URBANUS is emphatic that this vertical public route can be walked from end to end without a ticket to any venue. That detail is what makes or breaks the concept: put the turnstiles on the ground floor and the "three-dimensional plaza" is merely a lobby for paid space.

Structure and construction: a steel frame absorbing wildly different spans and loads in one stack. Above grade the building is steel-framed, enclosed by curtain wall, perforated metal panels and prefabricated wall systems. That choice is forced by the program schedule. A 25 m lap pool plus a youth pool and a children's wading pool impose enormous water loads and waterproofing requirements. The climbing gym needs a triple-height atrium through levels 8 to 10 and a wall base able to take dynamic loads. The badminton and basketball courts on level 10 demand long column-free spans. The rooftop tennis court pushes load and enclosure height up one more time. Stacking all of this over a single 5,761 sqm footprint means the vertical load path has to transfer at several levels — and steel handles transfer floors and long spans more readily than concrete. The perforated metal does double duty on the facade: shading by day, and by night letting the activity inside register semi-transparently from the street, an effect URBANUS sums up as an everyday visual theater. Construction cost, transfer-floor detailing and curtain wall build-up have not been disclosed publicly, and this briefing will not speculate.

Context and consequence: one of Shenzhen's most expensive districts gets a building with no admission fee. Yuehai Subdistrict is the core of Nanshan's tech economy, where land values are extreme and sites for public facilities are scarce. Allocating 5,761 sqm there to a community culture and sports center is itself a planning decision. What deserves a design student's attention is how the building handles the type: it declines the route of monumental massing and signature form, and spends its entire design budget on the vertical public route instead. Set it against the city's large cultural landmarks — the opera house, the art museums — and the division of labour is clear: those address civic image, this one addresses whether there is anywhere to go after work. For students at Syracuse Architecture, the useful comparison is the North American community recreation center: the same program schedule, built at FAR 1 versus FAR 7, produces two entirely different buildings, and whether publicness stays at grade or gets lifted nine floors up determines who actually walks in.

ROOM 401 / Design Order Group, Tayuan compound, Beijing: two crossing load-bearing walls cut 49.6 sqm into three near-identical "quadrants" — the response was to insert a timber box into each one
Sources: gooood (Sept 30) · Design Order Group
The apartment is in the Tayuan residential compound near Beijing's North Third Ring Road: 49.6 sqm, completed April 2025 to April 2026. The obstacle in the original plan is structural — two load-bearing walls, one running north–south and one east–west, cross at the center and divide the unit into three almost identical quadrants. The walls cannot move, so occupants pass through narrow openings between them and the space reads as fragmented. Rather than fight the structure, the designers inserted a semi-open timber box into each quadrant: high-density functions (entry, kitchen, bathroom, laundry, storage, work surface) are concentrated inside the boxes, while everything outside stays deliberately loose. Box walls and ceilings are site-fabricated birch plywood; box floors are brown mosaic tile; concrete is hand-polished and left exposed with no dropped ceilings; floors outside the boxes are blue tile, and the brick walls are finished in white latex paint or prefabricated terrazzo depending on whether the zone is wet or dry.
Lilac Apartment / BUREAU, Lisbon: 44 sqm, with the entrance hall turned into the kitchen, the central bedroom into wet areas, and a continuous loop wrapped around the core
Sources: designboom (Sept 30) · BUREAU (Daniel Zamarbide, Carine Pimenta, Galliane Zamarbide)
The 44-square-meter apartment in Lisbon was reorganized around movement, light and everyday rituals. The former entrance hall became the kitchen; the bedroom at the center of the plan became the wet areas; and a continuous passage now wraps the core, linking rooms through symmetrical openings so that circulation is a loop rather than a back-and-forth along a corridor. Translucent and mirrored materials are used throughout to build visual depth through reflection and transmission, with the designers citing the fluidity of cinematic space as their reference. Not fencing the ruins off, but wiring them into an election: Lima's collective nomination of 600-plus pre-Hispanic sites is a public question about what kind of city this is
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (Sept 28) · El Peruano (two pieces: the nomination initiative and the "Ciudad huaca" commentary) · El Comercio (Lima) · Caretas · ATV Perú · Red Lima Milenaria / Lima Milenaria public materials

The facts: a metropolis with six hundred ruins inside it. Metropolitan Lima holds roughly eleven million people and, within the same boundaries, more than 600 archaeological sites plus a number of pre-Hispanic irrigation canals — some of which still carry water. The campaign is led by Red Lima Milenaria, a citizens' network of archaeologists, historians and cultural activists founded by Javier Lizarzaburu. The first slate of nominated sites includes El Paraíso in San Martín de Porres (c. 2000 BCE), Pucllana in Miraflores (5th century CE), the Campoy fortress in San Juan de Lurigancho (12th century CE) and sections of the roughly 2,000-year-old Surco canal in San Borja. Four sites spanning more than four millennia, distributed across four non-adjacent districts: the composition of that list already tells you what is being proposed. This is not a nomination of a monument. It is a nomination of a condition of distribution.

Spatial strategy: rewriting points as a network. Conventional heritage practice draws a boundary, establishes a buffer zone and isolates the site from the city in order to protect it — which is largely how Lima's huacas (pre-Hispanic adobe platforms and temple mounds) have been handled: a perimeter wall, a cleared lot, and development continuing unchanged around it. The cost of that approach is that the site becomes a void in the urban fabric, neither used nor understood, sustained indefinitely by public money. Red Lima Milenaria's proposal changes the scale and the unit of account. By bundling scattered sites and canals as a serial property, it makes the spatial relationships between them — rather than the integrity of any single site — the core of the case. Technically this follows UNESCO's serial nomination route; in planning terms it means these points would have to enter one metropolitan-scale framework for conservation and use, instead of being disposed of district by district.

Conditions and conflicts: urban pressure and a maintenance gap. ArchDaily summarizes the situation as "intense urban pressure and inadequate maintenance," which is not an abstraction. These adobe structures are wedged between contemporary housing and commercial development and face informal occupation, erosion, dumping and infrastructure cutting through them. Adobe is acutely sensitive to moisture, and maintenance is not cheap in a city under near-permanent coastal fog (garúa). If inscription succeeds, what arrives first is not money but an obligatory management plan and periodic reporting duties — simultaneously a lever and a burden for a municipal system with limited fiscal and administrative capacity. The network's own framing of its goals is correspondingly restrained: improve conservation conditions and public awareness, and get the Peruvian government to formally prepare a nomination dossier.

Why the timing sits just before an election. The initiative was made public deliberately ahead of Peru's municipal and regional elections on October 4, 2026. The network's stated reason is to use the start of a new political cycle to force the question of what place this heritage should occupy in the city model Lima says it wants. That step is worth noting for architecture and planning students: it translates heritage conservation out of the technical remit of a cultural ministry and onto an electoral agenda. Whether a 2,000-year-old canal fragment counts as an obstacle or an asset is, in the end, a political question rather than an archaeological one. For students at Syracuse Architecture, this reads usefully against American cities whose historic neighborhoods were severed by highways: in both cases, survival depends on whether the heritage can be rewritten into the daily operation of the city, not merely onto a list. Note that the submission timetable for the formal dossier and the final list of sites have not been announced by the Peruvian government, and this briefing will not speculate.

In Depth
Every panel is both column and beam: the STROOG Headquarters slots 210 mm CLT into itself to get out from under the column grid
Cross-checked: gooood (Sept 30) · ArchDaily project page (Sept 24) · STROOG Co., Ltd. official site (product and headquarters materials) · Architectural Institute of Japan, 2026 Works Award announcement · GA JAPAN 175, "STROOG Headquarters" · MOUNT FUJI ARCHITECTS STUDIO

Design concept: don't draw an outline first — set a rule first. The building is in Toyama Prefecture, Japan: 905.04 sqm site, 494.02 sqm building area, 499.64 sqm total floor area, by MOUNT FUJI ARCHITECTS STUDIO, with structural engineering by KMC (Ken Kamachi) and photography by Ryota Atarashi. The architects' starting point was not a volume but a question: can you build an architectural system with no predetermined outline, one that stays open with respect to both space and time? That ambition is easy to leave at the level of a manifesto — except that the client here is STROOG, a manufacturer of structural timber connectors whose product line covers pinned, rigid, truss and hybrid joints. This building is simultaneously an office and a specification sheet. The joints have to actually work.

Structure and material: collapsing column and beam into one component. The move is to reduce all primary members to a single component family: CLT (cross-laminated timber) panels, uniformly 3,000 mm wide and 210 mm thick, varying in length from 4,500 to 12,000 mm. Slots are cut wherever members intersect, and the panels are assembled into a lattice by knock-down construction. What makes this work is a basic property of CLT: it is built from layers of boards laid at right angles and glued together, so it has strong axes in two perpendicular directions — the same panel stands as a column carrying vertical load and lies as a beam resisting horizontal force. Because it is solid laminated timber, slots can be cut at virtually any position to the nearest millimetre, and the depth at which those slots engage sets the structural span of the joint. Put differently: the rigid frame is not assembled out of added metal hardware, it emerges from the single act of cutting a slot. STROOG's own technical material notes that the headquarters joints use a 45-degree chamfer to maximize connection resistance.

Why "beyond the Domino system." Le Corbusier's 1914 Domino system is one of the starting points of modern architecture because it separates structure from enclosure: columns carry, slabs float, partitions go free. But Domino also leaves a constraint behind — the column grid. Once the column positions are fixed, the freedom of the plan is bounded by that grid. This is precisely what the STROOG Headquarters tries to get around: if every panel is column and beam at once, member positions need not obey a preset module and can be decided locally, space by space. The architects describe the result as a three-dimensional system free of grid-module constraints, and liken the building to an organism capable of continued evolution — knock-down construction means, in principle, that it can later be dismantled, altered and extended. That matters in timber: nailed and glued connections are irreversible, slotted connections are not. The building has received a 2026 Architectural Institute of Japan (AIJ) Works Award.

Limits and context: what a 500-square-meter building can prove. The scale needs stating plainly: 499.64 sqm total floor area is a small office building, not a tower. At this size, loads, fire compartmentation and connection forces in a CLT lattice are far more forgiving than in mid- or large-scale timber construction, and no public source addresses whether the system scales — this briefing will not speculate. What does transfer is the method: covering an entire structural system with one component type, one machining operation (cutting a slot) and one assembly logic (slotting together), shifting complexity from the variety of components to the position of the cut — which is exactly what CNC fabrication is good at. For students at Syracuse Architecture, it reads well against the timber projects covered here earlier: Herzog & de Meuron adding two storeys of Canadian black spruce onto the frame of a Brutalist teaching building takes the route of substituting the material, while the STROOG Headquarters takes the route of rewriting the joint. The first cuts embodied carbon; the second changes where the freedom of the plan comes from. Japan's recent policy push toward mid- and large-scale timber construction also gives a manufacturer-built demonstration like this significance beyond the single building.

Redefining the housing crisis as a problem of excess: the Luxembourg Pavilion argues it in Venice in 2027 through Europe's wealthiest country and Vietnam
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (Sept 30) · e-flux Announcements (curatorial statement) · Kultur | lx – Arts Council Luxembourg Biennale page · Luxembourg Ministry of Culture (commissioner) · designboom (Nov 26, 2025, chief curators announced) · La Biennale di Venezia official calendar

The premise: not "too little," but "too much." The Luxembourg national pavilion has announced its participation in the 20th Venice International Architecture Exhibition under the title "Excess! On the Overconsumption of Living Space." The framing is itself a position. Public debate about the housing crisis runs overwhelmingly on a narrative of shortage — insufficient supply, too few starts, permitting too slow. This proposal turns the camera 180 degrees. It addresses the inequitable distribution of living space between people, and treats that inequity as a reflection of "broader habits of excess that contribute to the current housing and ecological crises." In other words: the problem is not only that some people live in too little, but that others occupy too much — and the second is simultaneously an ecological problem, because every square meter corresponds to material, heating and cooling, and land.

The curators: a housing researcher plus a Hanoi-based critical spatial practice. The three curators are Céline Zimmer, a Luxembourgish housing researcher and activist who founded Eist Neit Wunnen Agence in 2026 to promote cooperative housing models; and Bùi Quý Sơn and Paul-Antoine Lucas, who co-founded Exutoire in Hanoi in 2019 — a critical spatial practice working on housing and social justice through design, and one of the winners of ArchDaily's 2026 Next Practices Awards. ArchDaily reports that the team's perspectives are rooted in housing systems, social justice design and queer architectural theory. That combination tells you something about method: this is unlikely to be an exhibition of models and drawings, and much more likely to be an argument built from data, institutions and lived scenarios.

Why Luxembourg and Vietnam. The pairing is not arbitrary. Luxembourg is among the wealthiest countries in Europe per capita, with a correspondingly high per-capita housing floor area; Vietnam is one of Southeast Asia's fastest-growing economies, with housing forms and consumption levels changing rapidly. Setting them side by side strips the question of how much space is enough of its apparent self-evidence — the same floor-area figure means entirely different things across two income levels, two climates, two household structures and two land systems. That, for architecture students, is the most instructive thing about the proposal: it declines to treat the housing crisis as a design brief (smaller units, leaner construction) and instead puts the measure itself under examination.
