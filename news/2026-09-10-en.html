URBANISM
UN General Assembly adopts Correct the Map resolution 164 to 1, recommending equal-area projections and naming Equal Earth
Sources: UN News and meetings coverage · ArchDaily · CNN · The Washington Post · NPR
The UN General Assembly has adopted the Correct the Map resolution by a recorded vote of 164 in favour, one against (the United States), with six abstentions (Estonia, Georgia, Lithuania, Moldova, Serbia and Ukraine). The resolution calls for wider use of equal-area projections in contexts where the comparative size of continents carries substantive meaning, and names the Equal Earth projection specifically. It does not ban the Mercator projection, nor does it set a single global standard; instead it encourages choosing a projection according to "regional context." One clarification matters here: equal-area projections do not make Africa bigger — they remove the exaggeration Mercator applies to high-latitude landmasses. The sponsors' argument rested on education, public understanding, culture, geopolitics and development narratives, that is, on how Africa's actual scale and potential are perceived. For architecture and planning, the practical relevance is direct: base map choice in regional-scale analysis, climate zone comparison and territorial planning coursework changes the readings you get for area, density and distance. The US deputy representative criticised the debate as a distraction from substantive work.

Sources: The Architect's Newspaper · Technical University of Munich (TUM) · Architekturmuseum der TUM · Museen in Bayern
In July 2026, Bavaria's arts minister Markus Blume announced sweeping reforms across eighteen state-run cultural institutions, one of which evicts the Architekturmuseum der TUM from the Pinakothek der Moderne. The museum has been housed there since the Pinakothek opened in 2002, in a building designed by Stephan Braunfels, and its holdings constitute one of the largest specialist architecture archives in German-speaking Europe — roughly 600,000 drawings, 200,000 photographs and 1,500 models, with material from more than 700 architects and landscape architects. An open letter opposing the move makes three arguments: that the built environment is "starkly underrepresented" in German museums; that the public has few institutions for open engagement with architecture; and that removing the museum will "jeopardize" both its reputation and the state's capacity to address pressing contemporary issues such as global warming and the housing crisis. As of publication, neither a new venue nor an archival storage plan has been announced — and for a collection built on paper drawings and physical models, climate-controlled storage and reading-room access are not incidental terms of a move but the precondition for the material remaining usable at all.

How a line 0.08 millimetres wide becomes a building: Richter's hexagonal Doha pavilion, and the part of the work Selldorf had to land
Cross-checked: designboom · The Art Newspaper · Qatar Museums · dpa / SN.at · Aachener Zeitung · Selldorf Architects

The Doha Pavilion, commissioned by Qatar Museums, opens on 20 November 2026 in MIA Park beside I. M. Pei's Museum of Islamic Art. It was conceived by the 94-year-old German artist Gerhard Richter himself, with Selldorf Architects as architect of record, and has taken roughly ten years. It is an unusual kind of commission: not an artist making an installation for an existing building, but a permanent structure intended to be read simultaneously as artwork and as architecture.

Concept: pushing a two-dimensional subdivision into three dimensions.To read the pavilion you first have to read its source. In 2010 Richter applied a digital procedure to one of his own abstract paintings: repeated halving along the vertical axis, ending in 8,192 strips each 0.08 millimetres wide — a dimension already below the resolving limit of the eye, so the result registers as continuous optical gradient rather than as countable bands. The work was collected in the 2011 book Patterns and developed into his late Strip series. The pavilion's proposition is to carry that operation from canvas to enclosure: six exterior walls carry printed patterns, three large-scale Strip paintings line the interior walls, two floor-to-ceiling mirrors and a freestanding sculpture called Strip Tower stand within, and the sculpture Brunnen (Well) sits outside. Architecture here is not a container for painting but an extension of the same logic of subdivision into volume.

Spatial strategy: the hexagon and the two mirrors together abolish the front.The hexagon is not a formal preference. Every side of a hexagonal plan is equivalent; there is no primary or secondary elevation, and approach can come from any of six directions — which corresponds exactly to the Strip series' own property of having no compositional centre, no strip more important than any other. The two floor-to-ceiling mirrors inside push this further by abolishing the endpoint: they fold the three Strip paintings back on themselves, visually extending a finite hexagonal room into something unbounded. In museum display this kind of move is often dismissed as a gimmick, but here it does conceptual work — Richter's recursive subdivision could mathematically continue indefinitely, and it is only the size of the canvas that stops it. The mirrors are how that "could have continued" gets stated in space.

Structure and material: turning 0.08 millimetres into a buildable drawing.Selldorf Architects' role is reported as architect of record, a position whose actual workload is routinely underestimated. The difficulty sits at two ends. The first is landing the printed pattern on the exterior: strips at the 0.08-millimetre scale are achievable in painting through pigment and print resolution, but on a building envelope they must contend with panel jointing, UV fading, Doha's heat and humidity, and dust deposition — there is a direct conflict between the resolution of the pattern and the weathering life of the cladding, and any joint interrupts a gradient meant to be continuous. The second is detailing the hexagonal plan: six 120-degree wall junctions, pattern registration around each corner, and the placement of entry, services and egress on a volume with no declared front. Technical requirements for a permanent pavilion are an order of magnitude beyond those for a temporary structure, which is part of the explanation for the ten-year timeline.

Background and impact: another model for Gulf cultural investment.The pavilion forms part of Rubaiya, Qatar's quadrennial opening in November across Doha with more than 50 artists and over 20 new commissions. Set in regional context: two decades of Gulf cultural construction have largely followed the route of importing an international museum brand and pairing it with a star architect — Louvre Abu Dhabi, Guggenheim Abu Dhabi. This instead builds a permanent structure for a single body of work by a single living artist, with the artist defining the form. It is closer to the American tradition of the single-artist place — the Rothko Chapel, Dia:Beacon — than to the comprehensive art museum. For architecture students, the case poses one concrete question: when the author of the form is not the architect, where does the architect's professional contribution sit? The answer is in the detailing, weathering and jointing described above — precisely the part student projects most often skip.

Amanoo, Yerevan: 11 hectares, 50 families per hectare, more than 75% green — and the landscape goes in before the buildings
Sources: ArchDaily · Khakhamyan Heritage · Soghomonyan Architects
The Amanoo residential district in Yerevan, Armenia, by Khakhamyan Heritage and Soghomonyan Architects, occupies 11 hectares and deliberately caps development intensity at 50 families per hectare, leaving more than 75% of the territory as green space. The genuinely unusual move is the construction sequence: landscape works are completed before building construction rather than planted after the buildings are topped out. The inversion has real consequences — trees planted first can already provide shade when residents move in, and the approach avoids heavy machinery destroying topsoil and existing vegetation during construction. The cost is that the landscape must be protected through a long construction period. For housing coursework in which high density is the default premise, this is a useful counter-reference.

250 feet on a sightline: Washington's triumphal arch breaks ground within weeks, and the site it will occupy is itself a piece of design
Cross-checked: The Architect's Newspaper · CBS News · CNN · WJLA · National Capital Planning Commission public testimony · The Hill / AP · PBS NewsHour

On 3 September 2026, US Interior Secretary Doug Burgum announced that the Triumphal Arch pushed by President Trump would begin excavation within weeks, on Memorial Circle on Columbia Island in Arlington, Virginia — the western end of the Arlington Memorial Bridge. The structure stands 250 feet (about 76 metres) from base to the top of its statuary, designed by Nicolas Charbonneau of Harrison Design, at an estimated cost of around $400 million, which Trump has said will come from a mix of private donors and taxpayer money. The controversy this has generated among architects and planners does not map neatly onto political alignment — its technical core is the site.

Concept and site: that circle is not vacant land.Memorial Circle is currently empty, but its emptiness is designed. The Arlington Memorial Bridge, completed in 1932, was planned precisely to connect the Lincoln Memorial with Arlington National Cemetery (and Arlington House within it) along a straight sightline, symbolising post-Civil War reconciliation. That axis is an inherited piece of urban design descending from the McMillan Plan of 1902, and the circle's vacancy is the condition on which it depends. Placing a 250-foot structure there physically interrupts that line of sight. This can be stated independently of any aesthetic judgement: whatever the form of the object, a solid mass at the midpoint of an axis ends mutual visibility between its two ends.

Spatial strategy and traffic: the circle's functional problem.Memorial Circle is also a high-volume traffic circle, taking turning movements from the George Washington Memorial Parkway and the Memorial Bridge. A second line of objection concentrates here: building a large structure at the centre of a traffic circle means prolonged construction hoarding and a permanent change to vehicle circulation, and once visitors are drawn to the centre of the circle, a pedestrian-vehicle conflict is created that did not previously exist — the circle has no safe pedestrian access. When the National Capital Planning Commission granted preliminary approval in July 2026, it explicitly requested that the Interior Department supply further material on the structure's potential impact on air navigation and on specifics of construction and area traffic. These are procedural requirements, and no final public response has been recorded.

Form and criticism: attribute the judgements.On questions of form this briefing relays only attributed professional criticism. Writing in The Architect's Newspaper in June 2026, critic Naomi Leigh argued that the scheme gets classicism wrong, calling it "a simulacrum of the classical" and writing that "the arch is oversized and visually top-heavy, its proportions strained." According to Fox News, the sculptural programme includes golden eagles, lions and a winged Lady Liberty at the summit. As a disciplinary matter, the triumphal arch as a typology is not in itself the issue — the Arc de Triomphe and the Washington Square Arch are established precedents. The argument is about the proportional system, the height-to-width relationship of plinth and archivolt, and the visual distribution of sculptural load: technical questions that can be checked against classical orders and proportional canons, not matters of taste.

Process and impact: the approval chain and unresolved litigation.On approvals: the US Commission of Fine Arts, whose members are appointed by Trump, granted final approval in May 2026, and the NCPC granted preliminary approval in July with the additional requirements noted above. The project has not received Congressional approval. On litigation: in February 2026 three Vietnam War veterans and architectural historian Calder Loth sued to block construction, describing the proposal as a "vanity project" and arguing among other points that it bypasses Congressional authorisation; the case is ongoing, and opponents sought emergency judicial relief around 3 September. For architecture and planning students, this case is worth following in full not because of whether it is ultimately built but because it exposes a set of normally invisible institutions: who protects inherited urban design, how approval authority over federal land is allocated, and how much constraint procedural review retains when the reviewing body's members are appointed by the project's proponent. These questions recur, in different forms, in major public projects in any country.

What equal-area projection means in the studio: change the base map and your area, density and distance readings all move
Sources: UN meetings coverage (GA/12779) · ArchDaily · Equal Earth projection literature
The UN's Correct the Map resolution recommends rather than mandates: it calls for equal-area projections where the comparative size of continents carries substantive meaning and names Equal Earth, but does not ban Mercator and does not set a single global standard, instead leaving the choice to "regional context." The operational implication for urban and regional research is direct. Mercator is a conformal projection: it preserves angles, not areas, and its exaggeration grows with latitude — which is also precisely why it remains the default for web mapping, since local shape is correct and zoom behaves consistently. But the moment your object of analysis is area (national land mass, climate-zone extent, green space per capita) or density (people per square kilometre), readings taken off a Mercator base are systematically wrong. For students working at regional scale, comparing climate zones, or on territorial planning briefs, the practical advice is single: separate area calculation from shape reading, use an appropriate projection for each, and stop using one base map for both.
Las Vegas Stadium Authority approves $75 million — for the plaza outside the venue, not the venue itself

In early September 2026 the Las Vegas Stadium Authority voted unanimously to release $75 million from an existing fund toward the Raiders' $158 million north entry plaza at Allegiant Stadium. Landscape before buildings: Yerevan's Amanoo inverts the construction sequence, trading 50 families per hectare for 75% green space
Sources: ArchDaily · Khakhamyan Heritage · Soghomonyan Architects
Amanoo sets development intensity at 50 families per hectare across an 11-hectare site, leaves more than 75% of the territory as green space, and completes landscape works ahead of building construction. That order is rare in housing development because it turns landscape into a sunk up-front cost and requires protecting mature planting through the whole construction period. The designers' logic is verifiable: trees planted first can provide shade at handover, whereas the conventional post-construction approach takes five to ten years to reach comparable canopy. The land economics of low density are unambiguous, and the reference value here is not the intensity figure itself but that the scheme treats "when does the green space exist" as a design variable rather than a line item at the end of construction.

Affordable housing is the real exam for industrialised timber: Stefano Boeri's 190 dry-assembled CLT rentals in Ibiza
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · designboom · Stefano Boeri Architetti · Pambianco · Entidad Estatal de Vivienda / Casa 47

Stefano Boeri Architetti has won Casa 47, the competition run by Spain's Entidad Estatal de Vivienda, with Sa Casa: roughly 190 below-market-rate rental apartments in Ca n'Escandell, a public housing district on the edge of Ibiza Town. The parcel is 5,505 square metres, and the wider neighbourhood is planned to hold more than 500 protected homes. The scheme was chosen from 13 entries. The weight of this story is not the name of the practice but the brief itself — affordable housing is the building type with the hardest cost constraints, and whether industrialised timber construction works here matters far more than whether it works in a cultural building or a high-end house.

Concept: split 190 homes into four blocks and let the landscape pass through.The scheme does not consolidate into one mass but distributes into four separated residential volumes. The jury's stated reason for selecting it was exactly how that separation negotiates the terrain, and how it consequently opens the housing toward the shared landscape between the blocks. This has a specific meaning for affordable housing: a single large volume is normally cheaper (better perimeter-to-area ratio, more concentrated vertical circulation), and splitting into four increases external wall area and the number of cores. The scheme is willing to pay that penalty in exchange for every apartment facing a shared green space of real dimension rather than the corridor of the next block. A central green area, courtyards, pathways, shared workspaces and communal areas are all built from the same modular system as the housing.

Spatial strategy: modular does not have to mean fixed unit plans.The scheme pairs the prefabricated timber system with adaptable housing layouts. This is a genuine problem in prefabricated timber housing: CLT panels are load-bearing, so once wall positions are set they are hard to move, and modularity's usual price is unit plans too rigid to absorb changes in household structure. The response here is to separate the structural module from the partition system, so one structural grid accommodates different unit combinations — and because the shared facilities (workspaces, communal areas) use the same modular system, residential and communal units are structurally interchangeable. For social housing with a design life of several decades, that adaptability matters more than the initial unit mix.

Structure and material: what dry assembly in CLT actually buys.The structural system is cross-laminated timber (CLT) — structural panels glued up from layers of wood set perpendicular to one another, serving as both load-bearing wall and floor plate. Components are prefabricated off site and dry-assembled on site with no wet trades. The process buys three things: compressed programme, reduced material waste, and less disruption to the surroundings. The third is unusually concrete in Ibiza, where a tourism economy strictly limits the construction window; shortening on-site works raises project feasibility by a whole notch. Climate strategy layers over the structure: native planting extends from the open spaces onto the roofs for shade, habitat, air quality and urban heat island mitigation, with photovoltaics on the roofs and building orientation, natural ventilation and water management folded into the design. One caution: combining green roofs with CLT roof plates imposes extra requirements on waterproofing and moisture control. Timber's greatest long-term risk is getting wet, and roof loading and waterproofing build-up are the details this kind of scheme most needs watched through construction documentation.

Background and impact: why this brief is worth following.Europe has accumulated substantial experience with timber high-rises and timber public buildings, but affordable housing is still mostly concrete. The reason is straightforward: CLT costs more per unit of material than concrete, so its cost advantage has to be recovered on the overall account through programme and labour savings — which requires a stable prefabrication supply chain and a skilled assembly workforce, precisely the two things affordable housing projects find hardest to guarantee. Sa Casa was selected through a competition run by a national housing agency, which means the public sector is willing to carry the cost of trial and error on this path. For students, the transferable point is to move attention from "is timber low-carbon" to "is timber deliverable under cost constraint": the first was settled some time ago, the second is the actual battleground of the next decade. Stefano Boeri's practice is best known for Bosco Verticale, and it is worth noticing that here the planted roofs are visibly reined in to a climate function rather than a formal argument.

ICON finishes 10 3D-printed barracks at Fort Bliss: 58 days of printing, nine months contract to delivery, $62.8 million
Sources: U.S. Army · DVIDS · Stars and Stripes · ICON · 3D Printing Industry
Austin-based 3D printing construction company ICON received a $62.8 million production contract from the U.S. Army in December 2025 to build ten 3D-printed barracks at Fort Bliss in west Texas. The embodied carbon paradox in CLT floors: the 1–3 inches of concrete topping needed for acoustics eats back part of the saving
Sources: ArchDaily · USG · Baker's Place, Madison, Wisconsin
An ArchDaily technical feature points at a step usually skipped in the timber decarbonisation narrative: CLT floor plates are light and acoustically insufficient on their own, so meeting party-floor sound ratings (STC/IIC) typically requires pouring 1 to 3 inches of concrete topping — and producing lightweight concrete generates roughly 1,230 pounds of CO₂ per metric ton. The embodied carbon saved in the primary structure is therefore partly given back in the acoustic build-up. The alternative path is a gypsum-based self-levelling system over an acoustic mat: comparing 1 inch of gypsum concrete against a conventional 3–4 inches of lightweight concrete, the claim is a reduction of at least 75% in embodied carbon, and substituting the lightweight topping in a 100,000-square-foot building is claimed to cut nearly 50 metric tons of CO₂e. The case study is Baker's Place in Madison — a 14-storey hybrid CLT building using a 5/16-inch acoustic mat plus 2 inches of gypsum concrete, achieving measured STC/IIC of 56/53 with installation completed in two days per floor. Note that the feature's data comes from materials manufacturer USG and should be read as a manufacturer's claim.
Meta puts CLT into data centre administration buildings: around 41% less embodied carbon, and up to half the foundation concrete
Sources: Meta Sustainability and Meta Data Centers · Construction Dive · Data Centre Magazine · Datacenter Dynamics
Meta has adopted mass timber — including cross-laminated timber and glue-laminated beams — in administration buildings at data centre campuses in South Carolina, Wyoming and Alabama, recording roughly 41% lower embodied carbon than conventional materials. The saving comes from two accountable sources: the wood itself sequesters carbon and displaces high-emission steel and concrete; and mass timber structures weigh far less than steel or concrete, cutting foundation concrete requirements by up to half — an effect that is particularly pronounced on soft ground. There is a programme benefit too: mass timber components are largely prefabricated, so extensive on-site steel welding is unnecessary and completion can come weeks earlier. Data centres are among the fastest-growing building types globally, which gives construction choices in their administration and ancillary buildings significance beyond any single project — though note that Meta's application so far is limited to administration buildings, not the data halls themselves.
PBFD 2026 in Venice, 16–18 September: putting facade failure on the main agenda
Sources: Facade Today · PBFD 2026
The Performance-Based Facade Design (PBFD) 2026 conference runs in Venice from 16 to 18 September 2026, three days on facade durability, failure cases, climate change and whole-life value. Making failures a main agenda item is unusual for a facade industry conference — most comparable events lead on new products and performance figures. The framing answers a practical problem: the facade is the shortest-replacement-cycle part of a building envelope and the one whose failure has the most immediate consequences, while climate loading (extreme temperature swings, intense rainfall, wind pressure) is now changing faster than most codes are being revised. For students working in facade design or building technology, the conference programme doubles as a reading list of the current research frontier.

Moving the storeroom into the gallery: Herzog & de Meuron opens two decades of China at M+ on 12 September, showing decisions rather than finished photographs
Cross-checked: M+ Museum · ArchDaily · Dezeen Events Guide · The Standard (Hong Kong) · Galerie Magazine · Ocula

Herzog & de Meuron: In Focus opens on 12 September 2026 in the M+ Focus Gallery in Hong Kong and runs to January 2027, curated by Ikko Yokoyama and Iris Ng. The exhibition marks two things at once: M+'s fifth anniversary, and the second phase of the practice's donation to the M+ Collection. That donation comprises more than 200 process items — models, prototypes, sketches and archival material.

Concept: show how it was thought, not how it turned out.The default mode of the architecture exhibition is outcome-driven: large photographs, finished models, wall text. This exhibition takes the opposite line, with the museum explicitly setting out to reveal "the experimentation and decision-making behind each work, from early ideas to built form." Ten Chinese projects are selected, spanning 2002 to 2021, among them the National Stadium in Beijing (2002–2008, the "Bird's Nest"), Tai Kwun Centre for Heritage and Art in Hong Kong (2006–2018), and M+ itself. Exhibiting the design process of a museum inside that museum is a rare self-referential arrangement in architecture exhibitions, and it gives the show an inherent layer of institutional self-examination.

Spatial strategy: replicating the Basel open storage in Hong Kong.The core decision in the exhibition design is the use of wooden vitrines replicated from those the practice developed for the open storage of its archival holdings at the Kabinett, its Basel-based foundation established in 2015. This is not a stylistic citation but the wholesale transplant of a storage logic: open storage is characterised by the absence of narrative curation, with objects arranged in inventory order rather than exhibition order, so the visitor sees the archive as it actually is. Senior curator Ikko Yokoyama puts it as follows: "The open-storage display, inspired by the Kabinett, repositions the gallery as a space for discovery and dialogue." The strategy has a cost — it transfers part of the burden of organising information to the visitor, raising the threshold for those without a professional background — but what it buys is raw material unretouched by an exhibition narrative.

Material: process objects are technical literature.The 200-plus donated items comprise models, prototypes, sketches and archival objects. Of these, the prototypes most warrant a dedicated visit: they are physical trials of construction details, material texture and surface treatment, and evidence of repeated exchange between architect and fabricator. In the National Stadium, every step of simplification and adjustment to the woven steel form survives in the model sequence; Tai Kwun, as a heritage conversion, makes the logic of the new-to-old interface far clearer in process material than in finished photographs. For students this material is irreplaceable — completed photographs show only the final choice, while a sequence of models and prototypes shows the options that were abandoned, and it is the latter that design teaching actually needs.

Background and impact: a geographical relocation of a practice's archive.Herzog & de Meuron engaged deeply with China from the early 2000s, coinciding with the country's period of rapid economic growth and large-scale infrastructure construction. Donating two decades of process archives to M+ in Hong Kong rather than keeping them in Basel is a substantive geographical shift: the primary material for researching these projects will henceforth sit mainly in Asia rather than Europe. That is consistent with M+'s institutional strategy for building a design and architecture collection — for a five-year-old museum, acquiring the primary archives of significant local projects establishes academic authority more effectively than acquiring finished works. For students at Syracuse Architecture with plans to be in Hong Kong in late 2026, this show is worth scheduling; it runs until January 2027, so it is still open over winter break.

Entries for the Architecture Drawing Prize 2026 close on 11 September. USTA Future Courts student competition: 70 entries from 45 schools of architecture, winner announced during the US Open on 31 August
Sources: The Architect's Newspaper · USTA competition rules · Weitzman School of Design, University of Pennsylvania
The United States Tennis Association's Future Courts competition asked architecture students to reimagine the spaces around tennis courts across the country — note that the brief is the surround, not the court itself: court dimensions are locked by the rules, and what remains designable is seating, shade, changing facilities and the community interface, the parts that turn a hard surface into a public place. The competition drew 70 submissions from students at 45 schools of architecture, and the winning concept was announced during the 2026 US Open on 31 August. Master of Architecture students Ann Teng Chua, Teng Long "Tommy" Lee, Liam Niland and Kotaro Shiraishi of Penn's Weitzman School were named finalists. Among the published finalist schemes, Flying Cloud Tennis Center — by students from Harvard and Princeton — reimagines a historic shipbuilding pier in East Boston as a year-round tennis destination; legacy industrial waterfront plus low-threshold sports facility is a pairing that has been recurring frequently in American public space briefs.
