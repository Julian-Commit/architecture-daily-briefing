The Lucas Museum of Narrative Art is finished and opens in Los Angeles on 22 September: Ma Yansong lifts nearly 27,900 square metres clear of the ground using 1,271 individually shaped fibreglass-reinforced polymer panels and a single 56-metre steel arch, trading an asphalt parking lot for a 13-acre park. Sources: designboom · Board of Forestry and Fire Protection · CAL FIRE · California Wildfire & Forest Resilience Task Force · Mork-Ulnes Architects

A museum with no straight lines: how Ma Yansong used 1,271 FRP panels and one 56-metre arch to float 27,900 square metres over Exposition Park
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · designboom · Lucas Museum press release · Arquitectura Viva · Artforum · Parametric Architecture

The Lucas Museum of Narrative Art is complete and opens on 22 September 2026. The institution was co-founded by George Lucas and Mellody Hobson; Tracey Bates is CEO. Ma Yansong's MAD Architects led the design, Stantec served as executive architect, Mia Lehrer's Studio-MLA designed the landscape, and the combined design and construction team exceeded 150 people. The building covers 300,000 square feet (about 27,900 sq m) on a 13-acre (5.3-hectare) campus in Los Angeles's Exposition Park. Construction began in 2018; the museum was originally slated to open in 2025.

Design concept: remove the straight line, and the viewing sequence loses its starting point.The official description says the building contains "not a single straight line" — five floors of continuously curved volume. This is not only a formal preference. Exposition Park already holds the California Science Center, the Natural History Museum and the Los Angeles Memorial Coliseum, all heavy volumes with emphatic frontality. MAD went the other way: no principal facade, no monumental flight of entry steps, the entire volume lifted clear so the ground plane returns to the park. Ma calls the vocabulary "emotional architecture." For a museum devoted to storytelling through images, dispensing with straight lines also dispenses with a single authorised route through the collection — consistent with a subject that has no canonical entrance.

Spatial strategy: the ground floor goes to the city, the galleries move up to levels four and five.The distribution across five public floors is unusual. Lower levels hold two 299-seat theaters — the Robert Flaherty Theater for documentary work and the Slavko Vorkapich Theater for experimental and technical cinema — plus the horseshoe-shaped research library, ten studios and classrooms, retail, a café and a restaurant, with roughly 930 sq m of ground-floor gallery. The main display space is concentrated on level four (about 82,000 sq ft / 7,600 sq m) and level five (nearly 8,000 sq ft / 750 sq m), together exceeding 100,000 sq ft (9,300 sq m) across more than 30 galleries. Those galleries are organised thematically rather than chronologically or by medium: love, family, community, fantasy, romance, adventure, alongside comics, manga, graphic novels, photography and murals. The research library is free to all visitors and requires no ticket. The opening display holds more than 1,300 works, including "The History of Narrative Art: Heroes • Myths • The Sacred" on the first floor and "Star Wars in Motion," with vehicles, props and costumes.

Structure and material innovation: one 56-metre arch buys a shaded plaza.What makes the lift possible is a central steel arch spanning 185 feet (56 m). It channels the load of the upper floors into a small number of bearing points and clears a sheltered elevated plaza beneath the building — under Los Angeles sun, that is a climate strategy as much as a structural outcome. The envelope consists of 1,271 uniquely shaped fibreglass-reinforced polymer (FRP) panels averaging roughly 8 × 32 feet. Two reasons argue for FRP over metal panel or glass fibre-reinforced concrete as the primary skin: the moulding freedom needed for doubly curved geometry, and self-weight — the areal density of an FRP panel is far below that of concrete or stone cladding at the same size, and on a volume with this much cantilever and span, weight saved is steel saved in the primary structure. The rest of the building uses concrete, steel, glass, plaster and glass fibre-reinforced concrete (GFRC); cylindrical glass elevator shafts on the north side make vertical circulation legible from outside, while the main lobby is built from sweeping arches and large glazed openings.

Context and consequence: the arithmetic of converting an asphalt lot.The campus was previously an asphalt parking lot. Studio-MLA converted it into roughly 52,600 sq m of park with more than 300 newly planted trees (jacarandas, oaks, pines) and extensive indigenous drought-tolerant planting, adding a hanging garden, an amphitheater, a meadow, a waterfall feature and ten monumental outdoor sculptures. The significance is not simply green area: Exposition Park is one of the few free public cultural gathering grounds in Los Angeles, and making the museum's ground level and its entire landscape accessible without a ticket effectively cedes part of a private collection's site to the city as public space. The other side of the ledger deserves student attention too — eight years from groundbreaking in 2018 to opening in 2026, a year later than originally planned, and 1,271 non-repeating doubly curved panels that demand digital control of geometry, moulds and installation tolerance through the entire process. The cost of a free-form surface never shows up in the rendering; it shows up in numbers like these.

Moreau Kusunoki is lead designer, Genton is local architect and Arup is engineer. The defining move is the external steel exoskeleton — repeating diagonal members forming a dense lattice that carries structural load while acting as a shading layer, a direct response to intense western exposure in Sydney light. At A$915 million it is the largest museum ever built in New South Wales, and it is designed to run at net-zero emissions from opening day. Boomerang Athens: Greece's first LEED Platinum single-family home earns it on envelope and KNX control
Sources: designboom · ECLÉDE · German Design Award 2026
Boomerang Athens, sited between Lycabettus Hill and Kolonaki, is the first single-family residence in Greece certified LEED Platinum under LEED v4.1 BD+C. ECLÉDE, directed by founder Vassilis Siafaricas, designed the 425-square-metre house with four en-suite bedrooms, a private elevator, panoramic terraces and a rooftop pool. The technical case rests on two systems: a high-performance thermal envelope with heat pumps, underfloor heating and smart ventilation; and KNX automation managing lighting, solar shading and microclimate, with ground-level solar panels generating electricity. A rhythmic vertical screen on the facade controls both solar exposure and privacy, and openings are triple-glazed. Materials run to smooth marble, raw stone, warm natural timber and bespoke joinery, with low-emission specification and acoustic dampening throughout. The project also received a German Design Award 2026 for Excellent Architecture.

Parking lots, old boatyards, a demolished freeway: the five AIA 2026 Regional & Urban Design Award winners are all telling the same story
Cross-checked: AIA · Archinect / Bustler · Machado Silvetti · OJB Landscape Architecture · Agency Landscape + Planning · Monocle · World Landscape Architect

The AIA 2026 Regional & Urban Design Award went to five projects, four in the United States and one in Mexico. What separates this award from the AIA Architecture Awards is its unit of assessment: it judges not a single building but how well a scheme reconciles the entire built environment, local culture and available resources — which is why the winners tend to be public works measured in decades. Line the five up and their starting conditions turn out to be nearly identical.

Design concept: start by asking what these five sites used to be.Waterfront Park in Seattle (James Corner Field Operations) sits on the corridor of the demolished Alaskan Way Viaduct and the rebuilt Elliott Bay seawall. India Basin Waterfront Park in San Francisco (Jensen Architects) is a contaminated post-industrial boatyard brownfield. The Bay: "One Park for All" in Sarasota converts 53 acres of city-owned bayfront from a condition that was mostly parking lot, its master plan co-led by Agency Landscape + Planning and Sasaki in 2018. Downtown Cary Park in North Carolina (OJB Landscape Architecture with Machado Silvetti) works a seven-acre site in an existing town centre. The Monterrey Tec Innovation Ecosystem addresses the rupture between a university campus and the city around it. None of the five is a new park on new land; every one is a recovery of existing infrastructure, parking or contaminated ground. That is the actual working surface of contemporary urban design.

Spatial strategy: treat connection as the primary function, not the park as a destination.The Seattle scheme runs 1.5 miles across 26 blocks, linking Pioneer Square to Belltown, and uses the Overlook Walk — an elevated route — to join Pike Place Market, the Seattle Aquarium and Pier 62 into one continuous pedestrian journey. The park's job here is to suture, not to supply lawn. India Basin works through winding trails and habitat restoration, with the emphasis on equitable shore access. Cary inverts the logic, using sculptural pavilions and immersive gardens to turn a historic town core into a civic landscape with year-round programming; what it connects is not two districts but a rapidly growing community and its own historic centre. Monterrey Tec links campus, industry and community through mixed-use buildings and public space, in a scheme the AIA jury credits with establishing "a new model for the urban university."

Structural and material innovation: resilience is a question of level, soil and tide line.The technical content of this work is not span but vertical datum and soil. India Basin turns on wetland restoration, remediation of contaminated land, and resilience designed against sea level rise scenarios. Sarasota includes a mangrove walk, a living shoreline, a performance lawn and ecological restoration. Seattle rebuilds the ground plane after removing an elevated freeway and reconstructing a seawall, replacing aging infrastructure with ecological restoration, multimodal access and civic space. All three waterfront projects share one judgement: the lifespan of a waterfront park is not determined by its paving but by whether it can absorb decades of change in water level. That shifts the starting point of vertical design — elevation stops being a drainage problem and becomes a scenario-planning problem.

Context and consequence: cost and time scale.The Seattle scheme cost roughly $800 million, delivered by the City of Seattle with Field Operations alongside Jacobs Engineering, WSP and HNTB. Downtown Cary Park cut its ribbon in December 2023 to more than 35,000 visitors and has since collected more than a dozen design awards. For students, the transferable lesson is not formal language but two structural facts. First, value in post-industrial waterfronts is almost always unlocked by removing a piece of transport infrastructure; the park is downstream of the demolition, and the decisive choices are often made in transport and civil engineering rather than in design. Second, community engagement in this kind of project is not procedural decoration — the Sarasota master plan was explicitly guided by a set of community-driven principles, and "one park for all" was a design premise rather than a slogan applied afterwards.

SB 9 duplexes and lot splits barred in the Palisades burn zone: one fire, two jurisdictions, opposite answers
Sources: LAist · CBS Los Angeles · Reason · Legal Planet
In August 2025, Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass — after obtaining authorisation via an executive order from Governor Gavin Newsom — signed an order suspending intake of SB 9 duplex and lot-split applications inside the Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone within the Palisades Fire area, requiring destroyed single-family homes to be rebuilt as single-family residences. Bass's stated reason was evacuation capacity: developers using SB 9 to convert recently destroyed single-family lots into multiple residences "could drastically further challenge ingress and egress" in a VHFHSZ and would strain local infrastructure. The restriction was not applied to Altadena, hit in the same fire season by the Eaton Fire. Housing advocates and some planning academics criticised the move, with the dispute turning on whether post-fire rebuilding should prioritise evacuation safety or housing supply. This restriction is the policy backdrop to this issue's materials feature.
China's Urban Renewal 15th Five-Year Plan takes effect: RMB 97 billion in central budget funds, 50 pilot cities first
Sources: Xinhua · Beijing Daily · State Council
The full text of the State Council's Urban Renewal "15th Five-Year" Plan was published in May 2026, with four ministries issuing a joint interpretation in June. On funding: RMB 97 billion of central budget allocation is earmarked in 2026 for urban renewal, directed at old residential compounds and dilapidated housing and reaching roughly 8 million households; a further RMB 160 billion in ultra-long-term special bonds goes to underground utility networks, covering gas, water supply, drainage and heating. On implementation: 50 key cities including Beijing, Shanghai and Shenyang will act first under their own action plans, testing mechanisms intended to be replicable at scale, while local legislation advances in provinces including Shanxi and Tianjin — urban renewal is shifting from a special campaign to normalised, law-based practice. The plan's framing moves from single-function renovation to four parallel dimensions: livelihood, cultural continuity, ecology and industry. For architecture and planning students in mainland China, this is the dominant source of briefs for the next five years.
An abandoned canal dock in Hackney: a community centre proposed as climbable terrain
Sources: designboom · Zhi Qian Jacqueline Yu
designboom published The Playscape Garden on 9 September, a proposal for an abandoned canal dock site at Kingsland Basin in Hackney, London, by architect Zhi Qian Jacqueline Yu with creative director Fanqi Yang. The scheme replaces the standard organisation of a community cultural centre outright: instead of a static institutional building of fixed rooms, the architecture becomes one continuous occupiable terrain — climbing structures, rope bridges, courtyards, gardens, café and library space layered together. As the design statement puts it, "architecture becomes a dynamic terrain: a place to climb, explore, gather, rest, and learn." Materials are dark rock, pink terrazzo, polished concrete, structural glass, lightweight steel and timber. The project is a concept proposal and has not been built.
A Roosevelt Island cultural plaza proposal: ground becomes roof, roof becomes public space
Sources: designboom · Zehua Zhang
New York-licensed architect Zehua Zhang published "Architecture as Inhabitable Landscape: Roosevelt Island Cultural Plaza" on 8 September, sited at the southern tip of Roosevelt Island facing the East River between Manhattan and Queens. The central move is a continuous green roof that extends the surrounding parkland across the building, turning the roof into occupiable public terrain. The architect's formulation: "ground becomes roof, roof becomes public space, and the building becomes terrain." The programme includes a cultural and community centre, exhibition galleries, a multimedia performance hall, cafés and restaurants, and an interior public space described as a civic living room; a central atrium with floor-to-ceiling glazing connects the interior programme directly to the East River landscape. This is a speculative proposal, not a built or commissioned project.

The tectonics of living with wildfire: sacrificial layers, fire blankets and the five-foot rule are rewriting the California wall
Cross-checked: designboom · Board of Forestry and Fire Protection · CAL FIRE · California Wildfire & Forest Resilience Task Force · Mork-Ulnes Architects · Faulkner Architects

On 19 August 2026, California's Board of Forestry and Fire Protection approved the first Zone 0 defensible space regulations in the nation; the Wildland-Urban Interface Code covering the city–wildland edge had already taken effect in January 2026. On 8 September, designboom surveyed how five California practices are building under this regulatory regime. Read the code text and the built work together and a technical consensus comes into focus: fire resistance is decided less by the wall than by the openings and the junctions.

Design concept: start by admitting how these houses catch fire.Roughly 90 per cent of wildfire home ignitions are caused by embers rather than by flame touching the wall. That single fact reorders the priorities — "what can an ember get into" matters more than "what the wall is made of." So the shared characteristic of this work is geometric subtraction: simplified roof edges, elimination of complex eaves and re-entrant corners, smaller openings, and every opening shielded. A 2024 house in Pacific Palisades by Chansen Architecture survived the 2025 Palisades Fire, one of the few empirical tests of this logic.

Spatial strategy: draw the five feet as part of the building boundary.Zone 0 is the ember-resistant zone within five feet (about 1.5 m) of a structure. The rule requires removal of all combustible ground cover in that band — bark mulch, wood chips, dead vegetation — and replacement of existing wood and vinyl fencing with non-combustible materials. Foundation and attic vents are the main ember entry point; mesh with openings no larger than 1/16 inch is strongly recommended, with the local authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) deciding whether it is mandatory. The design consequence is underrated: the rule converts a planting setback into a hard line of building control. Courtyard design can no longer plant against a wall, and paving or mineral material must form a continuous ring around the structure — the interface between landscape and building has been redrawn by code. The final draft has passed but awaits Office of Administrative Law (OAL) review before becoming enforceable.

Structural and material innovation: three characteristic approaches.The first is the sacrificial layer. Mork-Ulnes Architects built Frame House in Sonoma (2022) with concrete as the structural body and western red cedar over it; architect Casper Mork-Ulnes calls the cladding "a sacrificial layer of wood" outright — the timber finish is permitted to burn away while the concrete behind it stays intact. The second is the fire blanket. Deegan-Day Design & Architecture used steel trusses with a zinc roof at 4/Way House in Topanga (2018), making the roof a continuous non-combustible cover. The third is the wholly non-combustible shell. Faulkner Architects' CAMPout (Lake Tahoe, 2023) and Pine Flat Residence (Healdsburg, 2025), and Edmonds + Lee Architects' Amnesia House (Napa Valley, 2025), use doubled concrete walls with steel-sash tempered glazing, or Corten steel shells, with timber appearing only inside. Beyond these three, the common specification includes metal roofing in place of asphalt shingle, ember-resistant vents and eave closures, sliding metal screens that cover openings, exterior deck sprinklers, and vegetation set back from the facade.

Context and consequence: the urban politics beyond the detail.The technical path for a fire-hardened house is now reasonably clear, but it sits inside a far more contested land policy. In August 2025, after obtaining authorisation from Governor Gavin Newsom, Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass signed an executive order suspending intake of SB 9 duplex and lot-split applications inside the Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone of the Palisades burn area, arguing that multi-unit conversion would worsen evacuation pressure; the same policy was not applied in Altadena. In other words, after one fire, the answer at the level of construction is "make the house non-combustible," while the answer at the level of planning differs by jurisdiction. For architecture students this is an unusually direct case where a wall detail and a housing policy can be argued on the same sheet — fire-hardened construction raises the survival odds of an individual building, but it cannot on its own answer at what density a burned community should be rebuilt.

Biochar concrete goes carbon-negative: net GWP of −14 kg CO₂/m³ from forestry residues and used coffee grounds
Sources: Holcim UK · ESG News · Biochar Today · Clearly Concrete
Holcim developed a net-zero concrete with London's Canary Wharf Group using biochar made from forestry residues and used coffee grounds. The custom mix carries a footprint of −14 kg CO₂ per cubic metre in net global warming potential across A1–A3, meaning the concrete itself becomes a long-term carbon store. Earlier testing of a related mix showed roughly an 80 per cent reduction in net GWP against a conventional Portland cement mix, at 69 kgCO₂e/m³. The mechanism is straightforward: carbon absorbed by plants during growth is captured in the biochar and permanently embedded in the concrete. Running on the same trend line is calcined clay, which substitutes for limestone clinker and can cut cement CO₂ by up to 50 per cent; Holcim expects to reach 1 million tonnes of calcined clay cement in 2026. The practical implication for students is that low-carbon concrete has moved from laboratory mix design to a product specification you can actually name on a drawing.
The Architect's Newspaper on glazing: multicurved tempered glass and the current edge of high-performance glass systems
Sources: The Architect's Newspaper · sedak
On 8 September The Architect's Newspaper published a survey of currently specifiable high-performance glazing, including German manufacturer sedak's multicurved tempered glass — a single sheet curved in two directions and tempered, where doubly curved surfaces previously had to be achieved by cold bending or by subdividing into flat panels. The piece connects directly to two threads in this issue: free-form envelopes no longer necessarily depend on composite panel systems (the Lucas Museum used 1,271 FRP panels), and an acute awareness of embodied carbon is reshaping facade selection — glass, metals and composite panels are all being redesigned toward lower embodied carbon, forming, together with lighter precast concrete mixes and mass timber, the three mainstream routes in facade design right now.
Zone 0 has passed final draft but is not yet law: OAL review still pending
Sources: California Wildfire & Forest Resilience Task Force · Board of Forestry and Fire Protection · Zone Zero California
A timing caveat worth stating plainly: the Zone 0 defensible space regulation approved by California's Board of Forestry and Fire Protection on 19 August 2026 has only cleared final draft, and must still pass Office of Administrative Law (OAL) review before it is enforceable — so it is not yet a mandatory compliance basis. The rule text went through multiple public workshops and draft revisions from April 2026 onward. The technical requirements already settled include: removal of all combustible ground cover — bark mulch, wood chips, dead vegetation — within five feet of a structure; replacement of existing wood and vinyl fencing within Zone 0 with non-combustible materials; and ember-resistant mesh with openings no larger than 1/16 inch on foundation and attic vents (strongly recommended, and possibly required by the local AHJ). In the meantime, CAL FIRE's home hardening guidelines remain the technical reference that can be cited in practice.

Academia & Exhibitions ACADEMIA
IN DEPTH
25 deans on the 2026–27 year: when "what should we teach" is replaced by "what world are we preparing students for"
Cross-checked: Archinect · Planetizen · Pratt Institute · Tulane School of Architecture · LSU School of Architecture

Archinect's Alexander Walter spoke to 25 deans, directors and chairs of North American architecture programmes as the 2026–27 academic year opened, with Planetizen republishing the feature in September. The value of an annual survey like this lies less in any single statement than in the distribution of agreement: the recurring terms are adaptation, interdisciplinary collaboration, emerging technologies, research, housing, climate resilience and public engagement.

Pedagogical concept: the question itself has changed.The report's central judgement is that this cohort of school leaders has moved past the conventional question of what architecture should be teaching, toward a more unsettled one — how design education can prepare students for a profession and a world in flux. That shift is worth unpacking. The first question assumes the boundaries of the profession are stable and the task is to transmit established skills; the second makes no such assumption, which turns the curriculum structure itself into an object of design. The report characterises the underlying tone as a field reconsidering its priorities and its place in society.

Curriculum and facilities: AI and mass timber enter the syllabus together.The initiatives described by the 25 respondents span two poles: artificial intelligence and advanced fabrication at one end, mass timber and community-based design at the other. That juxtaposition is itself informative — schools neither believe technology will replace design judgement nor believe they can route around it. Multiple schools cite new degree programmes, new facilities and new external partnerships: the Tulane School of Architecture and Built Environment announced its 2026 faculty cohort and new leadership across several degree programmes; students and faculty from the LSU School of Architecture presented research at the Architectural Research Centers Consortium (ARCC) conference in Atlanta on 8–11 April 2026, held under the theme "Local Solutions for Global Issues."
