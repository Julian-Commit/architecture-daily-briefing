EDITOR'S NOTE

Today's four threads all handle the same problem: what to do with what is already there. In Tokyo, Nikken Sekkei rebuilt a 1967 Togo Murano office block in the same dark granite — inheriting a way of handling material rather than a shape. In the City of London, independent inspectors concluded on 23 September that City Plan 2040 already protects the setting of the Tower of London, and the housing minister withdrew his June halt the same day; between World Heritage views and central-city density, plan-level height control won. In Lugano, the Swiss territorial biennial titles itself Handle with Care and calls demolish-and-rebuild the easy path, in those words. Harbin Institute of Technology and the Chinese Academy of Sciences go the other way: if concrete itself stores charge, existing buildings are not only structure. Museum Vonk in Eindhoven is blunter still — it took the cross-laminated timber out of a 2019 pavilion and used it for interior walls.

Sources: ArchDaily (3 October) · Daibiru Corporation project page · Daibiru completion release (30 June 2025) · Nikken Sekkei · Archinect project entry
The new building stands at 1-1-1 Kyobashi, Chuo-ku, Tokyo, facing Yaesu-dori Ave. in front of Tokyo Station. Nikken Sekkei handled design and supervision, Kajima Corporation built it, and it completed on 30 June 2025: 22,655.19 sq m gross, a maximum height of 55.96 m, 11 storeys above ground and three below, with offices, restaurants, retail and parking, plus a connection to the underground shopping network. Printing two sets of electrodes into a cement slab like interlocking fingers: Harbin Institute of Technology and the Chinese Academy of Sciences report a structural supercapacitor at 23.00 MPa compressive strength and 22.52 µWh/cm², published in ACS Nano on 1 October
Sources: ACS Nano (1 October, DOI 10.1021/acsnano.6c09927) · American Chemical Society press release (October) · TechXplore (1 October) · Nanowerk · 3Dprinting.com · Interesting Engineering
The paper is titled "Monolithic 3D-Printed Interdigitated Cement-Based Supercapacitors for Structural Energy Storage," by Haiping Wu and corresponding author Jing Zhong of the Harbin Institute of Technology, with Wencai Ren of the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Institute of Metal Research. The team mixed carbon nanotubes, carbon black and cement into a printable electrode ink, then used a 3D printer to deposit it onto a small concrete slab in an interdigitated pattern — two sets of electrodes interlocking like fingers across a cement separator, so ions need only cross the narrow gap between neighbouring fingers. Measured performance at a 1.0 V operating window: 22.52 µWh/cm² areal energy density and 0.49 mW/cm² areal power density, with 23.00 MPa compressive strength, comparable to the commercial concrete used in slabs and stairs. Three devices on a single slab, wired together, powered a small LED array. The researchers are equally clear about the limit: the device holds up under moderate heating and cooling, but performance drops sharply near −18 °C as ion movement freezes within the hydrated pores, and cold-weather fortification remains the main hurdle before deployment.

Taking the cross-laminated timber out of a 2019 pavilion and using it for interior walls: Museum Vonk in Eindhoven wraps itself in hempcrete and pigmented lime plaster, and crushed the site's old driving-school asphalt into a sub-base
Sources: Dezeen (4 October, photography Ronald Tilleman) · Studio Marco Vermeulen project page · Archello · Dezeen's 2019 Biobasecamp report · IABR
Museum Vonk stands in Eindhoven, the Netherlands, designed by Studio Marco Vermeulen for the institution formed by the merger of the Prehistoric Village museum and the Eindhoven Museum. It tells 10,000 years of regional history through hands-on, interactive displays and includes a "making shed" where visitors produce objects representing local history. Materials are the whole argument here. The primary structure is reclaimed cross-laminated timber (CLT), salvaged from Biobasecamp — the studio's own pavilion for Dutch Design Week 2019, built by Derix from around 200 cubic metres of modular 16 by 3.5 metre CLT boards and designed from the outset to come apart and be reused. Those boards are now the museum's interior walls. The facades are hempcrete, a bio-composite of hemp hurd and lime, prefabricated in the Netherlands and finished with pigmented lime plaster. The roof is an accessible green roof planted with grass, plants and shrubs, with integrated nesting spots for birds and bats, irrigated by collected rainwater. The site was formerly a driving-school track; the existing asphalt was crushed and reused on site as a layer beneath the foundations. Thermally, the hempcrete walls provide mass and ventilation hatches in the upper areas give passive cooling, backed by mechanical air handling. The studio describes the building as a testing ground for affordable bio-based construction, and the project team calls it the first museum building in the Netherlands to apply bio-based materials and sustainable techniques on a large scale and in so many different ways. Published material does not give carbon figures or a floor area, and we will not infer them.

Inheriting a demolished facade: Nikken Sekkei did not reproduce Murano's stone piers — it bought the same granite and moved the expression back to the window openings
Cross-checked against: ArchDaily (3 October, photography SS Co., Ltd.) · Daibiru Corporation project story page · Daibiru completion release (30 June 2025) · Daibiru centennial history, chapter five · Nikken Sekkei · Archinect project entry

Concept: what is inherited is a method, not a shape. The Yaesu Daibiru Building it replaced opened in 1967, designed by Togo Murano (1891–1984). Its facade combined stone with cast aluminium components, and its emphatic stone piers set the character of this stretch of Yaesu-dori for decades. The reconstruction framed its own brief as "heritage and innovation," but deliberately avoided quotation at the level of form. There is no replica colonnade. Instead the new building uses the same dark granite as the old one and carries forward three of Murano's operating principles: material richness, precision in facade composition, and responsiveness to the street. Client and architect describe this explicitly as an alternative to the standard curtain wall. What is actually inherited, in other words, is the judgement that an office building need not express itself through glazing systems.

Spatial strategy: the expression moves from the piers to the openings. The new facade does not rely on heavy vertical members. It organises light and shadow through fine stonework around the window openings — granite worked in layers at the reveal, so the shadow shifts as the sun moves and the elevation acquires depth. It is a transfer of weight from structural display to surface fabrication: the old building held the street with the scale of its piers, the new one with the precision of its stone cutting. At grade, the building addresses Yaesu-dori and connects to the underground retail network around Tokyo Station, with restaurants, retail and parking alongside the offices. Published material does not disclose lobby dimensions, floor-to-floor heights or the setback at street level, and we will not infer them.

Structure and performance: mid-story isolation, plus two green labels. Eleven storeys above ground and three below, 22,655.19 sq m gross (about 6,853 tsubo), maximum height 55.96 m, built by Kajima. The structure uses mid-story seismic isolation — the isolation layer sits partway up the building rather than at the foundation, a common choice for redevelopment in dense Tokyo blocks because it does not demand the clearance for lateral displacement that base isolation requires at the property line. The system is rated at the highest grade by Japan's structural engineering body. On performance, the office floors are certified ZEB Ready and LEED Gold — the first new Daibiru property to reach Gold — and the building runs on zero-carbon renewable electricity backed by non-fossil certificates. One detail that is easy to skip: trees grown from seeds taken from the old building's roof garden have been planted into the new landscape.

Context and consequence: choosing 11 storeys in a height race. Redevelopment on the Yaesu side over the past decade has run to supertall; neighbouring blocks carry towers well past 200 m. In that context, a 55.96 m, 11-storey office building is a deliberate position — its reference is not the skyline but the street section and the scale the old building left behind. For students, the case is worth noting because it offers a third route to "responding to heritage": not preservation in place, and not facadism, but treating material specification, fabrication method and relationship to the street as the inheritable content, then carrying them on contemporary structural and performance systems. The old building lasted only 58 years, which is its own footnote on Japanese office renewal cycles; whether the new one lasts longer depends on whether its performance labels still read as assets in the next cycle.

Studio Armitage builds a fully demountable field shelter among the rice paddies of Nara: one water tank serves at once as ballast, as the divider between two benches, and as a shared water store
Sources: Dezeen (3 October) · Studio Armitage (architects, founder Freddie Armitage) · Free York · Space Department (local creative hub)
Field Shelter sits among the rice fields and allotments of the Amagatsuji neighbourhood in Nara, Japan, designed by the London practice Studio Armitage. The brief was specific: people tending the allotments had nowhere to rest, and Nara's summer sun is punishing while the rain arrives suddenly and heavily. So is the answer. Impermanent concrete blocks lift the floor clear of wet ground; the frame is standard timber sections and battens with blue-painted end grain, both for weather protection and so the components read clearly; the roof is overlapping corrugated polycarbonate sheet. The decisive element is the central water tank, which acts as ballast against uplift, divides the two benches spatially, and doubles as a shared water store for the allotment holders. The two benches deliberately face opposite ways — one toward morning light, one toward evening. The whole thing is fully demountable and required no specialised fabrication. Armitage's own summary: "The shelter is essentially a raised floor, a roof and two benches. Those elements are enough to give some protection and a sense of enclosure without separating you from the fields." Dimensions, area and cost were not disclosed.

World Heritage was not enough to cap the heights: three months after a minister halted it, City Plan 2040 is cleared, with inspectors finding that protection and growth need not be a choice
Cross-checked against: BD Online (25 September) · City of London Corporation statements · SAVE Britain's Heritage · New London Architecture, London Tall Buildings Survey 2026 · public remarks by Chris Hayward, Tom Sleigh and Matthew Pennycook · Historic England's earlier objection

What the plan is trying to do. City Plan 2040 is the City of London Corporation's local plan for the Square Mile. Its economic proposition is to make a monoculture less monocultural: 1.2 million sq m of additional office space by 2040, with more leisure, retail and cultural space alongside. In three dimensions, that means setting building height guidelines for the eastern cluster — the part of the City where the towers already are — and raising the permitted heights on the cluster's southern side. The southern side happens to be where development activity is heaviest, and also the side nearest the Tower of London.

The spatial conflict is not about land take. It is about sightlines. The Tower of London is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and part of its heritage value lies in its setting — what appears on the skyline when you look at it from across the river, from Tower Bridge, from inside its own wards. Historic England objected on that basis: higher limits would push more towers into those views. So in June, weeks before the plan was due to be adopted, housing minister Matthew Pennycook issued a ministerial direction halting it outright. For a local plan that had completed its full preparation and consultation process, that is a highly unusual step.

Process and evidence: two days of rehearing, and a finding that the protections already suffice. Independent planning inspectors Alastair Phillips and Jameson Bridgwater reheard the point at Guildhall on 2 and 3 September, and on 23 September informed the Corporation that the plan adequately protects the Tower's setting. Pennycook withdrew his direction with immediate effect, clearing the plan for adoption before year-end; the inspectorate's final report is expected within weeks. What students should notice is the character of the ruling. The inspectors did not assess future towers one by one. They found that the height-control mechanism within the plan document itself is sufficient to carry the protective duty through to individual applications. The burden of heritage protection, in other words, was handed from a plan-level prohibition down to project-level scrutiny.

What each side said, and what it means. Policy chair Chris Hayward called the outcome "an outstanding result," saying the independent inspectorate had recognised that it is possible to protect one of the nation's most important heritage assets while still supporting the growth the country needs. Planning chair Tom Sleigh was blunter: "This was never a choice between the Tower of London and jobs... the Tower is protected and the City has room to grow." Pennycook said the plan "clearly demonstrates a thorough and considered assessment of the balance involved." Read from the heritage side, the implication is that in the English planning system the setting of a World Heritage Site is not on its own sufficient to translate into an area-wide height cap. The argument survives, but its point of application moves downstream to the individual application. Given the development pipeline on the southern edge of the eastern cluster over the next few years, that boundary will be tested repeatedly. For anyone working on density-versus-heritage questions, this is a case worth following to the end: it shows exactly which document, and which procedural step, absorbs or dilutes a conservation claim in a mature planning system.

From letting concrete grow its own conductive network to printing the electrodes to shape: an interdigitated geometry rewrites structural energy storage from a materials problem into a detailing problem
Cross-checked against: ACS Nano (1 October, DOI 10.1021/acsnano.6c09927) · American Chemical Society press release · TechXplore (1 October) · Nanowerk · 3Dprinting.com · Interesting Engineering · MIT's 2023 PNAS paper and MIT News, as the comparison case

The paper first. "Monolithic 3D-Printed Interdigitated Cement-Based Supercapacitors for Structural Energy Storage," published in ACS Nano on 1 October, DOI 10.1021/acsnano.6c09927. The authors are Haiping Wu and corresponding author Jing Zhong of the Harbin Institute of Technology, with Wencai Ren of the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Institute of Metal Research. Structural energy storage means making a load-bearing element double as the storage device, rather than adding batteries to a building.

Concept: moving the bottleneck from mix design to electrode geometry. There is a well-known precedent. In July 2023, a team at MIT led by Franz-Josef Ulm, Admir Masic and Yang Shao-Horn reported in PNAS that mixing cement, carbon black and water in the right proportions causes the carbon to migrate into the branching pore network formed during hydration and self-assemble into wire-like conductive structures — a percolated network at as little as 3 per cent carbon by volume. That is the "let the material grow its own electrode" route. The Chinese team took a different line: rather than relying on bulk percolation, print the electrodes in the shape you want. Carbon nanotubes, carbon black and cement are mixed into a printable electrode ink, then deposited by 3D printer onto a small concrete slab.

The detail that does the work: interdigitation shortens the ion path. The two printed electrode sets are interdigitated — interlocking like the fingers of two hands, with a cement separator between them. Supercapacitor efficiency depends heavily on how far ions must travel through the electrolyte; in this arrangement, they cross only the narrow gap between adjacent fingers rather than the full thickness of the element. The geometry itself becomes a performance parameter. That is a distinctly architectural turn: performance is no longer set by proportioning alone but by how things are arranged inside the element — and arrangement is precisely what additive manufacturing controls best.

The numbers, and the hard limit, together. At a 1.0 V operating window the device delivers 22.52 µWh/cm² areal energy density and 0.49 mW/cm² areal power density, with 23.00 MPa compressive strength, comparable to the commercial concrete used in slabs and stairs — meaning the storage is not bought at the cost of structural performance. For demonstration, three devices on a single slab, wired together, lit a small LED array. The limit is equally clear: the device is stable under moderate heating and cooling, but near −18 °C ion movement in the hydrated pores freezes and performance falls off sharply, which the team names as the main obstacle before deployment. Keep the scale honest: areal energy density in the microwatt-hour-per-square-centimetre range is orders of magnitude away from powering a house, and three devices lighting a few LEDs is the true measure of where this stands.

Consequence: if this route holds, the existing stock is not only structure. Zhong's framing is that multifunctional building materials could turn buildings from passive shelters into environments that store energy, sense their surroundings and interact with users. For architecture students the more immediate lesson is methodological: an electrochemical problem was improved by changing how components are laid out. Printable cement-based electrodes imply that a future reinforcement drawing might carry an electrode layout, and foundations, slabs and shear walls — already large in area — are the obvious candidates. But because they are structural elements, failure and replacement are a real question: current reporting says nothing about cycle life, capacity fade or serviceability, and we will not infer it.

London's 2026 Serpentine Pavilion, "a serpentine," closes on 25 October: the first pavilion in the series built entirely of brick, 30,000 of them handmade, in a wall one brick thick
Sources: Dezeen (20 January and 3 June) · Serpentine Galleries · World-Architects · International Union of Architects (UIA) · Arquitectura Viva
The twenty-fifth Serpentine Pavilion was designed by the Mexico City practice LANZA atelier, led by Isabel Abascal and Alessandro Arienzo. It opened in Kensington Gardens on 6 June and closes on 25 October — three weeks left. Titled "a serpentine," it is the first pavilion in the series built entirely in brick: roughly 30,000 bricks, handmade in South London in an earthy orange. The formal prototype is the English serpentine wall, also known as a crinkle-crankle wall: one brick thick, taking its lateral stability from the continuous curve in plan, and therefore using fewer bricks than a straight wall of the same length. It is a case where structural efficiency and form are the same thing — the curve is not a styling decision but the condition under which this wall can stand on its own. For anyone studying masonry construction, it is a rare built reference: stability can come from geometry rather than from thickness or added buttressing.

Care as a verb: the sixth Swiss territorial biennial shows no schemes and runs no crits — it asked ten teams to each make one thing about mending, in the park of a villa
Cross-checked against: Istituto Internazionale di Architettura press kit · v2com newswire · BSA-FAS agenda page · Bustler event listing · ArchDaily (Call for Action announcement) · public remarks by director Ludovica Molo

Curatorial concept: name the opposite first. The sixth biennale svizzera del territorio is organised by the Istituto Internazionale di Architettura (i2a) under the direction of architect Ludovica Molo, with the theme "HANDLE WITH CARE." Exhibitions on care have been plentiful lately and tend toward posture. This one puts it hard. Molo's framing is that care is "a complex alternative to the easier path of demolition and construction, hallmarks of an architecture that consumes territory and resources." Calling demolish-and-rebuild the easy way out is a clear curatorial position — it promotes repair, maintenance and regeneration from a sub-branch of the sustainability agenda to an alternative method for the discipline itself, with its means stated explicitly: attuned listening to places and to the pre-existing, plus community engagement.

Spatial strategy: the park of one villa, across three cities. The biennial is centred on Villa Saroli in Lugano and extends to Mendrisio and Bellinzona — three Ticino cities that between them span the Italian-speaking region's full register, from the academy (Mendrisio is home to the architecture school) to the cantonal capital. The ten core installations are not in a gallery. They are distributed through the park of Villa Saroli. For a theme about caring for what already exists, the siting is itself the argument: each work is placed into somewhere that already has trees and a history rather than a neutral white box, and so has to negotiate with what is there before it does anything else.

Ten works, and what the titles tell you about the method. The installations were selected through an open international Call for Action. The named projects include CAR^CAR space, Surface de réparation (literally a repair surface, and in French also the football penalty area), Sit For A While, Under the canopy, May the earth be light upon you (from the Roman funerary formula), MANU TENERE (Latin for "to hold by hand" — the root of the word maintain), (IN)VISIBLE GESTURES and Edible Curtains. Read together, the titles break "care" into workable branches: the specific surface to be mended, resting and lingering, shelter, mourning and memorial, the labour of the hand, invisible maintenance work, and the edible boundary. MANU TENERE is the sharpest of them, reminding us that maintenance is etymologically a matter of holding something with your hands — a task modern architecture has largely outsourced to facilities management.

Programme, audience and consequence. The three days (1–3 October) were not only an opening: the programme ran a symposium on architecture, territory and care, an urban walk through sites undergoing transformation in Bellinzona, Pecha Kucha presentations (20 slides, 20 seconds each), workshops and performative activations, with participants chosen by an international committee from among emerging architects, researchers and scholars, and free admission across the Lugano opening days. The ten installations remain in the park of Villa Saroli until 28 November — which is to say that once the opening heat dissipates, the works still have to sit in real weather and real use for nearly two months. For an exhibition about maintenance, that is the appropriate test. In context, the European curatorial line this year is strikingly consistent: the just-closed Oslo Architecture Triennale, next year's Copenhagen biennial on "Elements," and this edition's "Handle with Care" all point the same way — deal with what exists before discussing what to build. For students at Syracuse, the value of a show like this is not in its formal language but in the kind of brief it proposes: given a site that already exists and is still in use, how small can your intervention be and still work?

MAK Center Los Angeles sets Vienna's social housing beside LA's: opening 22 October at the Schindler House with ten trailblazing projects, 200,000 social housing units on one side and a housing crisis on the other

"SOCIAL HOUSING VIENNA—LOS ANGELES" runs 22 October 2026 to 17 January 2027 at the Schindler House in Los Angeles, presented by the MAK Center and curated by Beth Stryker, Michael Obrist and Sabine Pollak. The exhibition examines how architecture and housing intersect through case studies, presenting ten trailblazing projects via architectural models, drawings, photographs and building materials, reflecting the range of experimental housing design in both cities — including approaches that make it possible to live affordably in a metropolis against a backdrop of climatic and socio-political crisis. The contrast between the two cities carries the whole show: Vienna has more than 200,000 units of social housing and treats housing as a human right, while Los Angeles is deep in a housing crisis. The question posed is how city councils, architects and local authorities can spur radical initiatives by taking up fresh ideas, new concepts and civic initiatives from each other. The venue is not incidental either — the Schindler House is R. M. Schindler's own 1922 home, itself an experiment in co-living and low-cost construction.
