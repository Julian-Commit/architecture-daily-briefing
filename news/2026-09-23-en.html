Zumthor's Fondation Beyeler expansion begins a phased opening on 26 September: three new buildings share the work of one museum, with the 1,500 m² Wyss Museum shaped in a Y-plan around three stands of existing trees
Sources: designboom (23 Sept) · Fondation Beyeler, "The New Ensemble" · ArchDaily · HENI News · Dezeen (2017 scheme announcement)
The Fondation Beyeler in Riehen, outside Basel, opens Peter Zumthor's new buildings in stages from 26 September 2026, reaching full daily operation in January 2027. It is the museum's first substantial expansion in nearly three decades — since Renzo Piano's building of 1997. Zumthor did not build onto Piano. He broke the brief into three separate, modestly scaled structures. The Wyss Museum holds roughly 1,500 square metres of exhibition space over three floors; its plan is an irregular Y, generated around an existing ginkgo, a redwood and a group of plane trees. The inaugural exhibition, "Constellations," spans the Wyss Museum and Piano's building and runs from autumn 2026 into autumn 2027, shifting with the seasons. Zumthor on his intent: "I wanted my buildings to blend in so naturally, that it felt completely self-evident."

Google releases new Thompson Center renderings: Jahn's 1985 postmodern landmark is off the demolition list, its atrium reworked by A+I, all-electric and targeting LEED Platinum, opening 2028
Sources: ArchDaily (23 Sept) · The Architect's Newspaper (22 Sept) · Chicago YIMBY (Sept) · Dezeen (18 Aug, new facade) · Urbanize Chicago
Google announced its redevelopment of Chicago's James R. Thompson Center on 16 September 2026 and has released a further batch of renderings this week. Designed by Helmut Jahn and completed in 1985, the building is among the most recognisable works of American postmodern civic architecture — and, because of high maintenance costs and poor envelope performance, it spent three decades in and out of demolition debates. In 2015 then-governor Bruce Rauner proposed selling it; preservation groups including Landmarks Illinois and Docomomo campaigned to keep it. The Prime Group bought the building in 2022 and committed to renovation rather than demolition. Google is working with Architecture Plus Information (A+I, now part of Perkins&Will): the original glass-and-metal exterior has been coming off since May 2024 and is being replaced; the entry colonnade is heightened to rework the ground-floor experience; the lower atrium becomes, in A+I's words, "a cascading staircase integrated with terraced seating and retail offerings"; three levels of covered terraces bring in more daylight and connect to green space and biophilic elements; the plaza gains food, retail and seasonal event space. The renovated building will be all-electric and is designed to meet LEED Platinum targets. On the plaza will stand Vector, a 30-foot (roughly 9.1-metre) sculpture by Sanford Biggers drawing on reimagined quilt patterns, alongside a large commissioned piece made with After School Matters. Google employs more than 2,000 people in Chicago and plans to open the building to staff, tenants and the public in 2028.

The People's Theatre: Centro Cultural Inmigrante sits at 407 West 207th Street in Inwood, at the northern tip of Manhattan. The building covers 19,000 square feet (about 1,765 square metres), designed by WORKac with theatre and acoustics consultant Charcoalblue and built by Shawmut Design and Construction. The 9th Oslo Architecture Triennale, "What if Nature Comes First?", enters its second week: curated by Line Ramstad, 30-plus projects organised across four scales — Materials, Structures, Territories, Systems — in an 1877 church
Sources: ArchDaily (23 Sept, on-site review) · Oslo Architecture Triennale · Wallpaper* · ArchDaily (August programme announcement)
The ninth Oslo Architecture Triennale opened on 17 September 2026 and closes on 1 October under the theme "What if Nature Comes First?", curated by Line Ramstad. This edition drops the long-run exhibition model for a two-week festival format, setting the show alongside conferences, seminars, workshops, film screenings and guided walks. The main venue is Sofienberg Church in Oslo's Grünerløkka district — an 1877 neo-Gothic brick building currently being converted from a place of worship into a local cultural venue — with the exhibition occupying both its interior and its grounds. The curatorial frame sorts more than 30 international projects into four scales: Materials (the selection and reuse of construction materials), Structures (the lifespan of buildings), Territories (the relationship between architecture and land) and Systems (the economic and regulatory machinery that shapes development). Staging the show inside a building that is itself mid-conversion makes the argument without a wall text: adaptive reuse here is not an exhibit but the condition under which the exhibition takes place. The Oslo Architecture Triennale was founded in 2000 and is the largest architecture festival in the Nordic region.

MLZD's Tempelhof airport tower conversion published in full: one suspended steel stair and two lifts inserted into 6,675 m² of existing structure, a reconfigurable exhibition floor on level six, and a stepped timber roof deck opened to the city
Sources: gooood (published 23 Sept) · MLZD (architect) · Tempelhof Projekt GmbH (client, public material)
Swiss practice MLZD's conversion of the tower at Berlin's former Tempelhof Airport (Flughafen Tempelhof) was published in full by gooood on 23 September 2026. A date first: the project itself was completed in 2023, so this is a publication rather than a completion — but its construction logic earns the space. The tower is part of the vast complex Ernst Sagebiel designed for Tempelhof under the Nazi regime; it covers 940 square metres of ground with 6,675 square metres of gross floor area. The brief was set as avoiding "restricting any future usage options as much as possible," so the intervention is compressed into three moves: a new main staircase, exhibition and event space on level six, and a roof terrace. The main stair is a suspended steel staircase inserted inside the existing structure, threading visitors past finished areas and past raw sections that were never used; one partition wall is deliberately left in a dissolving state to document the tower's history of transformation. Level six uses movable walls so the plan can be reconfigured exhibition by exhibition; the direction of the timber flooring traces the line of the original central corridor. The roof is a stepped timber platform — a new public vantage over Berlin. Two new lifts make the whole sequence barrier-free. The material palette is natural stone, steel, timber, concrete and coatings.

Not an extension, an ensemble: Zumthor splits a museum expansion into three buildings and hides them in the trees beside Piano's
Cross-checked: designboom (23 Sept) · Fondation Beyeler, "The New Ensemble" · ArchDaily · HENI News · Dezeen (2017 scheme) · Artnet News (2017 budget reporting)

Concept: replace the word "extension." Museum expansions usually go one of two ways — a volume grafted onto the existing building, or a floor dug beneath it. In Riehen, Peter Zumthor took a third route: he touched not one column of Renzo Piano's 1997 Beyeler building, and instead broke the entire brief apart, distributing it across three independent, modestly scaled structures set among the trees of the adjacent Iselin-Weber Park. The institution's own word is "ensemble," not "extension," and the choice is not merely semantic. It abandons the new-wing-versus-old-wing confrontation, and with it the idea that an institutional upgrade needs a new architectural image to represent it. For students, the typological question worth pressing is this: when a museum that already has a clear architectural identity needs to double its capacity, is what it should add floor area — or relationships?

Spatial strategy: three buildings, three different negotiations with trees. The Wyss Museum carries the exhibition load: roughly 1,500 square metres over three floors. Its plan is an irregular Y, and the shape is not a compositional preference — it was forced by an existing ginkgo, a redwood and a group of plane trees on site. The building walks around them; all of them survive. The Ammann Pavilion is a single-storey social space enclosed in glass and oak, finished inside in cherry and pear wood, seating up to 250. Its critical move is operational rather than formal: it is free and open to the public independently of museum hours, meaning that people who do not buy a ticket still get an interior they can occupy at length. The Erlen Torhaus is the least conspicuous of the three — a stained oak timber frame handling art deliveries, unpacking and exhibition preparation, connected underground to the Wyss Museum. Giving a museum's least visible but most rigid logistical demand its own building, rather than burying it in a mezzanine behind the galleries, is a notably disciplined decision. Beyond the three, several historic structures were reworked: the Villa becomes a restaurant with a fireplace room and extended terrace; the Ammann Atelier becomes a base for art education workshops and school programmes; there is a music salon with a high-fidelity system and an Artist Record Collection; an experimental project space called The Folly; and the Orangerie, redesigned by India Mahdavi as a greenhouse and botanical shop.

Structure and material: one stone, carried all the way through. The Wyss Museum's material logic can almost be stated in a single material — Jura limestone. The local stone appears in the facade, in benches and staircases, ground into terrazzo floors, as aggregate in the ceilings, and in the mineral plaster. The wall construction is the part to note: the rammed-concrete facade takes a warm yellow tone from the local limestone in the mix, and it was built up by hand over nearly a year. That is an increasingly rare choice in contemporary museum construction — it gives up prefabrication and speed in exchange for material continuity under light. The same stone appears in several different states of working within one building, compressing the colour-temperature difference between wall, floor and ceiling to almost nothing. The Ammann Pavilion goes the other way, using glass and wood to take the weight out; the Erlen Torhaus, in stained oak framing, is the closest of the three to an agricultural structure. Three different material strategies — and all three deliberately decline to compete with the porphyry walls and glass roof of Piano's building.

Context and consequence: a decade-long privately funded project, and a park that doubles. Zumthor was invited in 2016 and the scheme was published in 2017; as Dezeen and Artnet News reported at the time, the budget was set at around CHF 100 million, entirely privately funded, with the Wyss Foundation and the Daros Collection having already pledged CHF 50 million. We have not obtained a final post-completion cost figure and will not speculate. For the public, the most concrete change may not be architectural at all: the formerly private Erlengarten — nearly 10,000 square metres — opens with the buildings, roughly doubling the park, with water lily ponds, centuries-old trees and works by Calder, Holzer, Kelly, Bourgeois, Huyghe and Whiteread. The opening runs in phases: limited access from 26 September 2026 to 14 January 2027, then full daily operation from January 2027. The inaugural exhibition, "Constellations," unfolds simultaneously across the Wyss Museum, Piano's building, the enlarged park and the repurposed historic structures, running from autumn 2026 into autumn 2027 and shifting seasonally. Zumthor states the goal in one line: "I wanted my buildings to blend in so naturally, that it felt completely self-evident." Coming from an architect known for the sheer intensity of his buildings, that sentence is the most interesting thing about the project.

Prague Fire Rescue Service headquarters, Czech Republic / Architektura: a nine-storey tower behind a grid of red glass-fibre-reinforced concrete
Sources: gooood (published 23 Sept) · Architektura (David Kraus, Miroslav Styk)
The complex sits at Argentinská 170/00 in Prague's Holešovice district on a 6,504-square-metre site, with a built-up area of 517 square metres and 2,257 square metres of usable floor area. It was designed and built between 2018 and 2023 and completed in 2025; gooood published it on 23 September. The two-phase campus consists of a nine-storey administrative tower, a technical and operational block, and a three-level drive-through gateway structure between the phases. The facades are defined by a disciplined grid of red glass-fibre-reinforced concrete (GRC) panels, the red taken directly from the fire service's institutional identity; the connecting volumes switch to square aluminium elements at ground level for a more technical register. Elsewhere the building leaves reinforced concrete exposed and masonry unplastered, and carries an 18.2-metre steel lattice antenna mast. It is a useful case of institutional identity pushed into a facade construction system rather than delivered through signage and paint.
XY Investments headquarters, phase three, Shanghai / Atelier Liu Yuyang Architects: cold-rolled steel, then glass, now timber — the geometry holds, the material changes
Sources: gooood (published 23 Sept) · Atelier Liu Yuyang Architects
The project is in Pudong, Shanghai, for client XY Investments; phase three was designed and built between August and November 2025. The "three curves" of the title track a material progression across the phases — cold-rolled steel plate, then glass, now timber — all sharing one curved geometric logic while producing entirely different spatial atmospheres. The plan follows an "archipelago" concept: functional zones keep independent identities while forming an integrated whole. A modular furniture system built on 25, 33 and 50-centimetre units allows free reconfiguration; on daylight, the practice proposes the "sunlit workspace," distributing prime daylight to all staff rather than privileging window-side seats. The tea room is a timber box nested inside the larger volume, and a flexible multi-function hall with retractable doors and stepped terraces switches between presentation space and seating. Principal materials: timber flooring (DeZign), cork (GRANORTE), wood veneer (Lixiang) and carpet (VOXFLOR).

Located at Pannonhalma in Hungary and designed by Okka Studio, published by gooood on 23 September; the practice describes the aim as showing the public the role forests play in sustaining the environment. Small forest structures of this kind have become common across Central Europe in recent years, and their difficulty is not formal but constructional: no heavy machinery on site, components carried by hand or light equipment, and foundations that must minimise disturbance to root systems — conditions that directly determine structural selection and jointing. How a postmodern building sentenced to death becomes a tech company's office: preservation, ownership and the civic ledger at the Thompson Center
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (23 Sept) · The Architect's Newspaper (22 Sept) · Dezeen (18 Aug) · Chicago YIMBY · Urbanize Chicago · Jahn Studio project material

Concept: what is being renovated is not the building but its broken relationship to the city. Helmut Jahn's James R. Thompson Center, completed in 1985, has always been read as an extreme case of postmodern civic architecture: a full-height glazed atrium packing state offices, retail and a transit concourse into one section, geometrically something like a sliced cylinder wedged into a street corner. Its problem lives in the same place. The full-height atrium performed terribly — overheating in summer, bleeding energy in winter — and as the envelope aged, maintenance cost became the direct justification for three decades of demolition talk. The renovation's underlying judgement is that the atrium's publicness was right, and what failed was the envelope and the ground-floor interface. So the scheme does not fill the atrium in. It replaces the skin first, then rebuilds how the atrium meets the ground.

Spatial strategy: reconnect the bottom of the atrium to the street. Architecture Plus Information (A+I, now part of Perkins&Will) reimagines the lower atrium as "a cascading staircase integrated with terraced seating and retail offerings" — the atrium floor, previously circulation and display, becomes an occupiable grandstand. The entry colonnade is heightened to rework the experience of arriving; three levels of covered terraces are added to bring in daylight and connect to green space, with biophilic design elements throughout. The outdoor plaza is reprogrammed for food, retail and seasonal events rather than pure through-movement. The whole set of moves points at one diagnosis: the 1985 scheme put its publicness in the section — a spectacular atrium — and not in the plan, where its contact surface with the street was thin. This renovation addresses the plan.

Structure and material: a new skin and a new energy system. The original glass-and-metal curtain wall has been coming off since May 2024, replaced by a new facade designed by Jahn; Dezeen published construction photographs of it on 18 August. The renovated building will be all-electric and is designed to meet LEED Platinum targets. That is the technically notable part: making a building all-electric when its defining feature is an enormous full-height atrium and its original design predates any meaningful energy standard is considerably harder than doing so from scratch — envelope performance, air movement and plant capacity all have to be recalculated together. On the plaza will rise Vector, a 30-foot (about 9.1-metre) sculpture by Sanford Biggers drawing on reimagined quilt patterns, alongside a large piece commissioned in partnership with the youth arts organisation After School Matters.

Context and consequence: preservation worked because ownership changed hands. The survival sequence is worth stating in full. In 2015, then-governor Bruce Rauner proposed selling the building. Preservation groups including Landmarks Illinois and Docomomo campaigned to keep it, arguing its significance as postwar civic architecture and as a major work of postmodernism. In 2022 The Prime Group purchased it and committed to renovation over demolition. Google announced its redevelopment on 16 September 2026 and plans to open to staff, tenants and the public in 2028; it currently employs more than 2,000 people in Chicago. That timeline makes an unromantic but essential point: for a civic building with high maintenance costs, a preservation campaign can usually buy time, but what actually decides survival is whether a new owner willing to absorb the renovation cost turns up. There is a second side to the ledger. A building once held by the state and open to every citizen becomes, after renovation, privately held corporate property, and "open to the public" rests on an owner's commitment rather than an institutional guarantee. That is a structural pattern recurring in postmodern civic reuse worldwide, and worth tracking as a long-running question.

For readers working on planning policy, this is a ready-made case study in how expansion-led planning gets re-legitimised in a southern European resort city.

In Depth
One bamboo gridshell, two claddings of opposite provenance: Studio Saar and Atelier One build an argument about protection and violence out of riot shields and farmers' rain capes
Cross-checked: Dezeen (14 Sept) · Architecture Today · London Design Festival · Archipanic · Wallpaper* · Infrastructure Now

A disclosure first. Of our four sections today, materials and construction genuinely had the thinnest slate of new publications. Rather than pad it out, we fill the slot with this month's London Design Festival landmark installation (12–20 September). The original reporting ran on 14 September — it is not today's news — but the construction logic deserves to be taken apart properly.

Concept: the pangolin's scales as a double analogy, structural and political. The Pangolin Shield was designed by Studio Saar, which practises in both Britain and India, with structural engineering by Atelier One under founder Neil Thomas. It takes its name from the pangolin — the world's most trafficked mammal, whose overlapping scales are simultaneously its natural armour and the reason it is hunted. That contradiction is translated directly into the installation's proposition: vulnerability and protection, acceptance and displacement, and the cultural exchange between East and West. The subject is Britain and India's colonial history, and the medium is not image or text but the difference in provenance between two coverings.

Structure and material: a bamboo gridshell under two skins of opposite identity. The primary structure is a bamboo gridshell. The principle is that a set of slender, initially straight members is woven into a flat grid, then arched and edge-restrained as a whole, gaining stiffness through geometric curvature rather than member depth — which means individual elements can be very thin while the assembly still covers a substantial span. Bamboo satisfies several conditions at once: its elasticity and tensile behaviour permit large bending, it is extremely light, it is renewable, and India has a mature craft tradition around it. Two layers sit on top of the shell. The first is made of Indian police shields constructed from lathis — the bamboo sticks used by police. The second is knups, the woven rain capes traditionally used by farmers in north-eastern India. Laid over one another, they produce the overlapping arrangement of a pangolin's scales. The sharpness of the material selection is this: the same plant, worked one way, becomes an instrument of suppression, and worked another way becomes a farmer's protection against monsoon rain. Stacking them on one roof lets the material make the argument.

Context and consequence: the site is part of the argument. The installation stands on the newly pedestrianised stretch of the Strand, outside Somerset House and near India House, oriented towards the bust of Jawaharlal Nehru, India's first prime minister, outside the Indian High Commission. That orientation is not incidental — it draws a line of sight between the installation and the colonial geography around it. As the London Design Festival's 2026 Landmark Project it sits alongside other threads of the festival: the concurrent Material Matters fair (15–17 September, fifth edition) gathered bricks, tiles and panels made from waste, compostable biochar-based foam and regenerative biomaterials, with exhibitors including Front and Biomason. For architecture students, two things here transfer directly. First, the gridshell's logic of trading geometry for section depth, and how well that holds up under low-tech conditions. Second, a harder question: when a material carries an explicit social provenance, should the architect keep that provenance legible in the construction — and how — rather than sanding it down into exotic texture?

Finland's Elementic launches MO6, a lignin-based wall material the company says stores about 2.4 kg of CO₂ per kilogram — roughly twice that of wood
Sources: Dezeen (4 Sept) · Elementic (manufacturer material)
Finnish company Elementic has released MO6, a wall-surfacing material made from forestry and timber industry offcuts and composed almost entirely of carbon. Its core feedstock is lignin — a by-product of the forestry and timber industries that is considered difficult to use and is usually burned for energy. The company states that one kilogram of MO6 stores approximately 2.4 kilograms of atmospheric carbon dioxide, about double the figure for an equivalent weight of wood, and that the material does not decay and is easy to recycle, potentially locking carbon away for thousands of years. A necessary caveat: these sequestration figures come from the manufacturer and we have not seen independent third-party life-cycle assessment. We report them as stated without endorsing them. For readers tracking low-carbon construction, lignin is one of the few recent routes moving a residue from waste disposal into building material proper.
Atlassian Central tops out in Sydney: 180 metres, 39 storeys, a concrete core with a steel exoskeleton and roughly 10,000 m³ of engineered timber — now the world's tallest hybrid timber tower
Sources: Dezeen (23 July) · designboom · Archinect · New Atlas · SHoP Architects / BVN
Atlassian Central in Sydney topped out in July 2026 at 180 metres (591 feet) across 39 storeys, designed by SHoP Architects with BVN, exceeding the previous record holder — the 25-storey Ascent in Milwaukee — by roughly 100 metres. The structure pairs a concrete core with a steel exoskeleton and contains about 10,000 cubic metres of engineered timber, mostly glued-laminated (glulam) columns and cross-laminated timber (CLT) floor plates. Practical completion of the structure is expected in late 2026. The technical significance is not the "tallest" label but what the project makes explicit about the current limits of tall timber: pure timber structures remain unviable at this height, and hybrid systems are the only route past 100 metres, with steel and concrete taking lateral stability while timber carries vertical and floor loads. We will publish full figures at completion.

Put the exhibition inside a church mid-conversion: the Oslo Architecture Triennale asks an impolite question across four scales
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (23 Sept, on-site review) · Oslo Architecture Triennale · Wallpaper* · ArchDaily (August programme) · D5 Mag

Curatorial concept: turn "nature first" back into a question. The ninth Oslo Architecture Triennale runs under the theme "What if Nature Comes First?", curated by Line Ramstad. The phrasing matters. This is not the declaration "nature comes first" but a conditional with a question mark on the end. At a moment when sustainability rhetoric has inflated almost past usefulness, that construction shows some discipline: it concedes that architecture's current mode of production does not start from ecology, and treats "what if it did" as something to be argued rather than affirmed. The triennale runs from 17 September to 1 October 2026 and is now in its second week.

Organisation: a two-week festival, four scales. This edition abandons the two-to-three-month run typical of architecture biennials and triennials for a two-week festival format, placing the exhibition alongside conferences, seminars, workshops, film screenings and guided walks. The compression involves a clear trade-off — total attendance falls, but the density of live discussion rises, which suits treating the event as a concentrated academic occasion rather than a destination. The content sorts more than 30 international projects into four escalating scales: Materials, covering the selection and reuse of construction materials; Structures, covering the lifespan of buildings; Territories, covering the relationship between architecture and land; and Systems, covering the economic and regulatory machinery that shapes development. Running from a single material all the way up to a body of regulation answers the most common failing of ecological themes in architecture exhibitions: they stop at the material and never touch the institutional.

The venue is the argument. The main site is Sofienberg Church in Oslo's Grünerløkka district, an 1877 neo-Gothic brick church currently being converted from a place of worship into a community cultural venue; the exhibition uses both its interior and its grounds. That choice makes the venue the show's first exhibit: adaptive reuse here is not a case-study photograph on a wall but something happening under the visitors' feet. Wallpaper*'s on-site report also notes a sauna — a characteristically Nordic move to bring bodily experience into an architecture exhibition, and directly germane to the theme.

Context and consequence: what a small festival is good for. The Oslo Architecture Triennale was founded in 2000 and is the largest architecture event in the Nordic region. Compared with the Venice Architecture Biennale it is far smaller, and gains from it: its themes tend to advance continuously across editions rather than restarting each time. Two things here are directly usable for readers at Syracuse. One is the four-scale framework itself — a ready-made analytical structure for the research phase of a studio, and one that forces you to push a material decision all the way through to regulation and finance. The other is the use of the site: if your studio problem involves adaptive reuse, treating the process of conversion as the thing to be exhibited and discussed is usually more persuasive than presenting the finished state. The first Pan-African Biennale of Architecture opens in Nairobi: curated by Omar Degan under "Shifting the Center: From Fragility to Resilience," anchored in the 1973 Kenyatta International Convention Centre
Sources: The Architect's Newspaper (22 Sept, on-site report) · ArchDaily (participants announcement) · Dezeen (curator interview) · Wallpaper* · Architizer
The inaugural Pan-African Biennale of Architecture opened in Nairobi, Kenya, on 1 September 2026, founded and curated by Omar Degan, the Italian-born Somali architect who founded DO Architecture Group, under the theme "Shifting the Center: From Fragility to Resilience." Its anchor venue is the Kenyatta International Convention Centre (KICC), inaugurated in 1973 and designed by the Norwegian architect Karl Henrik Nøstvik with David Mutiso, one of Kenya's first African architects — a building that is itself a symbol of post-independence ambition. International coverage has consistently described it as the first continent-wide architecture biennale led from within Africa, with exhibitions planned from all 54 African countries and the diaspora, rotating between African cities every two years. We reported Lesley Lokko's African Futures Institute opening its permanent Accra headquarters in our 22 September issue; the two point at the same shift.
A new journal, Wedge, will publish the Journal of Architectural Education's cancelled Palestine issue: three editorial directors, first issue next spring in print and online, no paywall
Sources: The Architect's Newspaper (21 Sept) · Literary Hub · Archinect · Association of Collegiate Schools of Architecture (ACSA) public statement
A new peer-reviewed scholarly architecture journal called Wedge will publish the previously cancelled Palestine issue — issue 79.2 — of the Journal of Architectural Education (JAE). The background: in February 2025 the board of directors of the ACSA, JAE's publisher, halted publication of the issue, framing the decision as a reappraisal of legal risk; JAE's editorial board then resigned en masse and the executive editor was fired. Wedge takes its name from the two things a wedge does — splitting an object through force, or prying and keeping a door, a window, a portal open. The journal is guided by 12 editorial board members and led by three editorial directors: Ozayr Saloojee, professor at Carleton University in Ottawa; Caitlin Blanchfield, professor at Cornell; and Ersela Kripa, founding partner of AGENCY. The first issue arrives next spring as a book and online, with no paywall, produced in partnership with Landscape Architecture Projections, the design research arm of Leuven University Press in Belgium, which is providing funding support. We report the facts and the parties' public statements and offer no judgement on the dispute itself.
The Gaetano Pesce Foundation launches: a nonprofit taking custody of the archive, prototypes and authorised editions, with Glenn Adamson as president
Sources: designboom (22 Sept) · Salon 94 (concurrent exhibition)
A foundation named for the Italian architect and designer Gaetano Pesce (1939–2024) has been established as a nonprofit. Giulia Tosciri is director; the design historian and close colleague Glenn Adamson is president; Michael J. Greenberg is secretary; Edward Secker is treasurer; and Alessandro Byther, creative director at Gaetano Pesce Studio, is a director. The foundation safeguards Pesce's archive of objects, drawings, prototypes and documentation while supporting exhibitions, publications, scholarship and authorised editions, with emphasis on his interdisciplinary reach across art, design, architecture, performance and social commentary. Adamson's assessment: "Radical, poet, and provocateur, Pesce is one of the most influential figures in art and design of the past century." The launch coincides with "Gaetano Pesce Studio: Continued" at Salon 94, showing the continued production of authorised editions alongside archival preservation. Pesce on his own creative freedom: "To be free means to be incoherent."
