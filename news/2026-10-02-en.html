EDITOR'S NOTE

Today's through-line is what you do with a building you already have. MIT's School of Architecture and Planning held the formal dedication of its new home yesterday: Diller Scofidio + Renfro performed what Elizabeth Diller calls surgery on the 1894 Metropolitan Storage Warehouse — four cascading glass walls down the north façade, double-height studios suspended from the existing masonry and steel, new windows cut through two-foot-thick walls. It opens to the public tomorrow. In Gresham, Oregon, the East County Library takes the opposite route: 96 columns, 1,200 beams and 300 CLT panels left fully exposed with no fire-protective covering at all, $163 million for 95,000 square feet, reviewed in Architectural Record's October issue. On the policy side, New York City named the 12 community districts where its voter-approved Affordable Housing Fast Track will apply from January 2027. Those districts are roughly a fifth of the city and delivered 1.1% of its new affordable housing over five years; the final call there moves from the City Council to the City Planning Commission. The same day, federal passenger rail investment fell from $15.86 billion to $2.96 billion. Plus: Amman Design Week returns today after six years, and RCR Arquitectes' Large opens in Paris on October 17.

MIT's School of Architecture and Planning held the formal dedication of its new home yesterday: DS+R dropped four cascading glass walls down the 1894 Metropolitan Storage Warehouse and hung double-height studios off the existing masonry and steel. Public opening tomorrow, October 3, as part of MIT Future Fest
Sources: MIT News (August 12) · The Architect's Newspaper (August) · Dezeen (August 13) · The Tech, MIT student paper (September 17) · Tradeline · Daily Commercial News (September)
The Metropolitan Storage Warehouse — Met Warehouse on campus — sits in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Designed by Frederic Pope, it was first completed in 1894 and built out in phases to 1923, originally holding roughly 1,500 storage units. MIT has owned it since the 1960s; it was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1986, which means any exterior change requires City of Cambridge approval. The conversion architect is Diller Scofidio + Renfro (DS+R). The building is over 500 feet long and five storeys high. Moving in are the Department of Architecture, the Department of Urban Studies and Planning (DUSP), the Norman B. Leventhal Center for Advanced Urbanism and the MIT Morningside Academy for Design (MAD), along with studios, classrooms, an auditorium, galleries and extensive shared space. Four moves define the project: four cascading glass walls down the north façade; double-height studios suspended from the existing masonry and steel; windows expanded through walls two feet thick; and a new connective staircase plus building-long walkways on all five floors, with original brick left exposed at key points so old and new construction read together. The timeline: faculty and staff moved in during August, a ceremonial procession took place on September 8, the formal dedication was October 1, and the public open day is October 3 as part of MIT Future Fest. Elizabeth Diller: the building is stubborn and big and heavy, and we saw potential that it could endure some surgery. Dean Hashim Sarkis calls it a new centre of gravity for the campus.

96 columns, 1,200 beams, 300 CLT panels — all fully exposed with no fire-protective covering: Holst Architecture's East County Library in Oregon cost $163 million for 95,000 square feet, built on a decommissioned light-rail park-and-ride
Sources: Architectural Record (October 1 building review, October issue) · Willamette Week (May 19 opening report) · Multnomah County Library (client) · Holst Architecture (architect) · Portland Tribune (2023 groundbreaking) · International Mass Timber Conference (tour listing)
The East County Library sits in Gresham, Oregon, 17 miles east of Portland. Client: Multnomah County Library. Cost: $163 million. Area: 95,000 square feet. Completed May 2026, opening to large crowds on May 16. The architect is Portland-based Holst Architecture — Kim Wilson, principal in charge; Brittany Shreiner, design principal; Dave Otte, senior project manager. The project began as a Holst collaboration with Adjaye Associates, which was dropped after allegations of sexual misconduct against that firm's founder; Holst carried it through. The structure is a Type IV heavy-timber system: 96 columns, 1,200 beams and 300 cross-laminated timber (CLT) panels, all left fully exposed without additional fire-resistive treatment. Visible CLT faces use Douglas fir to match the columns and beams. Split-laminated beams conceal mechanical runs within their reveals. The façade combines a board-formed concrete base, massive timber columns and fluted ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC) panels; the roof extends 55 feet, which the architects liken to a front-porch awning, to shelter the entrances. The two-storey, 59-foot-tall building arranges use-specific rooms — quiet rooms, makerspaces, meeting rooms — around a double-height central atrium with a spiral stair. Open stacks hold 200,000 books. The parcel is only 23,000 square feet, wedged between a busy parkway, a rail station and a high-speed-bus stop. The project is LEED certified, with Qcells photovoltaics.

A 1,000-square-metre pillar-free hall on a car-factory island: RCR's first Paris building lifts 5,000 square metres off the ground, half for exhibitions and half for arriving
Cross-checked against: Arquitectura Viva (project record, photography Nicolas Trouillard) · ArchDaily (design announcement) · designboom (September) · Paris je t'aime (tourist board) · FAD Magazine (July 7) · Design Scene (July, Pointe des Arts report)

Design concept: lift the volume so the island's silhouette reads before the building does. Large — cultures contemporaines occupies the upstream tip of Île Seguin in Boulogne-Billancourt, France. Total area is 5,000 square metres; the architects are RCR Arquitectes with CALQ Architecture, and it opens on October 17, 2026. RCR is the three-partner Catalan practice of Rafael Aranda, Carme Pigem and Ramon Vilalta, awarded the Pritzker Prize in 2017; this is their first built work in Paris. The governing move is to lift the main volume off the ground and shape it as a continuous, sinuous form. That lift has a site-specific reason: Île Seguin is a narrow island in the Seine, legible in profile from both banks. A volume sitting flat on the ground would turn the island into a solid embankment; raised, it leaves a band of transparency between water, shoreline and building. Published material describes this elevated sinuous form as the building's defining feature.

Spatial strategy: half the floor area for exhibitions, the other half spent entirely on getting in. About 2,500 of the 5,000 square metres is exhibition space: one 1,000-square-metre pillar-free hall plus two 500-square-metre galleries. Pillar-free is the hardest commitment in the brief — large contemporary installations, moving image and performance setups are precisely what a mid-span column ruins. A 1,000-square-metre column-free span means the structure must carry everything to the perimeter, which in turn explains the raised, curved volume: a shell surface forms effective perimeter load paths far more readily than a flat plate. The other half is not a stack of back-of-house rooms but a full arrival sequence: a 370-square-metre atrium rising 7 metres, plus café, bookshop, education spaces and panoramic terraces. Circulation is deliberately organised to shift between scales and framed views, with daylight filtered through screens. For interiors dominated by anodised aluminium and Cor-Ten, filtering is not only atmospheric — it is how you avoid harsh specular glare off metal surfaces in direct sun.

Materials and construction: Cor-Ten and anodised aluminium, two ways of writing time onto a surface. The cladding pairs Cor-Ten steel with anodised aluminium. On a former car-factory island the combination points somewhere specific. Cor-Ten protects itself through a stable surface rust layer; it is left uncoated and deepens in colour with the years — the material vocabulary of industrial building. Anodised aluminium carries an electrolytically grown oxide film: colour-stable, corrosion-resistant, effectively maintenance-free — the material vocabulary of automotive and precision manufacture. Setting both on the same volume means one face keeps changing while the other holds constant. It should be said that published material does not disclose the structural system in detail — how the pillar-free hall is spanned, how the curved shell is built, or the cost — and this briefing will not infer it.

Context and consequence: a 53,000-square-metre arts district, and an island's third change of use. Large anchors the Pointe des Arts, a 53,000-square-metre mixed-use district that converts the Renault factory island into a cultural destination on the Seine. Île Seguin has therefore passed through three phases: car manufacturing, a long vacancy and contested demolition, and now culture and mixed development. The inaugural exhibition, Moteur Imaginaire, is curated by Cecilia Alemani — curator of the main exhibition at the 2022 Venice Biennale — running from the Renault corporate collection through to contemporary work, with 55 artists from 23 countries, on the relationship between humans and machines. That curatorial frame and the material palette follow one logic: do not erase the industrial history, but make it the shared subject of both the exhibitions and the building. For architecture students, the useful comparison is with European industrial-heritage conversions generally. Île Seguin did not take the preserve-the-shell route — the factory sheds are gone — but instead names that history through material and massing on a cleared site. The cost of that choice is that the building has to carry the entire narrative on its own.

Casa Ani / Chain + Siman, Valle de Bravo, Mexico: 689 square metres split into two buildings — a stone-and-timber single-pitch volume meeting a three-gabled form wrapped in black standing-seam metal
Sources: Dezeen (October 1) · Chain + Siman (architect) · Free York (October) · E-Architect (October)
The house sits in Valle de Bravo, a few hours from Mexico City, measures 689 square metres and was completed in 2025 by Mexico City practice Chain + Siman. The move is to split a weekend house into two forms and let them argue: on one side a single-pitch volume in stone and timber, its type drawn from local building and doubling as a bunkhouse; on the other an east-facing three-gabled contemporary form clad in black standing-seam metal, where independent A-frame roofs cover the living and dining room, the kitchen and the primary suite, each elevated above floor-to-ceiling glazing so the roofs read as lifted clear of the glass. The architects describe it as a house that grew out of a dialogue between old and new — a conversation across materials and time: stone against metal, tile against steel, the weight of history against the lightness of the new. Read it against the usual regionalist house: rather than imitating a traditional type in appearance, it assigns each material system to its own volume, so the junction between them, not the skin, carries the old-and-new relationship.
MIND / C.F. Møller, Middelfart, Denmark: an 1888 asylum building becomes the museum of Danish psychiatric history, keeping its classical plan and gaining a MINDwalk stair from ground floor to basement
Sources: Dezeen (October 1) · C.F. Møller Architects (architect) · VisitDenmark (official tourism)
The project is at Teglgårdsparken 17 in Middelfart on the island of Funen, Denmark, converting one of the original 1888 hospital buildings in Teglgårdsparken into a museum of the history of Danish psychiatry. Architect: C.F. Møller Architects; MIND opened in May 2026. The core judgement is to leave the building's classical character and clear plan structure intact, stage its history through colour and material, and then add entirely new elements to meet museum requirements. The specific interventions: a new main staircase; new connections cut between rooms that were previously unconnected; an internal stair called MINDwalk running from the ground floor down to the basement; and a café detailed with deliberate precision and simplicity. Converting a former asylum into a museum about psychiatry makes the building itself part of the exhibit — keeping the sequence of rooms and the scale of the corridors says more about how such institutions once organised people's lives than any wall panel could.

883 homes out of 63,870: New York used a voter-approved charter amendment to take the final land-use call away from the City Council in the 12 districts that built the least affordable housing
Cross-checked against: NYC Mayor's Office release (October 1) · The Real Deal (October 1) · Planetizen (October) · amNewYork · QNS (October) · Connect CRE

Where the mechanism comes from: not an executive order, but a November 2025 vote. The Affordable Housing Fast Track rests on a City Charter amendment approved by New York City voters in November 2025. That origin matters: the mayor and the Department of City Planning can decide the list, but they cannot decide whether the mechanism exists — it is already in the charter. On October 1, Mayor Zohran Kwame Mamdani and the Department of City Planning named the first 12 community districts where it applies, effective January 2027. Putting the institutional change to a citywide referendum rather than through legislation routes around exactly the body most likely to oppose it: the City Council itself.

How the districts were picked: by delivery, not by site conditions. The 12 were selected on a single outcome-based measure — the lowest actual affordable-housing delivery over the past five years. Not floor-area ratio, not land constraint, not demographics. The numbers are blunt: these 12 are roughly a fifth of the city's 59 community districts, and of 63,870 new affordable homes built citywide in that period, they account for 883, or 1.1%. The list is Brooklyn CD 10 (Bay Ridge, Dyker Heights), CD 12 (Borough Park, Kensington), CD 18 (Canarsie, Flatlands); Manhattan CD 7 (Upper West Side), CD 8 (Upper East Side, Roosevelt Island); Queens CD 4 (Corona, Elmhurst), CD 5 (Ridgewood, Maspeth, Middle Village), CD 10 (South Ozone Park, Howard Beach), CD 11 (Auburndale, Bayside, Douglaston), CD 13 (Queens Village, Bellerose, Rosedale); Staten Island CD 2 (Mid-Island), CD 3 (South Shore). The geography is worth noting: Queens accounts for five, both of Staten Island's listed districts appear, and the Bronx has none.

What actually changes procedurally: a shorter clock, and a different final decision-maker. Two changes. The first is a shortened land use review timeline — New York's standard process, ULURP (Uniform Land Use Review Procedure), sets statutory day counts at each stage, and compressing them directly compresses the window in which opposition can organise. The second matters more: for qualifying affordable housing projects, the final decision no longer rests with the City Council but moves to the mayorally appointed City Planning Commission. That touches a long-standing structural feature of American city planning — member deference, the convention by which a council defers on land-use votes to the member representing the district, which in practice gives a single council member a veto over projects in their own district. Moving the final call to a mayorally appointed commission replaces a district-by-district veto point with a citywide body.

What it means for design practice, and what to watch. For architects, the practical effect of this kind of procedural reform shows up early in a project: a shorter approval period and lower veto risk change the feasibility arithmetic for affordable housing, and they change how much the design is asked to concede. A great many of the storeys, densities and volumes stripped out during community review are products of procedural risk rather than design judgement. But two caveats need stating plainly. First, the Fast Track acts only on the approval process. It adds no subsidy and changes nothing about what zoning permits; a smoother process does not solve financing or land costs. Second, moving the decision from an elected council to an appointed commission is an explicit trade of democratic process for delivery speed, and its longer-term consequences cannot be assessed until projects actually land. Published material does not yet disclose the eligibility thresholds for Fast Track treatment — affordability share, minimum project size — and this briefing will not infer them.

US federal passenger rail investment fell from $15.86 billion to $2.96 billion on October 1 as IIJA advance appropriations expired and the stopgap declined to continue them; Federal-State Partnership grants dropped from $7.265 billion to $65 million
Sources: Planetizen (October 1) · Engineering News-Record · Transport Topics · Metro Magazine · American Public Transportation Association, APTA (estimates) · Rail Passengers Association
The surface transportation advance appropriations provided by the 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA) expired on October 1, 2026. A stopgap signed on September 3 extends Highway Trust Fund programmes at fiscal 2026 levels through December 11, but does not continue the IIJA's advance appropriations. The American Public Transportation Association (APTA) estimates the result is an over 80% cut to federal passenger rail investment and about a 20% cut to federal public transit investment. Passenger rail falls from $15.86 billion to $2.96 billion. Grants under the Federal-State Partnership for Intercity Passenger Rail drop from $7.265 billion to $65 million, a 99% cut. The Railroad Crossing Elimination programme goes from $600 million to nothing. Consolidated Rail Infrastructure and Safety Improvements (CRISI) grants shrink 88% to $137.4 million. APTA president and CEO Paul Skoutelas says it will immediately disrupt and delay ongoing planning, engineering and construction of surface transportation projects nationwide, and inject uncertainty into future state and local investment decisions. For urban design, this kind of funding break usually shows up first in the schedules for transit-oriented development and station retrofit projects.

The first US battery-powered commuter train is unveiled in Chicago on October 5: Metra has ordered eight battery FLIRT trainsets from Stadler, likely first on the frequent-stop 16.4-mile Beverly Branch, with passenger service expected around the start of 2028
Sources: Planetizen (October) · Stadler Rail press release · Trains Magazine · Railway News · Interesting Engineering

Skip the fire-protective covering entirely and let 96 columns, 1,200 beams and 300 CLT panels be the interior finish: a 95,000-square-foot mass-timber library on a 23,000-square-foot site
Cross-checked against: Architectural Record (October 1 building review, October issue next-generation libraries feature) · Willamette Week (May 19 opening report) · Multnomah County Library (client) · Holst Architecture (architect) · Portland Tribune (July 2023 groundbreaking) · International Mass Timber Conference (Southeast Metro tour)

Design concept: the library as a platform for self-determination, not a container for books. The East County Library is in Gresham, Oregon, 17 miles east of Portland. Client: Multnomah County Library. Cost: $163 million. Area: 95,000 square feet. Completed May 2026 and opened May 16. The architect is Portland-based Holst Architecture — Kim Wilson, principal in charge; Brittany Shreiner, design principal; Dave Otte, senior project manager. The project began as a Holst collaboration with Adjaye Associates, which withdrew after allegations of sexual misconduct against that firm's founder; Holst completed it alone. The former library director put the intent plainly: libraries are a platform for self-determination, and we made a strong commitment to serving the furthest from opportunity. That has a corresponding entry on the programme sheet — community-requested additions include a free-to-reserve auditorium, dedicated rooms accommodating neurodivergent users, and a recording studio.

Spatial strategy: 95,000 square feet on a 23,000-square-foot parcel, resolved by an atrium. The site is only 23,000 square feet, wedged between a busy parkway, a rail station and a high-speed-bus stop; it was previously a light-rail park-and-ride. Trading a parcel that existed only to store cars for a public building is itself the premise of the project. The library is two storeys and 59 feet tall — a tall section for two floors, and the reason is how the plan works: every room requiring enclosure (quiet rooms, makerspaces, meeting rooms) is arranged around a double-height central atrium with a spiral stair, and the atrium carries daylight, orientation and vertical connection all at once. Open stacks hold 200,000 books. The roof extends 55 feet to shelter the entrances, which the architects liken to a front-porch awning — in Pacific Northwest rainfall, an overhang that size is not a formal gesture but a practical provision for people waiting for a bus or for opening time.

Structure and material innovation: Type IV heavy timber, fully exposed, with no additional fire treatment. The structural system is Type IV heavy timber: 96 columns, 1,200 beams and 300 cross-laminated timber (CLT) panels. The key sentence is that all of it is left fully exposed with no additional fire-resistive treatment. This matters because mass-timber fire strategy splits two ways — encapsulate in gypsum board or sprayed protection, or rely on the timber's own charring layer to maintain load capacity for the code-required duration. The second route requires member sections sized at design stage with sacrificial thickness calculated from char rate, substantiated through structural fire engineering. The payoff is that once built, no additional finish layer is needed: the structure is the interior surface. Visible CLT faces deliberately use Douglas fir to match the species of the columns and beams — when everything is exposed, every variation in colour and grain is seen, so species consistency is not an aesthetic preference but a necessary corollary of exposure. Split-laminated beams conceal mechanical runs within their reveals, and the remaining mechanical plant is pushed into the basement and between floors. This is the hardest part of exposed mass timber: surface-run services destroy the whole approach, and adding a ceiling defeats it. The façade combines a board-formed concrete base, massive timber columns and fluted ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC) panels — concrete takes the durability and moisture exposure at grade, timber appears where people can see and touch it.

Context and consequence: the arithmetic behind $163 million and a LEED certificate. The project is LEED certified with Qcells photovoltaics. At $163 million for 95,000 square feet — roughly $1,700 per square foot — this is a high unit cost for a public library, and the reasons are scattered through the facts above: a constrained site, the tolerances exposed mass timber demands, embedded services, a large cantilevered roof. For architecture students, the most useful thing here is that it separates two different uses of mass timber. This is not timber substituting for steel and concrete to lower embodied carbon — the concrete base and UHPC panels are still there. It is timber taking every surface within human reach while eliminating an entire layer of secondary finishes. Compared with contemporaneous mass-timber projects pitched primarily on carbon, this library offers a different argument: exposed mass timber is less a material choice than a method of construction that requires structure, services and finishes to concede ground simultaneously.

Design Museum Gent's new wing is complete and reopens tomorrow, October 3: 2,200 square metres clad in more than 82,000 Gent Waste Bricks made from the city's demolished concrete frames, waste glass, car windscreens and porcelain, cured with low-energy lime

Cutting open a 500-foot-long 1894 warehouse with two-foot-thick walls: how MIT fitted an entire school into a National Register building
Cross-checked against: MIT News (August 12) · The Architect's Newspaper (August) · Dezeen (August 13) · The Tech, MIT student paper (September 17) · Tradeline (August) · Daily Commercial News (September, construction report)

Design concept: accept that the building can take surgery rather than only reverence. The Metropolitan Storage Warehouse — Met Warehouse on campus — stands in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Frederic Pope designed the first phase in 1894; construction continued to 1923, producing roughly 1,500 storage units. MIT has owned it since the 1960s, and it joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1986, which means every exterior change needs City of Cambridge approval. The conversion architect is Diller Scofidio + Renfro (DS+R). Elizabeth Diller states the basis of judgement precisely: the building is stubborn and big and heavy, and we saw potential that it could endure some surgery. The three adjectives are not rhetorical — walls two feet thick, over 500 feet long, five storeys, and an original use of storage, which means both floor loading and masonry thickness far exceed what a teaching building requires. Put differently, the redundancy is the margin that makes conversion possible. MIT structural engineering professor John Ochsendorf's verdict: it is almost inconceivable that this brick box has been opened up.

Spatial strategy: the studios do not sit on the floor plates, they hang off the existing structure. The school needed double-height design studios, but the warehouse's floor-to-floor heights were set for storage — neither tall enough nor freely removable. DS+R's answer is to suspend the double-height studios from the existing masonry and steel: not stacking new construction on the slabs, but letting new structure transfer load back into the old. What makes that possible is exactly the redundancy noted above — the masonry walls and original steel frame were designed for dense storage loading. Horizontal and vertical connection is handled by two new components: a new connective staircase, and building-long walkways on all five floors. A 500-foot length is a real problem for a school building — the distance from one end to the other is enough to keep two departments from ever meeting — and the building-long walkway converts that length from an obstacle into a route where you run into people. Original brick is left exposed at key points so old and new construction read in the same view.

Structure and envelope: windows expanded through two-foot walls, four cascading glass walls down the north face. Daylight is the hardest problem in converting a storage building into a teaching one: the window openings are small and sparse, and the walls are two feet thick. Two moves answer it. The first is expanding windows within the existing openings — cutting through two-foot masonry is not simply a matter of making a hole, since lintels above and the overall wall behaviour both have to be reorganised, which is precisely what Ochsendorf means by inconceivable. The second is four cascading glass walls down the north façade. North is the right choice: north light is stable and non-direct, the ideal condition for design studios and review space. Making the glazing four cascading segments rather than one continuous plane both avoids opening a single aperture out of scale with a listed building's massing and gives a 500-foot elevation some rhythm. At the construction level, the core approach is described as cutting strategies plus new support systems that redistribute existing loads to free up the clear heights studios and makespaces need.

Context and consequence: consolidating a school from several buildings into one. Moving in are the Department of Architecture, the Department of Urban Studies and Planning (DUSP), the Norman B. Leventhal Center for Advanced Urbanism and the MIT Morningside Academy for Design (MAD), plus studios, classrooms, an auditorium, galleries and extensive shared space. Timeline: faculty and staff moved in during August; a ceremonial procession named The Living Bucentaur took place on September 8; the formal dedication was October 1; the public open day is October 3 as part of MIT Future Fest. Dean Hashim Sarkis says it is going to be a new centre of gravity for the campus. The project matters to architecture students on two levels. First, the conversion itself: it demonstrates how redundant load capacity in existing industrial stock converts into spatial freedom, and how the façade constraints on a listed building can be negotiated through segmented rather than wholesale intervention. Second, the organisation of architectural education — MIT chose to put architecture, urban planning and design research into one building sharing a single 500-foot walkway, rather than leaving each in its own. For any school currently debating how to consolidate departmental space, this is a directly comparable sample.
