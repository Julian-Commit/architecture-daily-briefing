EDITOR'S NOTE

Twenty-five years after 9/11, three of today's leads interlock. On the night of 9 September, 2,977 points of drone light rose over New York Harbor in a helix before assembling into the full architectural outline of the Twin Towers — one light per person killed, which shifts the unit of remembrance from two towers to 2,977 individuals. The site itself has also reached year 25: 1, 3 and 4 WTC, the transit hub, the memorial, St Nicholas and the Perelman Performing Arts Center are all in use; 2 WTC broke ground again this summer; 5 WTC is on pause over costs. Materials offer a different answer entirely — Petra Heights has completed in north London, a ten-storey block carried by a load-bearing stone exoskeleton of 596 Mount Etna basalt columns and lintels, with CLT floors spanning between them and no steel reinforcement anywhere. Also today: the Buenos Aires Architecture Biennial turns 40, and Montana State opened its first mass timber building yesterday.

MATERIALS
Petra Heights completes in London: a ten-storey block carried by 596 basalt columns and lintels, with no steel reinforcement
Sources: Dezeen · The Architects' Journal · Stone Specialist · Building Design
Petra Heights, at 317 Finchley Road in Hampstead, north London, is by Groupwork (lead architect Amin Taha) with Webb Yates Engineers as structural engineer. Dezeen reported its completion on 7 September, describing it as a world-first high-rise carried by a load-bearing stone exoskeleton. The building is ten storeys and roughly 3,000 square metres, containing 22 flats plus lower-level office space. The structure is a post-and-lintel system of 596 stone columns and lintels in basalt quarried on Mount Etna in Sicily; The Architects' Journal reported roughly 725 tonnes at the start of construction, while Dezeen's completion report gives a figure of around a thousand tonnes. Floors are exposed cross-laminated timber (CLT) slabs spanning between the stone frame. Basalt was selected over limestone after fire testing at the Building Research Establishment (BRE): it resists fracture under fire conditions better, so roughly half the stone volume is required for the same performance. Taha's comparison figures are around 80 per cent less embodied carbon than a steel frame clad in stone and around 55 per cent less than a concrete structure clad in stone; cost runs roughly 15 to 25 per cent below steel or concrete, on the condition that stone is bought directly from specialist stonemasons. Planning permission for the site was granted in 2016.

Changing the unit of commemoration from two towers to 2,977 people: a building-scale work that existed for a few minutes
Cross-checked: designboom · organisers' release (PR Newswire) · DroneXL · hube magazine · Freeyork · Middletown Life

On the night of 9 September 2026, 2,977 Lights Over New York Harbor played out above the harbour for a few minutes: 2,977 individual points of light, executed as a drone formation, first rose in a helix and then assembled into the outline of the Twin Towers — rebuilt, in the organisers' words, at full architectural scale. Sky Elements produced it, with Addison Mehr as creative director and executive producer, Yan Paul Dubbelman as designer, artistic advice from Studio Drift, Shigeru Ban and Dean Maltz, and federal and city operational coordination by IDEKO.

Design concept: replacing "two" with "2,977".The decision most worth analysing here is the choice of unit. Tribute in Light, lit every 11 September since 2002, uses two vertical beams standing in for two towers — the object of remembrance is the building, and the number of people is folded inside those two shafts. This work inverts the relationship. The unit is the person, not the tower: 2,977 lights map one-to-one onto the number killed, and the towers' outline is merely a formation those points temporarily hold. Mehr's stated intention is to acknowledge individual loss within the scale of collective memory. In formal terms the outline is a result rather than an aim: the number comes first and the towers are assembled out of it, where Tribute in Light begins with the towers and substitutes light for them.

Spatial strategy: the opening helix is a citation of an unbuilt scheme.The lights do not simply stand up as two rectangles. They rise as a helix before resolving. Per the organisers, that passage has a specific source: in the 2002 Ground Zero competition, the THINK team's World Cultural Center proposed a pair of open latticework towers over the original footprints, lifting civic and cultural space into the air. It was not selected. Putting that unbuilt lattice spiral at the start of a memorial work amounts to redeveloping a rejected branch of the site's planning history — and for architecture students that is the most valuable layer of the piece: a commemorative work doing double duty as a footnote to architectural history.

Technique: the temporariness is a structural premise, not a compromise.The physics of a drone light formation determines what it can and cannot do. It can achieve three-dimensional positioning precision no fixed luminaire could approach, with every point independently addressable — which is precisely what makes one-light-per-person technically possible. The price is dependence on wind conditions and airspace clearance, and an inability to persist. The work was therefore defined as temporary from the outset rather than shortened by budget or approvals. That sets up a direct contrast with the permanent buildings on the site below: under the same sky, one register runs on decade-long negotiations over structure, façade and cost, the other holds for a few minutes yet renders the number 2,977 at a scale the eye can actually count. The music is content rather than atmosphere on the same logic: Adams's On the Transmigration of Souls was written for the victims of 9/11, while Glass and Richter supply the temporal structure.

Context and consequence: the removability of monuments is becoming a serious question.The piece was developed with input from family, survivor and responder communities, including Brenda Berkman, Tim Brown, Terry Strada, Jim Smith and Genevieve Siller — at Ground Zero that is not public relations but a necessary precondition for any design action on the site, and has been for twenty-five years. Placed in a longer lineage, the work belongs to the temporary or removable branch of contemporary memorial design: unlike a permanent monument it occupies no land, generates no maintenance obligation, and does not fix one generation's understanding into stone. Its limits are equally clear — it cannot be revisited or touched, and it depends entirely on a technical supply chain. The useful question for students is not whether this counts as architecture, but this: when a work's duration is compressed to minutes, the centre of gravity of design shifts from material and detail to timing and precise coordinates, and that is a separate skill set requiring separate training.

Urbanism & Planning URBANISM
IN DEPTH
Twenty-five years, sixteen acres, many architects: the two remaining parcels at the World Trade Center are stuck for entirely different reasons
Cross-checked: New York YIMBY · The Real Deal · The Architect's Newspaper · Gothamist · ArchDaily · 6sqft

Taking stock of the World Trade Center site on the twenty-fifth anniversary produces a ledger that has not been closed. Across sixteen acres, 1 WTC, 3 WTC, 4 WTC, 7 WTC, the transportation hub and the memorial plaza and museum are all complete and in use. Two smaller later pieces are done as well: Santiago Calatrava's St Nicholas Greek Orthodox Church and National Shrine fully opened on 6 December 2022, and REX's Perelman Performing Arts Center opened on 19 September 2023 at roughly 500 million dollars, nearly two decades from conception to opening. Two parcels remain: 2 WTC, and 5 WTC at the south end of the campus.

Planning concept: one masterplan executed by many authors.The site's planning history is the precondition for reading its present. The 2002 competition produced a set of proposals including the THINK team's World Cultural Center; in 2003 Daniel Libeskind's Memory Foundations was selected, establishing the basic framework — the original footprints preserved as memorial space, office towers around them, and a spiralling skyline formed by the tower group. Over the following two decades the individual buildings were handed out one by one: 1 WTC to SOM, 3 WTC to Rogers Stirk Harbour + Partners, 4 WTC to Fumihiko Maki, the transit hub and church to Calatrava, the memorial plaza to Michael Arad with Peter Walker, the performing arts center to REX, and 2 WTC — after changing hands more than once — back to Foster + Partners. That pattern of one masterplan, many authors, twenty-five years of phasing is the site's most direct lesson for urban design: what a masterplan can genuinely control is parcel boundaries, street network and sightlines, while formal consistency over a twenty-five-year horizon is close to unenforceable.

Spatial strategy and tenure: why 2 WTC could restart.2 WTC sat stalled at street level for nearly fifteen years before a ceremonial groundbreaking in summer 2026. The Foster + Partners scheme rises 1,226 feet across 55 storeys with about 2 million rentable square feet of office space, developed by Silverstein Properties. What moved it was not design but tenancy: American Express committed to making it their new headquarters, clearing the hardest threshold for a commercial office start, which is pre-leasing. The building is planned as fully electric, pursuing LEED certification and incorporating smart-building systems, with completion expected in 2031. That a primary tower is still under construction after twenty-five years says something specific: the pace of building at Ground Zero has never been set by the commemorative calendar, but by the leasing market.

Cost: what stalled at 5 WTC is the arithmetic, not the design.The opposite case is 5 WTC, described as "on pause" by the Port Authority in March 2026. Deputy chief Hersh Parekh told Manhattan Community Board 1 that rising material and labour costs meant the agency and its development partners had to reassess exactly what is possible on the site. The parcel had been assigned to a team of Silverstein Properties, Brookfield Properties, Omni New York and Dabar Development for a mixed-use tower with residential, office and public amenities. There is a structural difference here worth students' attention: 2 WTC is office space, and a long anchor lease transfers cost risk off the developer's balance sheet. 5 WTC is primarily housing — and housing with an affordable component in particular has a revenue side constrained by rent regulation and subsidy policy, so it cannot be marked up in step with construction costs. So on the same site, in the same round of cost inflation, the office tower restarting while the housing pauses is not coincidence; it follows from the cash-flow structure of the two asset classes. That is the arithmetic behind a decade of setbacks for the question of whether Lower Manhattan should become residential.

Context and consequence: three lessons from twenty-five years.First, commemoration and development coexisting on one site is achieved by dividing tenure and sequence rather than by coordinating form — the memorial plaza is held and operated by a non-profit, the towers by developers, and the two are stitched together at ground level. Second, the hard part is not the supertall towers but the small cultural and religious buildings: the church and the performing arts center each took around twenty years, because neither had tenants or a clear revenue model and both had to advance on donations and political will. Third, a masterplan's effective lifespan may be shorter than the construction cycle it plans for, and the document has to leave room for that. On 11 September The Architect's Newspaper published a retrospective of its own two decades of coverage of the site; the most instructive part is the schedules that were written with complete confidence at the time and have since been rewritten in full.

55 per cent of Houston renters are cost-burdened: some 293,000 households spend more than 30 per cent of income on housing
Sources: Planetizen · Houston Chronicle (original analysis)
An analysis of 2024 data puts Houston second among the ten largest US cities for the share of cost-burdened renters: roughly 293,000 renter households spend more than 30 per cent of income on housing, or 55 per cent of those whose spending rates were calculated. The figure matters to architecture and planning students because it severs a common inference. Houston has the loosest land-use regulation of any large American city — it has no conventional zoning ordinance — and has long been treated as the test case for the claim that liberalising supply resolves affordability. A 55 per cent burden rate shows that loose supply does not automatically translate into affordability. Planetizen notes in the same period that roughly 33 states have passed laws liberalising zoning to allow more density in single-family neighbourhoods, with thousands of communities re-legalising mixed-use development — policies whose real effect has to be tested against burden rates rather than housing starts.

Materials & Technology MATERIALS
IN DEPTH
Stone only fails in compression, so it gets no rebar: the 596 basalt members of Petra Heights, and a lesson in how embodied carbon is actually counted
Cross-checked: Dezeen · The Architects' Journal · Stone Specialist · Building Design · Building · Ateliers Roméo (stone contractor)

Petra Heights stands at 317 Finchley Road in Hampstead, north London: ten storeys, roughly 3,000 square metres, 22 flats plus lower-level office space, by Groupwork (lead architect Amin Taha) with Webb Yates Engineers as structural engineer. Dezeen reported its completion on 7 September, positioning it in the headline as the world's first high-rise carried by a stone exoskeleton. Taha's own formulation is more specific: the first ten-storey, load-bearing, self-finished stone structure requiring neither stainless steel reinforcing threads nor secondary structure since the last of the stone cathedrals.

Structural logic: post-and-lintel returns because it is correct for stone.The exoskeleton comprises 596 stone columns and lintels in a straightforward post-and-lintel system. That is not a formal preference but a consequence of material mechanics: stone is strong in compression and weak in tension, so as long as members carry almost nothing but compression, they need no reinforcement. In modern construction stone is almost always demoted to cladding, hung outside a steel or concrete primary structure — a method that requires stainless steel fixings and a backing structure, converting the stone's weight into pure load rather than load-bearing capacity. Petra Heights reverses that: the stone carries again, and reinforcement is eliminated entirely. The knock-on benefits of eliminating rebar are often overlooked. With no embedded steel there is no corrosion-driven spalling — expansion of corroding reinforcement is among the principal durability failure paths in concrete structures — and because members are connected dry, at end of life the stone components can be taken down and reused rather than becoming concrete rubble.

Material selection: basalt was chosen because it passed the fire test.The original candidate stone was limestone; the final choice is basalt quarried on Mount Etna in Sicily. The basis was fire testing at the Building Research Establishment (BRE): basalt resists fracture better under fire conditions, so roughly half the stone volume achieves the same fire and load performance. That is a conclusion specific enough to write into a construction detail — fire is the first regulatory threshold any load-bearing stone structure meets, and UK requirements for residential external wall materials have kept tightening since the Grenfell Tower fire. Reported quantities differ between sources: The Architects' Journal gave roughly 725 tonnes at the start of construction, while Dezeen's completion report gives around a thousand tonnes. We keep both figures with their sources rather than merging them. Floors are exposed cross-laminated timber (CLT) slabs spanning between the stone frame — stone in compression, timber in span, two low-carbon materials each doing what it is good at. At the level of the structural system, that is the cleanest thing about this project.

How to read the numbers: 80, 55 and 15–25 per cent each come with conditions.Taha's embodied carbon comparisons are around 80 per cent less than a steel frame clad in stone and around 55 per cent less than a concrete structure clad in stone. The comparison object deserves attention: not steel or concrete as such, but steel and concrete with stone hung on them. The saving therefore contains two components at once — the substitution of primary structural material, and the elimination of the secondary structure and stainless fixings that existed only in order to hold the stone up. On cost, the reported figure is roughly 15 to 25 per cent below steel or concrete, but with a hard condition attached: stone must be bought directly from specialist stonemasons, bypassing the successive mark-ups of the conventional supply chain. In practice that condition is often decisive — it means the method currently depends on an architect capable of organising material procurement directly, rather than being a technique that can be dropped into a standard tender process.

Context and consequence: this line of work is a decade old.Groupwork and Webb Yates's structural stone research is not a one-off. In 2020 the two published a proposal for a 30-storey stone skyscraper; they subsequently built a physical structural stone demonstrator with Arup under the Design Museum's Future Observatory programme. The UK professional press has been asking this year whether 2026 marks the start of a new stone age. What Petra Heights contributes is moving the line of work from a demonstrator to an inhabited, completed ten-storey building — clearing planning (permitted in 2016), fire testing and cost appraisal simultaneously, which are precisely the three points where structural stone has previously run aground. The thing for students to retain is not the ancient commonplace that stone can carry load, but this: making a technique usable again usually requires no new invention, only getting the fire test report, the procurement route and the carbon accounting boundary sorted out at the same time.

Sublime Systems' first commercial plant starts up this year: 30,000 tonnes a year of electrochemical cement, no kiln, no process CO₂
Sources: Sublime Systems · Engineering News-Record · CemNet (International Cement Review) · Concrete Products · Holcim
Sublime Systems' first commercial cement plant, in Holyoke, Massachusetts, is scheduled to come on stream in 2026 with a design capacity of 30,000 tonnes per year. The process originates in research at MIT: an electrochemical route breaks feedstocks down into constituent elements and reassembles them into cement, bypassing the high-temperature calcination that conventional cement requires. Since fuel combustion and limestone decomposition account for the bulk of the industry's emissions, eliminating the kiln removes both at once. The company positions the output as a fossil-fuel-free, drop-in replacement for traditional cement, requiring no change to concrete mix designs or site practice. On financing, building materials majors CRH and Holcim have invested a combined 75 million dollars, and the project is eligible for up to 86.9 million dollars in matching funds from the US Department of Energy's Office of Clean Energy Demonstrations. Thirty thousand tonnes is negligible against global cement output; the significance is pushing the process from pilot to commercial scale and producing real cost data.
Biochar concrete goes carbon negative: Holcim UK and Canary Wharf Group report a net GWP of −14 kg CO₂e per cubic metre
Sources: Holcim UK · Canary Wharf Group · Agg-Net · ESG News
A concrete mix developed by Holcim's UK business with Canary Wharf Group in London reports a global warming potential (GWP, stages A1–A3) of −14 kg CO₂e per cubic metre once biochar is added to the mix, making it a sink rather than a source within that accounting boundary. The biochar is pyrolysed from forestry residues and used coffee grounds; the mechanism locks carbon fixed during the growth of trees and coffee plants into the concrete in stable form, which nets out against fossil-source emissions. The companies also report reductions of up to 80 per cent against conventional CEM I concrete. One caveat deserves emphasis: A1–A3 covers only raw material extraction through the factory gate, excluding construction, use and end-of-life stages. Claims of "net zero" or "carbon negative" stand or fall entirely on the accounting boundary, so read the stage notation in the brackets first.
LC3 limestone calcined clay cement: up to around 40 per cent lower carbon intensity, up to 25 per cent cheaper to produce
Sources: Holcim · World Resources Institute · VINCI
LC3 — limestone calcined clay cement — replaces a large share of clinker with calcined clay and limestone, and is currently among the low-carbon cement routes closest to large-scale deployment. Manufacturers and researchers give figures of up to around 40 per cent lower carbon intensity and production costs up to 25 per cent lower. Its competitive advantage is not the largest reduction but the lowest threshold: calcining clay requires far lower temperatures than clinker formation, so existing kilns can be converted rather than replaced, and clay is widely distributed, which matters for an industry whose product cannot travel far economically. Electrochemical cement and carbon capture routes have higher ceilings on emissions reduction but both require entirely new capital. Three routes running in parallel is the real state of cement decarbonisation, rather than one displacing the others.

Forty years, twenty editions, no ticket: Buenos Aires treats HABITAR as a question to be answered, not a theme word to be worn
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · La Bienal (organisers) · ArchDaily en Español · Nueva Rioja · The Rio Times · The Pritzker Architecture Prize

The Bienal Internacional de Arquitectura de Buenos Aires reaches its twentieth edition this year, forty years after it was founded. This edition opens on 22 September and runs five weeks across September and October, with every exhibition and talk free of charge. The programme comes to around 100 practices, 15 exhibitions, more than 50 talks, 40 international guests and representatives from over 55 cities. The theme is one word: HABITAR, to inhabit.

Framing the question: writing a theme that can actually be answered.The commonest way a biennial theme fails is being broad enough that any work can be labelled with it. HABITAR is handled by attaching four qualifying dimensions — cultural, social, historical and technological — and asking how those four have repeatedly redefined the relationship between people, architecture and their territorial conditions across different eras. That qualification turns inhabiting from an adjectival state into a historical problem: not "what is good dwelling" but "under the technical and social conditions of a given period, how was the definition of dwelling rewritten". The central exhibition was assembled through an open call, which means the answers are not supplied by a curated list of invitees but filtered from submissions — for a fortieth-anniversary edition, that is a decision to let the present take precedence over retrospection.

Spatial strategy: the choice of venues is itself a reading of the city.This edition returns to two sites from the biennial's own history — the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, carrying the central exhibition, and the Centro Cultural Recoleta — and adds the Biblioteca Nacional, the Museo Nacional de Arte Decorativo, the architecture museum MARQ and Casa Victoria Ocampo. These institutions cluster along a single cultural corridor within walking distance, and the Biblioteca Nacional is among the landmark works of Brutalism by Clorindo Testa and his collaborators. The visiting route is therefore a physical thread through twentieth-century Argentine architecture rather than a set of neutral galleries. In a biennial themed on inhabiting, putting the exhibitions inside these buildings rather than renting a neutral hall can be read as part of the argument.

Mechanism: what free admission does to a biennial's readership.Making an entire edition free is a minority practice among international architecture biennials — ticket revenue is one of the main income streams at Venice, whose audience is consequently weighted towards professionals and international visitors. Free admission changes who the exhibition is addressing: local non-specialist publics become the primary audience, and the language of the exhibition has to adjust accordingly. That is consistent with the theme, since inhabiting is a subject everyone has direct experience of rather than a technical question only architects can discuss. There is one item aimed squarely at professional visitors: Smiljan Radić, the Chilean architect awarded the 2026 Pritzker Prize, leads a six-day international forum from 5 to 10 October with more than 50 talks, closing with the presentation of this edition's awards. Argentine artist Marta Minujín receives the lifetime achievement award. Among the themed shows, Habitar lo Extremo on Antarctica and Habitar la Naturaleza push inhabiting towards extreme environments and natural conditions respectively, while the touring 2026 EU Mies van der Rohe Awards exhibition supplies a European reference point.

Context and consequence: a form of self-organisation in South American architectural institutions.The biennial was founded by Jorge Glusberg in 1985, just after Argentina emerged from military rule and restored elected government. Forty years and twenty editions means a roughly biennial rhythm has largely held through Argentina's repeated economic crises, which is an organisational achievement in itself. For Chinese architecture students there are three practical uses in this edition: the central exhibition comes from an open call, so the submission route is available from outside; the forum and talks are free and feature a speaker of Radić's standing; and the EU Mies touring show puts drawings and models of Europe's award-winning projects in front of a South American audience. Worth more, though, is the comparative view it offers. In South America the question of inhabiting has long been constituted by informal settlement, self-built housing and land tenure — a completely different framing from Europe's, which centres on energy use and retrofitting existing stock. Reading the two framings together gets closer to the real map of the discipline than reading either alone.
