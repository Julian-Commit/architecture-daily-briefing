EDITOR'S NOTE

Today is World Architecture Day and World Habitat Day at once. The International Union of Architects has set this year's theme as “Housing is a Human Right. Build It.”; UN-Habitat's is “Adequate Housing for All”, with the global observance opening today in Muscat for four days and launching Urban October. The numbers are not in dispute: more than 3 billion people lack adequate housing and more than 1.1 billion live in informal settlements. Two days ago tens of thousands marched in some 50 Spanish cities, after parliament voted down two housing decrees on 2 October. Read the observance and the street together and today's question is not whether to build, but who gets to decide what is built and for whom. Three working leads also land today: a 200-year-old building in Kuldīga turned boutique hotel, a primary school in Esch that breaks a sealed block into four volumes around a courtyard, and the data centre — $800 billion a year of construction, and the one type architects have largely sat out.

Parliament voted down two housing decrees on 2 October; the next day tens of thousands marched in some 50 Spanish cities, with rubber bullets and tear gas in Valencia — the spark was the eviction of an 87-year-old pensioner
Sources: Al Jazeera (3 October, photograph Reuters) · Spanish Congress vote of 2 October · Tenants’ Union, Valeria Racu, on the record · Popular Party leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo, on the record · Spanish press reporting on the prime minister’s next move
On 2 October the Spanish Congress rejected two affordable housing decrees proposed by Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez’s left-wing government. Voting them down alongside the Popular Party (PP) and Vox was Junts, the right-wing Catalan separatist party, whose stated reason was that the measures would tighten housing markets further. The following day, 3 October, protests ran simultaneously in roughly 50 cities with tens of thousands taking part; Madrid saw the largest rallies, and in Valencia police deployed rubber bullets and tear gas. The trigger for this round of mobilisation was the eviction of an 87-year-old pensioner a week earlier. Valeria Racu of the Tenants’ Union said Saturday’s rallies showed “a new energy that we haven’t seen in a long time, and energy that is showing that eight days of people’s power can achieve more than eight years of progressive government.” Protesters called for affordable housing reform and general strikes; one placard read “Houses to live in, planet to inhabit.” Opposition leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo used the moment to demand immediate elections, arguing Sánchez has lost both parliamentary and public support; Spanish media reported that Sánchez would assess the intensity of the protests before deciding his course. Read against today’s two observances the contrast is plain: housing is being written up as a human right at the advocacy level in the same week a parliament struck down the specific instruments. A building more than 200 years old in the historic centre of Kuldīga becomes a 2,000-square-metre boutique hotel: Field Studio layers restoration, local crafts and “a new layer of design” onto the existing structure, in an old town inscribed as World Heritage only in 2023
Sources: ArchDaily (5 October, photography Daniel Kochkin) · Field Studio (lead architects Jānis Andersons, Danna Degle, Diāna Zalāne) · General contractor SIA KORP · Engineering and consulting SIA JC Grupa · UNESCO World Heritage List ref. 1658, Old town of Kuldīga (inscribed 2023)
The project sits in Kuldīga in western Latvia, designed by the local practice Field Studio, 2,000 square metres, completed in 2026, with SIA KORP as general contractor, SIA JC Grupa on engineering and consulting, and photography by Daniel Kochkin. The architects’ own account of the strategy is direct: the Ausma Hotel reuses a building more than 200 years old in the historic centre of Kuldīga as a contemporary boutique hotel, continuing the story of the existing building through restoration, local crafts and a new layer of design — note that the term is a layer added, not a restoration completed or a rewriting. Timber features throughout the interior, with furnishings from Ferm Living, Finnish Design Shop and HK Living. The context worth adding is the status of the site itself: the Old town of Kuldīga was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2023 (ref. 1658), on the grounds that it is an exceptionally well-preserved example of a traditional urban settlement that grew from a small medieval hamlet into an important administrative centre of the Duchy of Courland and Semigallia between the 16th and 18th centuries. So this is not an ordinary conversion of an old house but commercial reuse inside a World Heritage property — in which regulatory context “a new layer” carries a specific meaning. The structural strengthening method, room count and cost have not been published; this paper does not infer them.

Put the garden straight onto the roof, then sink the living room 500 mm below grade: Tato Architects’ timber house in Mibu, Tochigi, irrigates its roof automatically from a well already on site
Sources: designboom (4 October, photography DAICI ANO) · Tato Architects (lead architect Yo Shimada) · Structural engineer Takashi Manda, tmsd · Dezeen (September, on Tato’s skinny Kyoto house)
The house is in Mibu, Tochigi Prefecture, Japan, designed by Kobe-based Tato Architects (lead architect Yo Shimada, with Nobuhiko Sato and Yohei Uchino), structure by Takashi Manda of tmsd, photographed by DAICI ANO and published by designboom on 4 October. The occupants are a couple with three children. It is a two-storey timber structure, and both of its decisive moves concern level. First, the living floor sits roughly 500 mm below grade — seated in the half-sunken living room, your eye line runs along the surface of the garden. Second, the roof is a planted roof, watered by an automatic irrigation system drawing on a well already present on the site, which in effect folds the garden’s area up from the ground onto the roof. The house is also rotated at an angle, both to avoid cutting off the openness of the garden and to keep the existing vehicular circulation usable. The material list is notably restrained: charred cedar (yakisugi) cladding outside, corrugated galvanised steel roofing, lauan plywood and troweled mortar floors inside. The upper floor holds a set of small interconnected rooms plus a loft raised 690 mm above the floor. Floor area and cost were not disclosed.

ACADEMIA
An “engawa” of steel and salvaged billboard tarpaulin travels from Accra to upstate New York: TAELON7’s Limbo Engawa opened at the Limbo Museum in Accra on 12 March and landed on 19 September in Art Omi’s 120-acre sculpture and architecture park
Sources: ArchDaily (5 October) · Art Omi (joint commissioner) · Limbo Museum, Accra (joint commissioner) · Austrian Cultural Forum in Accra (support) · TAELON7, led by architect Juergen Benson-Strohmayer
The installation is called Limbo Engawa, by the practice TAELON7, led by architect Juergen Benson-Strohmayer. The name comes from the Japanese engawa — the transitional zone in a traditional Japanese house between interior and exterior, and equally between private and communal space. The brief the practice set itself: how can spaces for gathering and exchange respond to different landscapes, climates and cultural contexts? The answer is a modular, lightweight structure in steel roofed with salvaged billboard tarpaulin — a material choice that carries the everyday Ghanaian practice of reusing advertising tarps. On 12 March 2026 it first appeared at the Limbo Museum in Accra, where its role was to mediate between an unfinished concrete building and the surrounding grounds, fitted out with oversized daybeds whose prototype is the woven beds people set up inside unfinished structures. On 19 September 2026 the same components were reassembled in Ghent, New York, in Art Omi’s Sculpture & Architecture Park — this time attached to no building at all, working as a freestanding landscape pavilion providing shade and gathering points across a 120-acre park. It is a joint commission between Art Omi and the Limbo Museum, with support from the Austrian Cultural Forum in Accra. For students the interest here is that one kit of parts took on two different roles under two entirely different site conditions: portability is not a technical gimmick in this project, it is the brief.

Win the urban design first, then do the building: JSWD broke an impassable perimeter block in Esch into four volumes around a courtyard, then stitched the new work back to the historic school with first-floor bridges
Cross-checked against: gooood (5 October) · JSWD project page · v2com press kit 6515-05 · Education Snapshots (September) · e-architect (August) · Collaborating practice Jim Clemes Associates, Esch

Concept: the brief began as urban design, not as a school. The project is in the Quartier Brouch district of Esch-sur-Alzette, Luxembourg’s second city, by JSWD of Cologne in cooperation with Jim Clemes Associates of Esch. JSWD first won first prize in the urban design competition for the district; only afterwards was the practice separately commissioned by the city of Esch to handle the refurbishment of the historic school building plus three new replacement buildings — École Brouch. That sequence sets the character of the scheme: the previously impassable perimeter block was broken into three building ensembles, each enclosing a green courtyard, with a car-free public pathway running between them through the site. In other words, what the design addresses first is right of way and public access; the school is one parcel within that. gooood headlined the project as organising old and new through a “school village” — and “village” here is not a formal metaphor but the consequence of re-subdividing a block that had been sealed shut.

Spatial strategy: give the courtyard away rather than enclose it. In the final layout four volumes enclose a nearly square courtyard. The decisive move is at ground level: generous openings between the volumes ensure visual permeability, so the courtyard stays visible and accessible from all four sides — the most direct possible inversion of a closed block. On the first floor, bridges link all the building parts, with glazed bridges connecting the new construction back to the historic main building. Ground level is therefore tuned for openness and porosity, the first floor for continuity and efficiency; the two circulation systems are resolved on separate levels and do not interfere. Internally, space is organised in three grades — open, semi-open and enclosed — which the architects justify as adapting to changing contemporary pedagogy without major structural change. For a building type whose life far exceeds any single round of curriculum reform, that puts adaptability into the plan rather than into the services.

Materials and construction: the facade defers to what is already there; new materials appear inside. The new buildings use brick facades coordinated with the existing plaster and stone finishes on site — in a courtyard that has to hold a historic main building and three new ones at once, this is the cheapest available means of making four volumes read as a single ensemble. Inside it is another palette: light beige terrazzo floors, exposed concrete soffits, oak fittings and anodised aluminium window frames, with watercolour works on the walls using the regional kingfisher as a motif. Environmentally, the window systems are climate-adaptive, managing airflow by season, and rooftop photovoltaic panels support on-site generation. Gross floor area is approximately 11,000 square metres. On timing, gooood gives 2025/2027: the first phase is complete and in operation, comprising the three-storey Building C to the south and the two-storey Building D to the west, with the remainder phased.

Context and consequence: the school as public infrastructure, written into the land rather than the elevation. Esch sits in Luxembourg’s former steel belt in the south of the country and has been working through the structural transition of an industrial town; public projects embedded in existing blocks are the main vehicle for that transition. For architecture students, what is worth remembering here is not the form but the causality between three facts: the competition was for urban design (which is why the practice had the standing to re-subdivide a block); the car-free path crosses school land (so “openness” is a matter of tenure and management, not massing); and the first-floor bridges keep dispersed volumes operable as one school (so the “village” does not cost administrative efficiency). One caveat — opening a campus to the city carries real security and management costs, and nothing published sets out how Esch handles time-based access control, nor the construction cost or the completion dates of the later phases. This paper does not infer them.

Populous reveals the design for Vietnam’s new national stadium: 70,000 seats, the first venue of its size in Southeast Asia with a fully retractable, acoustically rated roof, its diamond-patterned facade drawn from the nipa palms along the surrounding waterways
Sources: Dezeen weekly round-up (3 October) · Populous project page (September) · The Stadium Business (24 September) · Populous release on the 15 January groundbreaking · stadiumdb.com
The Rach Chiec National Sports Complex sits on the outskirts of Ho Chi Minh City across 186.78 hectares, developed by Vietnam’s Sun Group, which broke ground on 15 January 2026 with a total investment of roughly VND145,000 billion. Populous was appointed to design the 70,000-seat central stadium and an 18,000-seat arena, with the design published this week by Dezeen among others. Seventy thousand seats puts it about 30,000 above My Dinh National Stadium, Vietnam’s current largest, and close to double the existing venue. Roof and building form take their reference from the nipa palms that line the waterways around the site — the design team, led by senior principal Andrew Tulen, studied how the plants hold the riverbanks while leaving space beneath their canopy for community and trade, which resolves into a diamond-patterned facade. Technically, this is the first athletic stadium of its size in Southeast Asia with a fully retractable, acoustically rated roof: beyond giving organisers full control of conditions in the bowl, its practical function is to keep noise off neighbouring residential communities during major concerts. Zone-based air conditioning and flexible seating are also specified. The masterplan is set up to accommodate around 90% of Olympic sports, with target events including the Southeast Asian Games and the AFC Asian Cup. This paper does not forecast bid outcomes.
C.F. Møller converts an 1888 psychiatric hospital ward building into Denmark’s psychiatric museum: 1,500 square metres, with the budget spent selectively on a few decisive moves — a new main stair, new connections between rooms, and a “MINDwalk” down to the basement
Sources: Dezeen (October, this week) · C.F. Møller project page · C.F. Møller appointment announcement · VisitDenmark / VisitMiddelfart venue information
The MIND Museum is at Teglgårdsparken 17 in Middelfart on the island of Funen, Denmark, designed by the Danish practice C.F. Møller. The task was to transform one of the original 1888 buildings of a former psychiatric hospital — in operation from 1888 to 1999 — into a museum of the history of Danish psychiatry. It covers 1,500 square metres and holds the largest psychiatric history collection in Denmark, opening in May 2026. The core design judgement is to retain the building’s classical character and clear plan structure, staging its own history through colours and materials while adding entirely new elements that give a contemporary interior expression and optimise the building for museum use. What students should note is the budget strategy: funds were used selectively on a few key interventions with significant impact — a new main staircase, new connections between rooms, an internal “MINDwalk” stair from the ground floor to the basement, and a café with precise, simple detailing. In a protected historic building this “few cuts, each one counting” approach is both more common and harder than a comprehensive refurbishment: it requires deciding in advance which handful of moves will change how the whole building is used. For students working on adaptive reuse, the interesting part of this project is that the real technical difficulty is acoustics: putting a live music hall still in nightly operation and guest rooms into one building makes the sound isolation detailing of slabs and shafts the thing that decides whether the scheme works at all. Hubo Studio’s Redhill Early Learning Centre in South Africa is organised as “a mini city for children”: streets, squares and individual houses all set to children’s dimensions
Sources: gooood (4 October) · Hubo Studio project page · ArchDaily · Project photography
The project is in South Africa, by Hubo Studio, published by gooood on 4 October under the line “a mini city for children”. Its organising logic treats the nursery as a miniature settlement: instead of classrooms threaded along a corridor, space is divided using the vocabulary of a city — streets, squares, individual “houses” — and door openings, counters and sight lines are all set to children’s bodily dimensions rather than scaled down from adult ones. How do you actually build “Housing is a Human Right”? Unpacking today’s slogan into four routes: change the systems of distribution, change the form of dwelling, use the existing stock, and change the construction industry itself
Cross-checked against: ArchDaily (5 October, by Antonia Piñeiro) · UIA announcement · UN-Habitat World Habitat Day 2026 concept note and official page · Al Jazeera (3 October) · Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, The State of the Nation’s Housing 2026 (released 17 June) · Muscat Daily (29 September) / Times of Oman

Concept: turning a declaration into a buildable question. The part of the UIA’s theme worth pressing on is the imperative at the end. Writing housing up as a human right is a statement in law; “Build It.” hands the problem back to architecture and demands answers about who builds, with what, and who owns the result. ArchDaily’s commentary the same day, by Antonia Piñeiro, runs along exactly that line: the housing crisis is a multidimensional problem that cannot be solved by building more alone, but also demands building better, sharing more, and making full use of what already exists. The order of magnitude in front of it: more than 3 billion people lack adequate housing, more than 1.1 billion live in informal settlements. That figure determines the character of the discussion — at this scale, the places where architecture can act are mainly institutional, legal and industrial, not compositional.

Routes one and two: fix distribution before discussing form. The first route the commentary identifies is to build systems to distribute — new legislation and shared-ownership models, with examples including the Balearic Housing Model (Cris Ballester and Carles Oliver), the Las Americas residential complex by SO-IL, and housing reforms under way in Spain, France, Australia and the United States. Putting institutions first corrects a common misreading: insufficient supply is frequently not a capacity problem but a problem of how housing is distributed and held. The second route is to build structures for alternative living, pointing to co-living and climate-resilient design, with examples including gon architects’ Dozen Doors coliving project, Marina Tabassum’s Khudi Bari for people displaced by climate, and the collective housing work of the Spanish architecture cooperative Lacol. Khudi Bari matters because it concedes a particular kind of need: when the precondition of “adequate housing” — a piece of land you can stay on — is itself unstable, housing has to be demountable and movable.

Routes three and four: use the stock, then change the industry. The third is to rebuild existing structures, placing renovation and adaptive reuse ahead of new construction, with examples including the Marquês Project by Bric Arquitectura, several post-earthquake reconstruction projects, and a European citizens’ initiative on the right to housing. This route is the critical one in today’s context: turning architecture’s attention from “the next project” to the tens of billions of square metres already standing means skill structures, forms of contract and systems of valuation all have to change with it. The fourth is to build sustainable industry practices, pointing at collective construction and material innovation — USINA in Brazil, the technical assistance body that has worked with housing movements for decades, and Cuba’s Microbrigadas programme, plus circular construction hubs in New York, London and Brussels. USINA and the Microbrigadas are both precedents for treating the organisation of building labour as a design object. Taken in order, then, the four routes point at legislation, typology, stock and labour — and not one of them is solved by shape.

Context and consequence: in the same week, the observance and the parliament voted in opposite directions. The reality check arrived fast. On 2 October the Spanish Congress rejected two affordable housing decrees; on 3 October tens of thousands marched in some 50 cities, with rubber bullets and tear gas in Valencia. Meanwhile UN-Habitat’s Global Observance runs 5 to 8 October at the Oman Convention and Exhibition Centre in Muscat, led by Executive Director Anacláudia Rossbach, with representatives of around 50 countries and some 50 specialised papers, addressing the policies, partnerships and practical solutions needed to turn the right to adequate housing into reality — and emphasising secure land tenure, well-planned and well-serviced neighbourhoods, and a wider range of models including social, public, rental, cooperative, community-led and market-based housing. The data side is equally concrete: the Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies’ State of the Nation’s Housing 2026, released on 17 June, found that as of 2024 some 11.0 million extremely low-income renter households in the United States were competing for just 3.8 million affordable and available units — a 7.2 million-unit shortfall. The same report carries a second structural figure: adjusted for inflation, rental units renting below $1,000 a month fell by more than 30% between 2014 and 2024, a net loss of about 7 million units. For students starting architecture school this year, that juxtaposition says more than any theme line: advocacy, policy and stock attrition are not moving at the same speed, and the third is moving fastest.

11.0 million extremely low-income renter households in the US are competing for 3.8 million affordable and available units: Harvard puts the shortfall at 7.2 million, and counts a net loss of about 7 million sub-$1,000 rentals over a decade
Sources: Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, The State of the Nation’s Housing 2026 (17 June) · JCHS press release · Harvard Graduate School of Design (June) · Smart Cities Dive · Novogradac
The State of the Nation’s Housing is the annual report of Harvard’s Joint Center for Housing Studies (JCHS); the 2026 edition was released on 17 June. Two figures are worth committing to memory. First, as of 2024 the United States had 11.0 million extremely low-income renter households against just 3.8 million affordable and available units — a shortfall of 7.2 million. Note that qualifier “available”: it excludes low-cost units already occupied by higher-income households, which makes this gap a better reflection of actual competition than a price count alone. Second, adjusted for inflation, rental units priced below $1,000 a month fell by more than 30% across the decade from 2014 to 2024, a net loss of roughly 7 million. The report also finds construction activity softening, cost burdens climbing for both renters and homeowners, and federal housing assistance profoundly underfunded. Set the two figures side by side and the conclusion is that low-cost stock is being lost at roughly the same order of magnitude as the shortfall is accruing — which is precisely the “make full use of what already exists” clause in today’s UIA theme.

India’s new affordable housing policy is expected within six months: developer body CREDAI wants the ₹45 lakh price cap removed and only the carpet-area criteria retained
Sources: Business Today (3 October) · CREDAI President Shekhar Patel, on the record · CREDAI-Anarock joint report · Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs public information
Shekhar Patel, president of the Indian developers’ association CREDAI, said on 3 October that the revised affordable housing framework is expected within six months. CREDAI’s central ask is to remove the current ₹45 lakh price ceiling and retain only the prescribed carpet-area criteria — 60 square metres in metropolitan areas and 90 square metres outside them. The logic is that in high-land-cost cities the price cap no longer corresponds to any buildable product, which has the effect of pushing the “affordable housing” category out of major urban centres; but removing it also means the category’s policy benefits could flow to more expensive product, and views differ on that. The accompanying CREDAI-Anarock report gives several figures: the value of residential projects under construction reached $430 billion in 2025, up from $235 billion in 2019; residential sales have stayed above ₹1.3 lakh crore for seven consecutive quarters; and the share of affordable homes in overall residential sales continues to decline. This paper does not forecast the policy outcome.

IN DEPTH
$800 billion a year of construction, and architects have barely been involved: the data centre is becoming the largest building type of our moment, and its design problems are only now being treated as design problems
Cross-checked against: Dezeen (29 September) · Dezeen weekly round-up (3 October) · Marina Otero Verzier (Columbia University, 2022 Wheelwright Prize), on the record · Gensler, Rob LoBuono · Corgan, Tom Kruger · Legacy Investing, Daniel English

Concept: a building type without architects. The starting point of Dezeen’s 29 September investigation is blunt: global data centre infrastructure spending is expected to reach $800 billion annually in 2026, and US data centre building is projected to double on AI demand — while architects have been largely absent from the trend. Marina Otero Verzier, the scholar of data centre spatial politics at Columbia University and winner of the 2022 Wheelwright Prize, puts it as: “Data centers are, in general, an example of poor design.” Her argument is not merely about appearance: if these structures are to be positive, or at least neutral, contributors to the built environment, architectural involvement is a precondition. Another figure explains the urgency — nearly 70% of the US population opposes data centre construction. Which is to say the type has already moved from being a question of technical facilities to being a political question about land, water, power and community consent — and consent is precisely what architecture has long handled.

Spatial strategy: settle massing and siting before discussing the facade. The approaches currently visible differ sharply in depth. The most superficial is facade treatment: Gensler gave the Stratos Hyperscale Data Center in Box Elder County, Utah, striated facades intended to echo the bedding of desert mountains, and the same practice uses Corten steel on its Menlo Digital cluster in Arizona to help the buildings settle into their surroundings. Gensler’s Rob LoBuono says “the architecture of the building does matter”, bearing directly on how a community perceives a project. A level beyond that is massing and siting: SOM’s work in Kansas City is a vertical data centre — a high-rise, prompted by rural zoning constraints, which in effect replaces the default sprawling shed with intensified land use. A level beyond that again is Grimshaw’s mixed-use plan positioning a data centre as a catalyst for community renewal, whose central mechanism is waste heat recovery — turning server-hall heat into a usable resource nearby, currently the only technical route that rewrites a data centre from NIMBY nuisance into infrastructure. Corgan’s Tom Kruger supplies the reality check: mixed-use integration remains “an exception and not the rule”.

Materials and stock: putting decommissioned industry and timber frames into server halls. Two cases deserve students’ attention. First, Terawulf’s conversion of a decommissioned smelter in Kentucky — fitting a data centre inside an existing heavy-industrial structure, on the logic that such plants already come with power interconnection, slabs rated for heavy equipment, and cooling water, which are precisely the three most expensive preconditions for a data centre. So this is not sentimental reuse but cost-driven adaptive reuse. Second, Microsoft’s mass timber data centres, substituting timber for some steel and concrete to reduce embodied carbon. Jan Gehl turns 90 this year, and the Danish Architecture Center set the opening for today: “Cities for People” takes his 12 quality criteria for public space and makes whether a place is bearable to stay in something you test with your body
Cross-checked against: ArchDaily (29 September) · Danish Architecture Center (DAC) exhibition page · DAC press release, Jan Gehl Turns 90 · Gehl Studio (collaborator) · Gehl, Life Between Buildings (first published 1971) · illustrarch

Concept: the opening date is itself the argument. “Cities for People” opens today, 5 October, at the Danish Architecture Center (DAC) in Copenhagen — World Architecture Day, and in the year the Danish architect and urban researcher Jan Gehl turns 90. The exhibition was developed by DAC with Gehl Studio and with Gehl himself, draws on his life’s work, and is built around his 12 quality criteria for public urban spaces, exploring how people-centred urban design can improve citizens’ health, wellbeing, communities and quality of life. Setting the opening on World Architecture Day, in a year whose theme is “Housing is a Human Right”, is more than scheduling: Gehl’s lifelong argument is that architecture has long put its attention in the wrong place — on the buildings, rather than on the space between them where life actually happens. In his own words: “A good city is one that takes people as its starting point. We are walking, sensing and social beings…”

Spatial strategy: turn assessment into experience. The exhibition design is immersive and participatory rather than a wall of text and images. The decisive move: visitors are to physically experience when a place feels safe and when it feels unsafe, and to register that the same space reads differently according to age, gender and life situation. That step restores the part of Gehl’s method most easily flattened in practice. Public space quality tends to get compressed into a few countable indicators — footfall, dwell time, number of seats — whereas the 12 criteria were always a framework about the experience of a subject. Staging it as something the visitor tests bodily is a refusal to let it become a scorecard. The closing question the curators put to visitors: can you imagine a city with room for both community and freedom, energy and calm? Those four terms are two opposed pairs, and holding all four at once is the hardest requirement in public space design.

Method and lineage: one book from 1971, and a way of looking translated into 30 languages. Gehl’s method rests on Life Between Buildings, first published in 1971 and since translated into 30 languages. Its contribution is not theoretical but observational: it takes what people actually do in public space — walking, stopping, sitting, looking, talking — as the unit of measurement, and turns it, through repeatable recording methods, into data that can enter planning decisions. The method has been tested continuously in Copenhagen, not least through the pedestrianisation of Strøget. In 2000 he founded Gehl Architects, now Gehl Studio, which made the method into exportable consulting practice. In Gehl’s own ordering, the 12 criteria are usually grouped into three sets of four — protection, comfort and delight; DAC’s published material does not enumerate them individually, and this paper will not list them on its behalf.

Context and consequence: two concrete uses for students. The accompanying AfterDAC programme carries conversations and debates, among them a discussion between Gehl and the architect Ane Cortzen. For students at Syracuse this exhibition is useful on two levels. First, method: Gehl’s observational technique requires no equipment and no budget — a pen, a plan and an afternoon will execute it on any street — which makes it the lowest-barrier site-research training available, and it can go straight into the early stages of a studio project in place of analysis built only on aerials and zoning maps. Second, position: read this exhibition against today’s UIA theme and you see two ends of one problem — housing settles whether there is anywhere to live, public space settles where life takes place once you are housed. Neither is a question of form.
