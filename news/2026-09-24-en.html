EDITOR'S NOTE

Two just-finished buildings made the news today: one burned, one opened. In Ordino, Andorra, La Querola — Jean Nouvel's €130 million, fourteen-years-in-construction residential ensemble — caught fire in the early hours of 22 September; six of its seven buildings are severely damaged and roughly 22,000 square metres burned. The units were unveiled only in June and not a single resident had moved in. In Toronto, Trinity College's Lawson Centre for Sustainability opened to the community: 14,450 square metres of hybrid mass timber, a rooftop farm feeding the dining hall downstairs, targeting both Zero Carbon Design and LEED Platinum. In Washington, the National Trust launched a $10 million Historic Preservation Defense Fund at its first Preservation Summit, with the money aimed squarely at litigation. On Kenmare Street in New York, Storefront completed its first substantial renovation in decades — and Vito Acconci's 1993 concrete letters are backlit for the first time. In Calgary, the lower nine floors of a 1982 oil-company headquarters are now an architecture school.

Fire severely damages Jean Nouvel's La Querola in Andorra: six of seven buildings hit, some 22,000 sq m burned, three months after the €130 million scheme was unveiled and with no resident yet moved in
Sources: ArchDaily (24 Sept) · La Veu Lliure (Andorra) · all-andorra · Ara (Catalonia) · FTN News
The fire at La Querola in Ordino, Andorra, broke out at roughly 1:45 a.m. on 22 September 2026, spread across the seven-building complex, and was brought under control on 23 September. Thirty-four firefighters in fourteen crews responded, drawn from Andorra and neighbouring France; no injuries were reported. According to the Andorran outlets La Veu Lliure and all-andorra, roughly 22,000 square metres were affected — about 10,000 of residential space and 12,000 of parking — with six of the seven buildings severely damaged. Building-by-building assessments are ongoing and the cause has not been established. The development was designed by Jean Nouvel with the local practice Ribas & Ribas Arquitectes and architect Jordi Sala: roughly 34,000 square metres of built area holding about 50 residences of 300 to 700 square metres each, priced between €2.4 and €6.3 million (roughly €8,000–9,000 per square metre). It took nearly two decades from first planning to completion, fourteen of them in construction, at a total investment of about €130 million. The complex was unveiled publicly on 5 June 2026, with around half the units sold — but nobody had yet moved in. Rather than a conventional apartment block, it is seven separate volumes distributed down a steep slope, finished in slate, timber and stone so as to sit into the mountainside; collaborating architect Josep Ribas has described it as "a sculpture with a function."

Mecanoo and RDHA's Lawson Centre for Sustainability opens at Trinity College, Toronto: 14,450 sq m of hybrid mass timber, 357 beds and a rooftop farm, targeting Zero Carbon Design and LEED Platinum
Sources: ArchDaily (project published 22 Sept) · Trinity College (welcome celebration, 14 Sept) · RDHA project page · Graham Construction (general contractor) · Holcim Foundation 2025 Best Practice Award
The Lawson Centre for Sustainability at the University of Toronto's Trinity College has opened to the community, with ArchDaily publishing the full project on 22 September. It is Trinity's first new building since 1979 and its most ambitious capital project in more than six decades. Designed by the Dutch practice Mecanoo with Toronto-based RDHA, the four-storey, 14,450-square-metre building is a hybrid mass timber, steel and concrete structure clad in local brick and limestone. Programmatically it is residence and academic building at once: 357 beds spanning singles, doubles, studio units and barrier-free typologies, alongside academic offices, seminar rooms, a full-service cafeteria, a teaching kitchen, student lounges and study space, and an event pavilion. The sustainability systems are deliberately stacked into a single building — hybrid mass timber, a geothermal exchange system, a cistern for rainwater collection and reuse, and a rooftop farm whose produce goes straight to the cafeteria downstairs, closing a farm-to-table loop. The project targets Canada Green Building Council (CaGBC) Zero Carbon Design certification and LEED Platinum, and almost all of its major materials — mass timber, compressed brick and limestone — are sourced within Ontario. Structural engineering is by Blackwell, MEP by Smith + Andersen, and sustainability consulting by Footprint Consulting. The design previously won a 2025 Holcim Foundation Award for Best Practice in Sustainable Design.

National Trust launches a $10 million Historic Preservation Defense Fund: announced by Brent Leggs at the first National Preservation Summit, with the money earmarked for legal defense and public advocacy
Sources: Dezeen (23 Sept) · The Architect's Newspaper (23 Sept) · National Trust release (PR Newswire) · AP / US News (23 Sept)
The National Trust for Historic Preservation announced the Historic Preservation Defense Fund — a $10 million fundraising campaign — on 23 September 2026 at the inaugural National Preservation Summit. It was announced by Brent Leggs, who became the organisation's president and CEO in June 2026 and is its first African American chief executive. In the Trust's own words, the fund will supply "resources for legal defense, public advocacy, and community action when historic places and the laws that protect them are under threat." The purpose is stated with unusual bluntness: this is money for lawsuits. The summit ran 22–24 September at Washington National Cathedral, drawing more than 500 preservation, business and policy leaders in person and online under the theme "All People Are Created Equal." The disputes the fund points at are named explicitly: the East Wing demolition and ongoing ballroom project at the White House (the Trust has already sued the federal government over it), the closure of the Kennedy Center amid renaming and renovation plans, a proposed Triumphal Arch in Washington DC described as an intrusion on historic sightlines, and moves to weaken Section 106 review under the National Historic Preservation Act. For architecture students, the significance is less architectural than institutional: America's principal private preservation body is shifting its centre of gravity from persuasion and nomination to litigation.

Storefront for Art and Architecture completes its first major renovation in decades: GRT Architects replaces the systems, backlights Acconci's 1993 concrete letters, and the gallery reopens 25 September with a new show
Sources: The Architect's Newspaper (23 Sept) · Storefront exhibition calendar · Steven Holl Architects project archive
Storefront for Art and Architecture, the non-profit at 97 Kenmare Street in New York, has completed its first substantial renovation in decades, designed by GRT Architects, a practice with offices in New York and Mexico City. As reported by The Architect's Newspaper on 23 September, the work is described as "long overdue" infrastructural upgrading: the electrical and HVAC systems were replaced outright. On the exterior, GRT designed a new door handle to make the main entry legible and easier to find, and added back-lighting behind the concrete letters designed by Vito Acconci in 1993 — the letters spell out the institution's name and had been all but illegible after dark. That facade is a fixture of architectural history in its own right: in 1993 Storefront commissioned Acconci and architect Steven Holl to remake the street frontage as a row of rotating panels of varying orientation along the length of the elevation; opened, they dissolve the boundary between gallery and sidewalk. Conceived as temporary, the installation has remained ever since and was restored once, in summer 2008. The organisation was founded in 1982 by Kyong Park with R.L. Seltman and Arleen Schloss at 51 Prince Street; the current space is a triangular ground-floor room nearly 100 feet long, tapering from 20 feet wide to 3 feet at its western end. The first exhibition in the renovated space, "The Biography of a Building: Runik's House of Culture" by the Hajde! Foundation and artist Petrit Halilaj, opens 25 September.

hcma turns the lower nine floors of a 1982 Calgary oil-company headquarters into an architecture school: 16,722 sq m, CAD$30 million, 1,200 students and faculty relocated downtown
Sources: The Architect's Newspaper (24 Sept) · Azure Magazine · hcma project page · STOREYS · The Globe and Mail
The University of Calgary's School of Architecture, Planning and Landscape (SAPL) has moved into The Valentine at 801 7 Avenue S.W. in downtown Calgary, with interiors by Vancouver practice hcma architecture + design; The Architect's Newspaper published the full project on 24 September. The host building is a 37-storey tower designed by Fred Valentine and completed in 1982 as the headquarters of the oil and gas company NOVA Corporation. The school occupies the lower nine floors plus a three-storey annex — 16,722 square metres in total, at a cost of roughly CAD$30 million — and came into use in January 2026, bringing about 1,200 students and faculty downtown from the main campus. The program comprises eight floors of design studios, classrooms and research labs plus a three-storey advanced fabrication workshop. hcma's central move is a split-floor strategy: each floor is halved between colour-coded, classroom-oriented zones and neutral polished-concrete open studio areas. Select floors are painted in UCalgary red and gold so the building reads from the street at night. A mobile pin-up board armature and a steel canopy were added around the concrete core to organise circulation; workstations sit at 45 degrees, aligned to the building's geometry; the tower's existing facade notches are clad in hot-rolled steel to sharpen their points; freestanding classrooms in circular and orthogonal geometries land as coloured volumes within the open plan; timber-framed critique nooks let students install their own temporary cladding; and window-mounted shelving displays models visible from downtown. The context: Calgary's downtown office vacancy hovers near 30 percent, and the city's Downtown Post-Secondary Institution Incentive Program has, per public figures, already converted 2.68 million square feet of office space citywide.

In Depth
A "sculpture with a function" burns: La Querola's fourteen-year build, seven volumes, and the gap between handover and occupancy
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (24 Sept) · La Veu Lliure (Andorra, 23 Sept) · all-andorra (22 Sept) · Ara (Catalonia, 22 Sept) · FTN News · Lugares de Nieve (Spain)

Concept: not one building, but seven. La Querola sits high on a steep slope at Ordino, in northern Andorra. The founding decision by Jean Nouvel, working with the local practice Ribas & Ribas Arquitectes and architect Jordi Sala, was to refuse to compress 34,000 square metres into a single mass. The scheme breaks it into seven separate buildings distributed across the terrain, letting the architecture follow the ground rather than flattening it. Collaborating architect Josep Ribas summarised the approach as "a sculpture with a function." In luxury Alpine and Pyrenean development this is not the obvious choice: dispersal means far more envelope, more complicated foundations and access roads, and a higher cost per square metre. What it buys is independent orientation and outlook for every residence, and — seen from the valley — no single overbearing silhouette.

Spatial strategy: fifty homes, 300 to 700 square metres each. Per La Veu Lliure, the seven buildings hold roughly 50 residences of 300 to 700 square metres, priced between €2.4 and €6.3 million, or about €8,000–9,000 per square metre. The combination of scale and density is worth pausing on: a 300-square-metre floor is the entry unit, which means very few dwellings per building, circulation cores and shared corridors reduced to a minimum — closer to stacked courtyard houses than to apartments. The parking, at 12,000 square metres, exceeds the 10,000 square metres of residential space. On a slope this steep, that substructure typically also retains earth, ties together different datums and absorbs the building services; it is the single hardest element to control for cost and programme, and it goes some way to explaining a fourteen-year construction period.

Structure and materials: slate, timber and stone, set into the mountain. According to La Veu Lliure, the three architects used slate, timber and stone to integrate the buildings into the mountainside — a direct extension of Pyrenean vernacular construction, where slate roofs and stone walling are long-standing practice in Andorra and Catalonia. Nouvel's move is to scale that language up and set it against large expanses of glass. One caveat must be stated plainly: as of publication, no public source has disclosed the project's structural system (timber, reinforced concrete or hybrid), its insulation build-up, or its fire compartmentation strategy, and the point of origin and spread path of the fire have not been released. This briefing offers no technical inference as to why the fire moved between seven detached buildings. That is precisely what an investigation has to answer, not something derivable from a materials list.

Context and consequence: three months from unveiling to fire. The timeline is worth setting out in full. Nearly two decades from first planning to completion, fourteen of them in construction; total investment about €130 million; public unveiling on 5 June 2026 with around half the units sold; ignition at roughly 1:45 a.m. on 22 September; the fire under control on 23 September; 34 firefighters in 14 crews, Andorran and French, no injuries; roughly 22,000 square metres affected and six of seven buildings severely damaged. The single most consequential fact is that nobody had ever moved in. The buildings were in a particular condition — physically complete, legally handed over, not yet in use. For students, that interval is worth committing to memory: fire safety is not only a design and sign-off question. Security, temporary power, fit-out work and the allocation of insurance liability during a vacancy all carry risk, and that phase appears almost nowhere in a school curriculum. The plainer lesson is this: a project that took twenty years and €130 million can change character in a few hours. We will follow the Andorran investigation and will not repeat any unconfirmed account of the cause before it reports.

Infinite Grove travelling pavilion, Shanghai / Chang Hao Studio: two curved mirrored-steel walls multiply one living tree into a forest, and the whole thing fits on a single truck with no foundations
Sources: designboom (23 Sept) · Chang Hao Studio (design team: Hao Chang, Fan Liu, Chao Li)
Infinite Grove, by Chang Hao Studio, first appeared at the Shanghai International Flower Festival from 18 April to 10 May 2026, with its main venue in Xintiandi, Huangpu district, and has since travelled to several other Shanghai locations. The components are minimal: two curved mirrored stainless-steel walls, a set of frosted panels and one living tree. The mirrors reflect the single tree repeatedly, making the interior read as far larger than its physical dimensions; the frosted panels diffuse daylight until the real tree is hard to distinguish from its reflections; a circular aperture reveals the interior gradually. The studio cites two devices from the classical Chinese garden — framed view (kuangjing) and borrowed scenery (jiejing) — as its operational basis. The tree changes with the season: a flowering azalea in spring, an olive tree in summer. The construction logic is the part worth noting: every component is factory-prefabricated, the pavilion needs no foundations on site, and it can be transported on a single truck.
MPNYC office and showroom, Piraeus, Greece / Manhattan Projects New York City: a 150-year-old port warehouse reworked by subtraction, with the suspended ceiling stripped to expose the fir roof
Sources: designboom (24 Sept) · MPNYC · photography by Yorgos Kaplanidis
The project occupies a roughly 150-year-old building on Polydefkous Street in Piraeus, Greece, which has served in turn as port storage, cargo workshops and a film production facility; it opens in October as a combined office, showroom and gallery. Manhattan Projects New York City worked by subtraction: stripping accumulated layers of fit-out, restoring original doorways and timber shutters, removing the suspended ceiling to reveal the fir roof, and leaving masonry and concrete structure exposed throughout. Additions are held to a handful of moves — reclaimed brick for partitions and thresholds, minimal steel pivot doors, liquid-metal insulation finishes. The team describes the interior as a "slider," where rooms shift between functions according to the needs of the people using them: kitchens become workshops or pop-up dining, meeting areas convert to galleries or project space. The stated position is to let "the architecture remain part of the display" rather than adopt the neutral backdrop a showroom usually assumes.
Two early-20th-century apartments in Bovisa, Milan / co.arch studio + Alessandra Negri: two ways of living inside one historic shell
Sources: gooood (published 24 Sept) · co.arch studio
The project sits in Bovisa, north of central Milan — formerly the city's industrial hinterland, and in recent years repopulated with students and young families following the expansion of the Politecnico di Milano's Bovisa campus. co.arch studio, working with Alessandra Negri, renovated two apartments within the same early-twentieth-century building; gooood published the project on 24 September under the theme "two apartments, two ways of living." The difficulty in this kind of brief is structural: in a historic residential building, load-bearing walls and vertical service risers are essentially fixed, so the available room to manoeuvre concentrates in three operations — reorganising partitions, consolidating storage, and redistributing light. Readers interested in housing retrofit should compare the two schemes along those three lines. In Depth
Turning an emptied oil headquarters into an architecture school: Calgary bets CAD$30 million and 1,200 people on a more realistic exit for office towers than housing
Cross-checked: The Architect's Newspaper (24 Sept) · Azure Magazine · hcma project page · STOREYS · The Globe and Mail · City of Calgary incentive-program materials

Concept: office-to-residential is hard, so convert to a school instead. For five years the North American conversation about surplus office space has been dominated by office-to-residential conversion — and the built record lags far behind the discourse. Excessive floor depth, off-centre cores, and the need for a bathroom and kitchen riser per dwelling are enough, between them, to make most 1970s–1990s office towers uneconomic to convert. Calgary has taken a different route: convert to post-secondary use. Teaching space tolerates depth far better than housing does, daylight requirements can be met by zoning the plan, and riser demand is far lower. The University of Calgary's School of Architecture, Planning and Landscape moved into The Valentine at 801 7 Avenue S.W. — a 37-storey tower by Fred Valentine, completed in 1982 as NOVA Corporation's headquarters — taking the lower nine floors plus a three-storey annex, 16,722 square metres in all, for roughly CAD$30 million, occupied from January 2026.

Spatial strategy: cut each floor in half, colour one side, leave the other blank. hcma's central device is the split floor: each level is divided between a colour-coded, classroom-oriented zone keyed to the university's visual system, and an untreated open studio area on polished concrete. The split answers the basic spatial contradiction of an architecture school — studios need long-term occupation, free modification and a neutral background; classrooms need clear edges, legibility and timetabled turnover. The freestanding classrooms are built as coloured volumes in circular and orthogonal geometries, dropped into the open plan like furniture rather than chopping the floorplate up with partitions. Workstations are set at 45 degrees, aligned to the building's geometry, which breaks the uniformity of the standard gridded studio. A mobile pin-up board armature and a steel canopy were added around the concrete core, releasing the crit from dedicated rooms into the circulation — an increasingly common judgement in architecture school design: the review is not an event, it is the everyday.

Structure and materials: don't touch the structure; work the surfaces and interfaces. Almost nothing structural changed. Every intervention sits at the level of surface and interface: the tower's existing facade notches are clad in hot-rolled steel to produce sharp points; polished concrete as studio flooring keeps the office slab's texture and durability; the timber-framed critique nooks are designed as armatures onto which students can install their own temporary cladding, which makes that surface itself a piece of teaching material; window-mounted shelving puts student models on show to the street. Select floors painted in UCalgary red and gold let the building be read from the street at night — a very cheap move with a disproportionate urban effect, turning a tower that used to go dark at 6 p.m. into a facade with content in it.

Context and consequence: one municipal policy, 2.68 million square feet. Downtown Calgary's office vacancy has sat near 30 percent for years, among the worst of any major North American city, driven by contraction in oil and gas and by changes in how offices are used. The city's Downtown Post-Secondary Institution Incentive Program subsidises exactly this kind of conversion and has, per public figures, already moved 2.68 million square feet of office space to other uses. SAPL's relocation brings roughly 1,200 students and faculty downtown — not just rent, but a whole daily rhythm, since an architecture school's hours guarantee the building is occupied at night and on weekends. For planning students the value of the case is that it reduces adaptive reuse from an architectural question to a combinatorial one: available stock, an institution willing to move, and public money to close the gap — all three, or it does not happen. The open question is replicability. Not every vacant office tower has an expanding university next door, and there is a finite number of faculties any one city can move downtown.

In Depth
Every sustainability system in one building: the Lawson Centre's hybrid mass timber, geothermal exchange, rainwater cistern and rooftop farm — and a supply chain almost entirely inside Ontario
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (project published 22 Sept) · Trinity College (welcome celebration, 14 Sept) · Mecanoo project materials · RDHA project page · Graham Construction · Holcim Foundation 2025 awards announcement

Concept: one building that is residence, academic block and farm at once. The Lawson Centre for Sustainability at the University of Toronto's Trinity College is the college's first new building since 1979 and its largest capital commitment in more than six decades. The premise is not "a green building" but the compression into a single section of functions usually scattered across a campus and unrelated to one another: a 357-bed student residence, academic offices and seminar rooms, a full-service cafeteria and teaching kitchen, an event pavilion, and a rooftop farm. The farm's produce goes directly to the cafeteria below, closing a farm-to-table loop. What that combination achieves is to make sustainability something a student encounters daily — not a certification plaque in the lobby, but the fact that today's lunch came from the roof. Mecanoo led the design with Toronto's RDHA; the building is four storeys and 14,450 square metres.

Spatial strategy: a complete set of room types, with accessibility built in rather than bolted on. The 357 beds span singles, doubles, studio units and barrier-free typologies — treating barrier-free accommodation as a parallel room type rather than a small quota added afterwards is a subtle but meaningful distinction in student housing. The teaching kitchen sits alongside the full-service cafeteria, making food service both a service and a teaching setting; the lounges, study spaces and event pavilion carry the social functions of Trinity's collegiate tradition. The configuration answers a real condition of contemporary student housing: when much of a student's working time happens in the residence rather than the library, the residence has to acquire the spatial hierarchy of an academic building.

Structure and materials: hybrid mass timber, plus a provincial supply chain. The structure is hybrid mass timber, steel and concrete, clad in local brick and limestone. Choosing hybrid over all-timber is the usual judgement for a residence of this size under current Canadian code and cost conditions — timber takes the floor plates and part of the vertical structure, while steel and concrete resolve the cores, long spans and fire requirements. The point worth recording separately is provenance: according to project materials, almost all major building materials — mass timber, compressed brick and limestone — are sourced within Ontario. That local chain serves two ends at once: cutting the embodied carbon of transport, and making where the material came from part of the building's account of itself. For students this is a checklist item you can apply directly to your own studio work: on your materials schedule, how many entries can you name a source for?

Systems and consequence: it takes four systems stacked to reach zero carbon. The environmental systems comprise the carbon stored in the hybrid mass timber structure, a geothermal exchange system, a cistern for rainwater collection and reuse, and the rooftop farm. Together they underpin the project's target of Canada Green Building Council (CaGBC) Zero Carbon Design certification and LEED Platinum. The lesson is that no single technology gets you there: timber addresses embodied carbon, geothermal exchange and envelope performance determine operational carbon, and the water and food systems handle two boundaries routinely excluded from carbon accounting altogether. Blackwell provided structural engineering, Smith + Andersen MEP, Footprint Consulting sustainability oversight, and Graham Construction was the general contractor — a consultant list that itself makes the point that zero-carbon buildings are a delivery problem, not a design decision an architect makes alone. The design had already won a 2025 Holcim Foundation Award for Best Practice in Sustainable Design. The welcome celebration was held on 14 September; ArchDaily published the full project on 22 September.

ArchDaily essay: time as a building material — Kéré's laterite bricks, human-chain pours, and buildings that keep changing after completion
Sources: ArchDaily (24 Sept) · Kéré Architecture project materials
ArchDaily published an essay on 24 September arguing that time should be counted among the materials in Francis Kéré's work. Kéré's own formulation: "After all, time is a key ingredient of my profession." The projects cited include Schorge Secondary School in Burkina Faso (laterite brick extracted on site), Naaba Belem Goumma Secondary School (workers forming a human chain to pour the walls), the Opera Village at Laongo (a spiral layout centred on a theatre), and the Surgical Clinic and Health Centre. The methodology is broken into three temporal phases: the drawing at design stage, the adaptation of construction method to the labour and materials actually available, and the continued modification of the building by its occupants afterwards. For student work that terminates at the rendering, this is a useful counter-reference: when the construction process itself is carried out by community labour, drawing resolution stops being the sole measure of a project's quality.

In Depth
Keeping a 1993 temporary installation alive: Storefront's renovation is almost invisible, and it determines how many more years that facade has
Cross-checked: The Architect's Newspaper (23 Sept) · Storefront exhibition calendar and about page · Steven Holl Architects project archive · Architect Magazine archive coverage · Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts grant records

Concept: the restraint of the renovation follows from the standing of what is being renovated. Storefront for Art and Architecture is a tiny institution with outsized influence in New York's architectural culture: founded in 1982 by Kyong Park with R.L. Seltman and Arleen Schloss at 51 Prince Street, later relocated to 97 Kenmare Street. Its facade there was remade in 1993 by artist Vito Acconci with architect Steven Holl, and is among the most-cited small-scale works of late-twentieth-century American architecture. Faced with that, GRT Architects' governing judgement is evident: leave no authorial trace. As reported by The Architect's Newspaper on 23 September, the work is described as "long overdue" infrastructural upgrading, and the visible additions number exactly two — a door handle and a strip of light.

Spatial strategy: a triangle nearly 100 feet long, tapering from 20 feet to 3. Storefront's gallery is a ground-floor triangular room close to 100 feet (about 30 metres) long, 20 feet (about 6 metres) at its widest, narrowing to 3 feet (about 0.9 metres) at the western end. That nearly unusable plan is precisely what defines the institution's exhibition language: any work shown must contend with a violent perspectival compression, and no installation can avoid the sliver at the far end where a body barely fits. The 1993 facade is a direct response to that plan — Holl and Acconci set a row of rotating panels of varying orientation along the length of the elevation; opened, the boundary between gallery and sidewalk disappears and the exhibition spills onto the street. The move turns the name "Storefront" into the spatial operation itself.

Construction and intervention: the backlighting is the only change to the exterior. The substance of this renovation is the wholesale replacement of the electrical and HVAC systems — for a small institutional space whose fit-out dates to 1993 and which has seen only one facade restoration since, in summer 2008, those systems are long past their service life. Visibly, there are two changes. GRT designed a new handle for the main entry so the door can be identified within a continuous elevation made of panels — a long-standing practical problem of the rotating facade is that visitors cannot find the way in. And back-lighting was added behind Acconci's concrete letters, which spell out the institution's name and had been nearly illegible after dark. Both moves point at the same thing: accessibility. When a 1993 avant-garde installation is reassessed three decades on, the priority turns out not to be its form but whether people can still get through the door.

Context and consequence: what happens once the temporary becomes permanent. It is worth remembering that Holl and Acconci's facade was designed as a temporary intervention, and has remained in place ever since. That produces an increasingly common conservation problem: a large body of experimental work from the late twentieth century was never built to the durability standards of permanent construction, yet its cultural value has been firmly established in the thirty years since. How to renew the technical systems of such work without altering its form or material expression is the shared question, and Storefront's renovation offers a small but clear-headed sample: replace everything mechanical, add one strip of light outside. The first exhibition in the renovated space — "The Biography of a Building: Runik's House of Culture" by the Hajde! Foundation and artist Petrit Halilaj, opening 25 September — happens to be about the life story of a building (the House of Culture in the Kosovan village of Runik), an unplanned rhyme with the renovation itself.

Orhan Ayyüce died suddenly at his home in Albuquerque, New Mexico, on 20 September 2026, aged 75. Born in İzmir, Turkey, he came to the United States in the 1970s intending to stay three months while visiting his sister in Sacramento, and remained. As an architect he kept an independent practice working largely on residential and small commercial projects, but his career was broader than practice: teaching, writing, criticism, preservation, research and community-building were interconnected parts of the same work. For more than two decades he was among Archinect's most devoted contributors — writing, interviewing, reporting, arguing in the Forum, introducing people to one another, and holding together relationships across generations of the Los Angeles architecture community. He is a reminder that the public character of architectural media is not created by platforms but by the people who take the trouble to speak on them seriously, over years.
