EDITOR'S NOTE

Two of today's stories put cost at the centre. In Holstebro, Denmark, Skovsporet has delivered 36 3D-printed student apartments and taken in its first residents, built for roughly €1,880 per square metre — inside Denmark's statutory budget ceiling for subsidised housing. For once, 3D concrete printing appears not as a pavilion or a show home but as an ordinary housing project that cleared a subsidised-housing budget and approval process. In England, the first phase of York Central won detailed planning approval on 14 September: 110 acres of railway brownfield, a £2bn scheme, 1,014 homes in phase one with 20 per cent affordable. In Guangzhou, the Marubi Headquarters is now published in full: 68,000 m² on a 5,770 m² site, capped at 138 metres, four stacked and offset volumes that force the structure onto transfer trusses. ArchDaily named the 20 winners of its sixth Next Practices Awards, from Niger, Haiti, Mozambique, Vietnam and Ecuador among others. Also today: VISTA in Austria, Sordo Madaleno's sunken gallery in Baja, thirteen public toilets in Shanghai's Xuhui District, and a rooftop addition in Prague.

IN DEPTH
A 5,770 m² site asked to hold 68,000 m²: at Marubi Headquarters the constraint was translated into a structural problem
Cross-checked: gooood · Shing & Partners Design Group (architect) · Haizhu District Government (joint completion inspection notice) · Guangzhou Planning and Natural Resources Bureau (post-approval disclosure) · Sina Finance · The Paper · NetEase (topping-out report)

The Marubi Headquarters occupies a plot on Xingang East Road in Pazhou West, Haizhu District, Guangzhou. The architect is Shing & Partners Design Group; the client is the cosmetics company Marubi. Designed in 2022 and completed in 2025, it has four basement levels and twenty-nine storeys above ground, with the tower held to 138 metres by a local height cap. The basic figures already contain the whole problem: a 5,770 m² site carrying 68,000 m² of floor area — a very high plot ratio — with the height locked down.

Concept: treat the constraint as the source of character rather than an obstacle.The architect's stated position is that the limits were not obstacles but the generator of the building's identity. As rhetoric that means little; what matters is that it lands on a verifiable operation. With the plan restricted by the site and the height restricted by the cap, the only remaining variable was the vertical position of the volumes relative to one another. The tower was therefore broken into four stacked and offset volumes, each rotated and cantilevered towards the north-west — the direction from which the building is mainly seen across the Pearl River. That move does two things at once: it gives a tower flattened by a height cap a legible silhouette again, and it claws back floor area through the cantilevers to compensate for the small footprint.

Structure: once the mass twists, the vertical load path stops working.This is the hard part of the project and the part worth reading closely. The architect's own words: a twist at this scale means the vertical load path common to tall buildings no longer holds, and a new equilibrium has to be established through structural transfer trusses. In other words the offsets are not a cladding gesture — they move the columns of upper volumes off the columns below, so at certain levels the loads have to travel horizontally before rejoining the vertical system. That is the work of a transfer truss. The cost is the depth and steel tonnage of the transfer levels themselves; the benefit is stated plainly by the architect, which is that the transfer removed the corner columns and produced open corner spaces with 4.2-metre ceilings. In an office tower the corner is the highest-value position, with the best views, so deleting the corner column converts directly into usable value. That is also the test for whether a twisted form of this kind is justified: did the twist buy a specific spatial gain, or only a sense of movement on the elevation?

Spatial strategy: give the ground floor away, and make vertical circulation an event.The second move happens at grade. Nearly 40 per cent of the ground floor is left open under a 12-metre clearance. In Guangzhou's hot, humid subtropical climate that has a physical rationale: removing the enclosure at ground level lets air move through, produces a cooler microclimate at the base of the building, and hands the area back to the city. Paired with it is a steel spiral stair 13.6 metres in diameter with a 12-metre unsupported run, linking five levels from basement one to the fourth floor of the podium and connecting the underground retail straight into the metro. According to the project material published by gooood, the stair has been certified by Guinness World Records for the greatest unsupported spiral-stair section height. A record is not in itself a measure of architectural quality, but the technical fact behind it is real: a 12-metre run with no intermediate support means the loads have to be absorbed by the steel helical shell of the stair itself. The building also sets observation decks and sky terraces at several heights, and uses an arcade system to make semi-outdoor gathering space available around the clock — the arcade being Guangzhou's most basic climatic and commercial interface type, and a direct citation of local typology in the podium of a corporate tower.

Context: the Pazhou West headquarters cluster is solving the same problem.Pazhou West is a district designated in recent years for artificial intelligence and the digital economy, and a series of corporate headquarters have landed on narrow plots along the Pearl River, all facing the same three conditions at once: small sites, strict height caps, and a brief that asks for a landmark. Shing & Partners' earlier Shangpin Home Collection headquarters in the same district took the opposite route, reinforcing horizontal lines with an extra-wide glass curtain wall on a 3.1-metre horizontal module to echo the river. Put side by side, the two projects show two answers to the same constraint: one extends horizontally, the other stacks and offsets vertically. What matters for readers is that neither begins from a formal preference; both are derived from the same specific question of how to achieve legibility under a height cap. At city scale, this generation of high-density headquarters gives much of its ground plane over to open floors and arcades — private land compensating for a shortfall in public space, which has become one of the more common and more effective trades on heavily developed plots in China's first-tier cities.

IN DEPTH
Land fenced off by the railway for more than a century is finally going to be built on: at York Central the hard problem was never the design, it was getting in
Cross-checked: Architects' Journal · York & North Yorkshire Combined Authority · RailBusinessDaily · Built Environment News · Allies and Morrison (masterplanner) · 3DReid (building architect) · World Construction Network

On 14 September 2026 City of York Council approved the Reserved Matters Application for Phase 1C of York Central. The site lies immediately west of York Station and covers about 110 acres (roughly 44.5 hectares) of former railway land, making it one of the largest city-centre brownfield regeneration projects in the UK, with total investment of around £2 billion. Phase one as approved comprises 1,014 mixed-tenure homes with 20 per cent affordable, a 213-bedroom hotel, a 9,210 m² innovation hub, 6,430 m² of retail and leisure space, a new western entrance to York Station and parkland the authority describes as extensive. Allies and Morrison leads the masterplan; buildings are by Haworth Tompkins, Cartwright Pickard, Whittam Cox and 3DReid, with public realm and landscape by Grant Associates. The developers are McLaren Regeneration and Arlington Real Estate. Construction is expected to begin next year, with first occupation in early 2029 and phase one complete during 2030.

Concept: the central problem here is access, not density.York is a city defined by its medieval walls, with the station pressed against their outer face — and the railway land west of the tracks has been effectively sealed off from city life for more than a century. Not because it is far, but because tracks, sidings and fences form a boundary that is hard to cross. The first question for this project is therefore not what to build but how anyone gets there. The least glamorous item in the phase one schedule is also the most consequential: a new western entrance to York Station. Only once the station works as a route in both directions does the land behind it turn from the back of the city into the front. Projects of this kind are usually decided by one or two connections rather than by total floor area.

Spatial strategy: settle access and public space first, then argue about the mix.The phase one programme reads like a standard British regeneration recipe: housing as the base, hotel and innovation hub as the economic engine, retail and leisure for street activity, parkland as environmental compensation. What is worth noting is the division of labour — one masterplanner sets the framework, four practices take individual buildings, one landscape practice holds the public realm. That "one masterplan, many architects" structure is close to standard on large British brownfield sites, the aim being to keep street scale and frontage continuous while avoiding the uniformity that comes of a whole district drawn by a single office. Grant Associates' remit over the public realm matters especially: in a scheme phased across years, public space is often the only element running through the whole thing, so putting it in one pair of hands is what allows plots finished years apart to read as a single place.

Process: what a Reserved Matters Application actually means.For readers outside the British planning system, the term is worth unpacking. Large UK projects usually secure outline planning permission first, fixing land use, quantum and broad principles, while the specifics — appearance, layout, scale, landscaping, access — are "reserved" for later applications approved one at a time. That is why the same York Central keeps reappearing in the news across different years: what was approved this week is the detailed design of phase one, not the viability of the project. The practical implication for students is that in this system design work is split into two stages — winning the principle, then resolving the detail. The first is policy and negotiation work; only the second resembles architectural design as usually understood. And almost all of a project's schedule risk sits in the first stage.

Context: railway brownfield is the last big land bank in European cities.York Central belongs to a wider category. As rail operations change, the yards, goods depots and maintenance land in European city centres are being released; these sites share three advantages — excellent location, large area, consolidated ownership — and three problems: contamination, live railway operations that cannot be interrupted, and poor access. Set against New York's Interborough Express, which this paper covered on 14 September and which keeps a freight corridor in transport use, York Central takes the other route: transport land converted to city blocks, at the price of having to solve access itself. The 20 per cent affordable requirement is middling by current English standards, and in a historic city with long-standing affordability pressure that figure will probably remain a live local political argument. First occupation only in 2029 means three years between a consent for 1,014 homes and any actual supply — the common lag of every large brownfield scheme, and the thing most easily overlooked when housing policy is judged by planning-permission numbers.

IN DEPTH
The first 3D-printed housing that does not need the word "first": what Skovsporet proves is that it repeated six times and stayed on budget
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · designboom · 3D Printing Industry · VoxelMatters · SAGA Space Architects (architect) · 3DCP Group (contractor) · Yanko Design

Skovsporet stands in Holstebro in western Denmark, beside the VIA University College campus. The client is the social housing organisation NordVestBO; buildings by SAGA Space Architects with MS+ Arkitekter; 3D concrete printing (3DCP) by 3DCP Group. The scheme runs to 36 single-storey apartments in six clusters, 1,650 m² in total, units of 38 to 50 m², built over 12 months, with the first students moving in this month.

Concept: take the technology's working envelope as the design premise.It helps to be clear about what 3D concrete printing currently does well. It is good at extruding continuous vertical walls; it is not good at horizontal members, and it is not good at multi-storey structure. Skovsporet works entirely within that envelope — single-storey, low, dispersed into six small clusters. This is not timidity; it converts a technical condition into a settlement form. The 36 units are not lined up in one long block but form a student village organised around shared outdoor space, courtyards and walking paths. For student housing, a low dispersed layout is in any case better at producing encounters than a double-loaded corridor, so the technical limit and the brief happen to pull in the same direction.

Spatial strategy: locate the print beds first, then the buildings.The single fact that best explains the method is how the site was handled: print beds were positioned between the existing trees, and about 90 per cent of the trees on site were retained. Note the order. Conventional construction fixes the building outline first and then clears whatever vegetation stands in the way; on-site printing equipment needs a level working strip, so the design instead went looking for the gaps between trees and placed the buildings into them. Ninety per cent tree retention was not achieved by transplanting or replanting — it is a direct consequence of a layout decision. That suggests something worth studying: how on-site additive construction engages a site may matter more than the formal freedom it is usually advertised for.

Material innovation: three decarbonisation moves stacked in one project.The first is cement substitution. The printing concrete was batched on site using FUTURECEM, a lower-carbon cement developed by Aalborg Portland; per 3D Printing Industry its emissions run up to 30 per cent below conventional cement. The second is more radical: the project also used cement-free printed elements made with MEVOcem, supported by Innovation Fund Denmark. Eliminating Portland cement altogether is the end point of concrete decarbonisation, and seeing it in a real housing project rather than a laboratory specimen is the detail here most worth following. The third is the interior: timber, cork panelling and glass, with cork serving both as finish and as part of the thermal and acoustic performance. The reason to separate the three is that 3D printing is not intrinsically low-carbon. It saves formwork and cuts waste, but it is still concrete; the actual emissions reduction comes from replacing the binder. Conflating those two is the most common error in assessing projects of this kind.

Context: repeatability and a cost ceiling matter more than any "first".3DCP Group chief executive Mikkel Brich put it precisely: "The technical breakthrough was not printing one successful structure. It was repeating the process across six clusters while improving speed, maintaining accuracy." That is the line between demonstration and construction. The figures back it up: a three-person crew with a single COBOD BOD3 printer; one 275 m² building containing six apartments printed in five working days. On cost, 3D Printing Industry reports roughly €1,880 per square metre for construction, excluding design, engineering, utilities and site works, with rents from €670 a month for a 50 m² unit, and total expenditure below Denmark's statutory budget ceiling for subsidised housing — the article does not state the ceiling itself. COBOD co-founder Philip Lund-Nielsen calls it the largest example of the technology in Europe so far. For architecture readers the value of the project is not formal or spectacular: it is among the first 3D-printed housing schemes to pass all the way through the budget and approval framework of subsidised housing. For a decade this technology's news cycle has been single pavilions, show homes and "world's first" claims, all of which failed to answer cost and repeatability. Skovsporet answers with specific numbers and six repetitions.

ArchDaily on long-span steel: span is not a question of size but of what remains once the columns are taken out of the plan

ArchDaily's "Beyond the Replica": the problem with digital heritage is not precision but that visitors cannot tell evidence from interpretation
Sources: ArchDaily · Virtual Avebury project research · Digital Ajanta project material
ArchDaily published Ananya Nayak's essay "Beyond the Replica: Digital Heritage and the Limits of Preservation" on 15 September, on the uses and limits of 3D scanning and VR reconstruction in architectural preservation. Cases cited include India's Digital Ajanta project, which uses 3D scanning and VR to reconstruct the Ajanta Caves in Maharashtra, enabling remote access and non-intrusive research; Virtual Avebury in the UK, which reconstructed a Neolithic landscape as it stood around 2300 BCE and, in testing with more than 1,200 participants, found that "the more believable participants considered the simulation, the stronger their reported sense of place"; the Baths of Caracalla in Italy, described as the first large Italian archaeological site entirely usable in 3D; and a 360-degree VR experience of Persepolis in Iran. The author's central problem: when reconstructions mix scanned evidence with interpretive additions, "the difference can become difficult for a visitor to perceive", and technological precision is easily misread as historical accuracy. Useful material for preservation and digital-representation seminars.
