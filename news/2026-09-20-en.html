Sunday is a thin day in the architectural press, and this issue does not pad. Four stories held up to verification. MATERIALS
Studio RAP and Powerhouse Company complete Powerwall: 322 robotically printed ceramic tiles turn a 150 kV switch station's invisible electromagnetic field into an 8-by-5-metre glazed relief
Sources: ArchDaily (Sept 19) · designboom (September) · Wallpaper* · MaterialDistrict · Parametric Architecture · Studio RAP
Powerwall sits on Switchstation Beverwijk, a 150 kV transformer station in Beverwijk, the Netherlands. The client is TenneT, the Dutch transmission system operator; Powerhouse Company is the building's architect; Rotterdam-based Studio RAP designed the relief and printed the ceramics; JP van Eesteren and Mobil built it; Pim Top photographed it. Completed 2026. The work covers an 8-metre-high, 5-metre-wide area of the brick façade (ArchDaily records the project area as 40 m²) and comprises 322 individually distinct ceramic tiles. The starting point was not a decorative motif but the one physical quantity that genuinely exists inside the building and cannot be seen: the flow lines of the transformers' electromagnetic fields. Studio RAP used computational design algorithms to convert magnetic-field geometry into buildable ceramic forms; spirals and sweeping lines move across the surface in a composition that reads as simultaneously static and in motion. Every tile was produced by robotic clay 3D printing in Studio RAP's own Rotterdam workshop, with the print layer height deliberately tuned to leave a fine ribbed texture — the additive process stays legible on the surface rather than being sanded away. Because clay shrinks and can warp during drying and firing, each tile's tolerances had to be compensated for at the digital model stage. A translucent turquoise glaze finishes the assembly, sinking into the printed grooves and pooling in the recesses so the relief reads deeper or shallower as the day's light shifts.

PROJECTS
HENN's Kingdee Tower published on ArchDaily: 211 metres and 98,800 m² in Shenzhen's Nanshan, a pentagonal plan stepping outward floor by floor to win back a site squeezed between two existing wings
Sources: ArchDaily (published Sept 20) · Archiposition · World-Architects · Archello · HENN (architect) · Architecture MasterPrize
Kingdee Tower (also published as Kingdee Cloud Tower) stands in the Kingdee Software Park in Shenzhen's Nanshan district. The client is Kingdee, a major Chinese enterprise-management cloud services provider; the architect is the German practice HENN (lead architect Martin Henn), with Gensler on interiors, PAG Façade Systems as façade consultant, ACLA on landscape and CSCEC as general contractor. Gross floor area is 98,800 m², height 211 metres, 44 storeys, completed 2025; ArchDaily published the full project on September 20, 2026. What makes the building worth reading is where its plan came from. The tower base is wedged into a narrow, irregular residual space between two existing office wings, and the pentagonal footprint is not a formal preference but the shape left over once that residual site is used up. Because the base floors are constrained by the wings, their plates are small; higher up, the floor plates step progressively outward, producing a clear set of cantilevers that recover the lost area and, from the street, mark the entrance to the software park. The façade uses diamond-shaped units of triple-silver low-emissivity glass to balance daylight against solar gain. The project was built to China's Three-Star Green Building Standard and holds LEED Platinum pre-certification. Photography by Tian Fangfang.

New York's East Side Coastal Resiliency project enters its final year: $1.45 billion, 2.25 miles, and a flood barrier that BIG and MNLA built entirely as parkland, due for completion in 2026
Sources: The Architect's Newspaper (Sept/Oct 2026 landscape issue) · ArchDaily · BIG (designer) · MNLA · ONE Architecture & Urbanism · NYC Parks / City of New York ESCR
The East Side Coastal Resiliency project (ESCR) is New York City's post-Hurricane Sandy coastal protection scheme for Manhattan's Lower East Side: $1.45 billion, running roughly 2.25 miles from Montgomery Street to East 25th Street. It is led by the NYC Department of Design and Construction (NYCDDC), with a design team of BIG (Bjarke Ingels Group), Mathews Nielsen Landscape Architects (MNLA), ONE Architecture & Urbanism and AKRF, developed with Lower East Side residents. The central move is to raise the ground plane of East River Park so the parkland itself acts as the flood barrier, combined with floodwalls, berms and upgrades to the existing stormwater system, delivering FEMA-accredited flood protection for more than 110,000 Lower East Side residents. Sections of East River Park have reopened in stages since May and September 2025. New amenities include the Corlears Hook pedestrian bridge, a Delancey Street pedestrian bridge, six additional tennis courts, basketball courts, an amphitheatre, a multi-use turf field, picnic and barbecue areas and an extended esplanade. Per The Architect's Newspaper's Sept/Oct 2026 landscape issue, delivery is on time and under budget, with final completion expected in 2026; work continues in parts of The Battery and around South Street Seaport. The project has earned an Envision Gold Award from the Institute for Sustainable Infrastructure and WEDG verification from the Waterfront Alliance.

Donald Judd's Artillery Sheds go under major restoration in October: Schaum Architects leading, the Getty Conservation Institute on the team, and 100 aluminium works moved for the first time in over forty years
Sources: Chinati Foundation announcement (Aug 19) · The Art Newspaper (Aug 19) · ArchDaily · The Architect's Newspaper · Artforum · ARTnews
The Chinati Foundation in Marfa, Texas, has announced a major restoration of the Artillery Sheds — two former artillery storage buildings from the U.S. Army's Fort D.A. Russell that Donald Judd adapted in the 1980s for the permanent installation of his 100 untitled works in mill aluminum (1982–1986). Judd's own intervention replaced the roofs and enlarged the original small openings into large gridded glazing, so the interiors depend entirely on daylight and the works change continuously through the day; the binding of building to artwork is itself part of the work. Schaum Architects leads the restoration, with a team including Alfred Lippincott, who fabricated the aluminium boxes with Judd in the 1980s, plus the Getty Conservation Institute, structural and climate engineers, conservators and landscape designers. The scope covers building envelope, accessibility, environmental performance and landscape while preserving Judd's site-specific installation. To address the buildings' infrastructure, all 100 aluminium works will be moved for the first time in more than forty years so conservators can treat them individually. Construction begins in October, with completion targeted for 2029. The Artillery Sheds were listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2025 as part of the expanded Donald Judd Historic District.

IN DEPTH
When the site is down to a slot, the tower grows back above it: Kingdee Tower's pentagon is not a shape, it is what was left over
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (published Sept 20) · Archiposition · World-Architects · Archello · HENN (architect) · Architecture MasterPrize

Design concept: the residual site came first, the plan came second. The Kingdee Software Park in Shenzhen's Nanshan district is an established corporate campus that has been operating for years, and two existing office wings already occupy most of the land. Whatever a new tower could use was the irregular leftover space between them. That is the provenance of HENN's pentagonal plan — not a formal preference derived from a geometric motif, but the outline that remains once the boundaries of a wedged site are fully used. The methodological point for students is direct: when you insert a building into a dense, already-built campus, the first constraint on the plan is rarely programmatic zoning. It is the passive residual line defined by setbacks, solar-spacing requirements and the exterior walls of what is already there. Accepting that line is cheaper than fighting it, and it tends to produce a form you can actually justify.

Spatial strategy: give way below, take it back above. The pentagonal base is compressed by the two wings, so its plates are small. A constant-section extrusion could never accommodate 98,800 m², and small low-rise plates make poor open-plan offices in any case. HENN's answer is to let the floor plates step progressively outward as the building rises, so the upper office floors are appreciably larger than the base, producing a clear set of cantilevers. That single move does three things at once. It recovers upstairs the area conceded downstairs, making the gross floor area viable. It lifts the office floors clear of the immediate obstruction of the two wings, giving them uninterrupted daylight and outlook. And the soffit of the cantilever forms a directional covered space above the entrance to the software park — seen from the city, the flaring of the tower's lower edge is itself the entrance sign. At 211 metres and 44 storeys the tower is the high point of the campus and of this tech district, but what does the work at city scale is the give-and-take of those lower floors, not the top.

Structure and technology: a diamond-grid façade and two green standards. Stepping outward means every floor's edge condition differs, so a single repeated standard unit cannot run to the top. The façade uses diamond-shaped units of triple-silver low-emissivity glass. A diamond grid absorbs a gradually shifting slab edge through small adjustments to unit angle, avoiding a proliferation of one-off bespoke panels; the triple-silver coating answers Shenzhen's climate — high solar radiation and a long cooling season — by holding down the solar heat gain coefficient while keeping visible light transmittance usable. The project was built to China's Three-Star Green Building Standard and holds LEED Platinum pre-certification. Running both systems in parallel has become standard practice for premium Chinese office towers: Three-Star maps onto domestic approvals and policy incentives, LEED onto multinational tenants and capital markets. One caveat: the published material does not disclose the structural system in detail — the core-and-frame configuration, how the cantilevered floors transfer load, whether outrigger trusses are used — and this bulletin will not speculate.

Context and consequence: a building that completes a campus, and a transnational team sheet. Kingdee is one of China's principal enterprise-management cloud providers, and the tower gives it offices, conference space and a restaurant. Programmatically it completes the existing software park rather than founding a new headquarters. The collaboration structure is worth recording: HENN of Germany leads the architecture (lead architect Martin Henn), Gensler handles interiors, PAG Façade Systems consults on the curtain wall, ACLA does landscape, CSCEC is general contractor, Tian Fangfang photographs. This is a thoroughly typical configuration for high-rise offices in China's first-tier cities over the past decade: an overseas practice drives massing and façade concept while international interiors teams and domestic contractors deliver. The building completed in 2025 and ArchDaily published it on September 20, 2026, which is the occasion for this report. Readers should note that in architectural publishing the gap between completion and publication frequently exceeds a year; cite the completion year.

Bangbae Geunsaeng Neighbourhood Facility / aoa architects: 494 m², with the lift and stairs pushed to the south side where no sunlight setback applies
Sources: ArchDaily (Sept 20) · aoa architects (architect) · Hyosook Chin (photography)
The Bangbae Geunsaeng Neighbourhood Facility is in Bangbae-dong, Seoul, by aoa architects (lead architect Jaewon Suh), 494 m², completed 2026, in concrete, steel and glass. It is the kind of small rental neighbourhood facility common across Seoul, and the governing decision is a single practical one. In the architects' words, the elevator and other vertical circulation, which require more height, are placed on the south side where there are no sunlight-restriction setbacks — leaving the setback-constrained portion of the massing for rental units that can absorb the stepping, so the lettable floor area stays as close to a clean rectangle as possible and the core does not have to negotiate the terracing. For students working on tight urban infill, this is a clean sample of a regulatory constraint converted directly into a plan strategy.
The Button – VAVI manufacturing facilities and headquarters / Mjölk architekti: 1,493 m² in concrete, joining a shirt-sewing workshop to a corporate head office
Sources: ArchDaily (Sept 20) · Mjölk architekti (architect) · BoysPlayNice (photography)

The client, VAVI, is a Czech clothing manufacturer that both produces its own collections and does custom sewing for large companies; the architects worked with current management on a building meant to reflect the company's commitment to quality and craftsmanship in shirt manufacturing. Placing production and headquarters in one building is an increasingly common contemporary form among European small and mid-sized manufacturers, and its difficulty is specific: the workshop wants long spans, high illuminance and clear logistics, while offices and showroom want reception spaces at a domestic scale — and both have to share one structure and one envelope without compromising each other.

IN DEPTH
Burying the floodwall inside the park: $1.45 billion and fourteen years bought New York not a levee but a chain of high ground you can play ball on
Cross-checked: The Architect's Newspaper (Sept/Oct 2026 landscape issue) · ArchDaily · BIG (designer) · MNLA · ONE Architecture & Urbanism · NYC Parks / City of New York ESCR · Landezine

Design concept: flood defence should not be a scar across the city. After Hurricane Sandy in 2012, Manhattan's Lower East Side became one of New York's most urgent candidates for coastal protection. The most direct engineering answer is a floodwall along the river — fast, cheap, effective, and paid for by severing residents from the East River and leaving a miles-long concrete wound through the waterfront. The East Side Coastal Resiliency project (ESCR) takes the other route: rather than putting a levee in front of the park, it raises the ground plane of East River Park so the parkland itself becomes the barrier. The thinking descends from BIG's "Big U" research — protective infrastructure should also be public space — and BIG describes the result as a "parkipelago" of connected green spaces. Planning students should see the trade-off clearly: raising the ground plane means demolishing and rebuilding an entire existing park, including felling a large number of mature trees, which is exactly why the project drew fierce community opposition during construction. The controversy in resilience work is rarely about whether to protect; it is about what, and whose present, gets sacrificed to do it.

Spatial strategy: a continuous 2.25-mile line, broken into many usable everyday places. The project runs roughly 2.25 miles from Montgomery Street to East 25th Street, using a composite system of raised ground, floodwalls, berms and closable flood gates, together with upgrades to the existing stormwater network to increase capture of heavy rainfall. That last point is easy to overlook: coastal protection that only holds back the river without addressing interior drainage can end up trapping stormwater behind its own barrier. Above ground, the line is cut into a series of specific everyday places. The Corlears Hook and Delancey Street pedestrian bridges cross the FDR Drive and stitch the neighbourhood back to the water; new amenities include six additional tennis courts, basketball courts, a multi-use turf field, an amphitheatre, picnic and barbecue areas, nature-exploration and water-play zones, and an extended esplanade. What residents perceive day to day is courts and promenade; the flood elevation is hidden in the sectional design of those places. Officially, the system delivers FEMA-accredited protection for more than 110,000 Lower East Side residents.

Structure and technology: certification is the hard currency here. Unlike a building, a coastal protection scheme is substantially defined by third-party accreditation. FEMA accreditation determines whether the line is recognised within the federal flood insurance system, which bears directly on premiums and development rights for tens of thousands of households behind it. The project also holds an Envision Gold Award from the Institute for Sustainable Infrastructure and WEDG (Waterfront Edge Design Guidelines) verification from the Waterfront Alliance. The three regimes address, respectively, flood performance, infrastructure sustainability, and waterfront ecology and public access. The work is led by NYCDDC, with BIG, Mathews Nielsen Landscape Architects (MNLA), ONE Architecture & Urbanism and AKRF designing, and Lower East Side residents involved in the process.

Context and consequence: a megaproject that is on time and under budget, which is itself the news. Sections of East River Park reopened in stages from May and September 2025. Per The Architect's Newspaper's Sept/Oct 2026 landscape issue, ESCR is proceeding on schedule and within budget, with final completion expected in 2026; work continues in parts of The Battery and around South Street Seaport. Against the general record of large American public infrastructure, those two phrases carry weight. But the more useful thing for architecture and planning students to remember is the timescale: from the 2012 disaster to completion in 2026 lie fourteen years of planning, environmental review, litigation, community negotiation and construction. Climate adaptation runs on decade-long cycles, and sea level and storm intensity do not wait — which is precisely where "resilience" stops being an adjective in professional discussion and becomes a question of budget and programme.

The four mechanisms MOHURD set out: binding "urban physical examination" to renewal, and listing financing and heritage integration separately
Sources: State Council Information Office briefing (Sept 18) · 21st Century Business Herald (Sept 18) · ifeng (Sept 18)
At the September 18 briefing, Zhang Qiang set out four mechanisms to be established and improved: integrated advancement of urban physical examination and urban renewal; a mechanism for implementing urban renewal plans; a sustainable investment and financing mechanism for urban renewal; and a mechanism organically integrating urban renewal with historic and cultural preservation. Each addresses something specific. The first institutionalises the sequence — diagnose first, then renew — to curb projects chosen on impulse. The third goes straight at the hardest part of stock-based renewal: renovating old residential compounds generates no stable cash-flow return, which makes private capital difficult to attract. The fourth responds to recent disputes over demolishing genuine fabric to build replicas, and over excessive commercialisation. For planning students, these four can be read as the principal institutional framework for approving and reviewing Chinese urban renewal projects in the coming years.
Where the 97 billion yuan goes: old residential compounds and dilapidated urban housing, reaching about 8 million households
Sources: State Council Information Office briefing (Sept 18) · Sina Finance (Sept 18) · Tencent News (Sept 18)
Central budget investment for urban renewal in 2026 is set at 97 billion yuan, directed principally at renovating old urban residential compounds and dilapidated housing, and reaching approximately 8 million households. The figure is more informative read against the targets of the Urban Renewal 15th Five-Year Plan, which calls for 115,000 old residential compounds to begin renovation over five years (reported in No. 013). At an annual order of magnitude of 97 billion yuan, central budget funds can only cover part of that; the remainder must come from local fiscal budgets, special-purpose bonds, private capital and resident contributions — which is precisely why a "sustainable investment and financing mechanism" is listed as a mechanism in its own right.

Materials & Technology MATERIALS
IN DEPTH
322 tiles, no two alike: Studio RAP computed a substation's electromagnetic field into geometry, then had a robot print it
Cross-checked: ArchDaily (Sept 19) · designboom (September) · Wallpaper* · MaterialDistrict · Parametric Architecture · Studio RAP (designer)

Design concept: take the one thing that is genuinely present in the building and cannot be seen. Substations are the most mute buildings in a city: no permanent occupants, no need for daylight, functional requirements limited to safety and heat rejection. They therefore tend to arrive as sealed brick boxes, onto which public art is later applied as a pattern bearing no relation to the interior. Powerwall inverts that. Inside this 150 kV switch station in Beverwijk, the Netherlands, powerful electromagnetic fields are running: invisible, inaccessible, and the entire reason the building exists. Studio RAP took the flow lines of the transformers' fields as the design vocabulary — spirals and sweeping lines spreading across the wall, organised by algorithm into a composition that reads as both still and moving. This is not printing an image onto a wall; it is making the façade an externalisation of an internal physical process. What students can take from it is a method of finding a subject: when a building has no conventional "human activity" to express, its technical content is a legitimate source of architectural expression.

Spatial strategy: 8 by 5 metres, a question of the scale of one wall. The work covers an 8-metre-high, 5-metre-wide area of the brick façade — ArchDaily records the project area as 40 m² — assembled from 322 individually distinct ceramic tiles. The scale is well judged in both directions. It is large enough to register at the scale of the street as the principal event of the façade rather than a decorative panel hung on it, and small enough that every tile can be produced individually on one studio's robotic workbench without recourse to industrial kiln batching. The translucent turquoise glaze unifies the surface chromatically while making it highly sensitive to changing daylight: the glaze sinks into the grooves left by printing and pools in the recesses, so the relief reads deeper or shallower through the day. The "pattern" on this wall is not fixed; the time of viewing is one of its parameters.

Material and fabrication innovation: robotic clay printing, where the difficulty is shrinkage and warping. All 322 tiles were formed by robotic clay 3D printing in Studio RAP's own Rotterdam workshop. Three technical points are worth recording. First, each tile's form is generated directly from magnetic-field geometry by computational design algorithms, so "every one different" adds no design hours, only fabrication time — this is the fundamental break between digital fabrication and mould-based craft, where non-repetition explodes cost, whereas in additive manufacturing it is close to free. Second, the print layer height is deliberately tuned to leave a fine ribbed texture, so the trace of additive manufacturing becomes part of the surface quality rather than a defect to be polished out. Third, and most practically: clay shrinks and can warp during drying and firing, and shrinkage behaviour varies with the thickness of the form, so each tile's tolerances must be pre-compensated at the digital model stage. That is the real threshold for ceramic 3D printing moving from the laboratory to a building façade — not whether the shape can be printed, but whether 322 of them still fit together after firing. Studio RAP's own framing is that because every tile is generated by algorithm and produced by a robot, ornament can return to contemporary architecture without the cost of handcraft.

Context and consequence: the client is a grid operator, and that matters. The client is TenneT, the Dutch transmission system operator; Powerhouse Company is the building's architect; Studio RAP designed the relief and printed the ceramics; JP van Eesteren and Mobil built it; Pim Top photographed it; completed 2026. When infrastructure buildings are willing to fund design and craft on a façade, it usually means two things. One, such facilities are increasingly sited near residential areas, so public acceptance has become a practical precondition for delivery. Two, the grid expansion driven by the energy transition is multiplying substation numbers across Europe, turning their civic appearance from a marginal question into a systemic one. Studio RAP has been pursuing robotic ceramic façade fabrication in Rotterdam for several years, with retail and housing façades widely published; Powerwall pushes the same technique from the commercial street onto infrastructure. Read it alongside the Gent Waste Brick reported in No. 014: both rewrite the brick, the oldest building component there is — one changing its material recipe and carbon, the other its geometric freedom and mode of production.

The real threshold in ceramic 3D printing is not shape but shrinkage and warping: every tile's tolerance is pre-compensated in the digital model
Sources: Studio RAP · Parametric Architecture · MaterialDistrict
Clay shrinks during drying and firing, and can warp where a form varies in thickness. When a façade is assembled from 322 individually distinct tiles, any dimensional deviation accumulates along the joints. Studio RAP's method is to build engineered tolerances into each tile at the digital model stage, compensating the post-firing deformation back into the printed form. Students working on digital fabrication should note the general lesson: the bottleneck for additive manufacturing at architectural scale is usually not the printing, but the material's post-processing behaviour and assembly tolerance.
The Sound of Wood / Faverges-Seythenex: a 15 m² timber installation that turns a valley's air movement into audible architecture
Sources: ArchDaily (Sept 20) · Antoine Aunave, Flora Peyrot, Mathilde Steenhaut, Luc Loviton (designers)
The Sound of Wood (Le Bruit du Bois) sits in a mountain valley at Faverges-Seythenex, France, by Antoine Aunave, Flora Peyrot, Mathilde Steenhaut and Luc Loviton, 15 m², entirely in timber, completed 2026. The installation exploits the resonant properties of wood so the structure sounds under moving air, and the designers describe a seasonal split between winter silence and summer vibration: "the sound of wood singing at full volume can be heard in the distance, stirred by the first rising breeze." Small follies of this kind function as a testbed between American architectural education and practice: low cost, simple approvals, and permission to build a material hypothesis outright in order to check it.

Academia & Exhibitions ACADEMIA
IN DEPTH
Three Los Angeles institutions are showing one architect at once: what is being re-proposed about Paul R. Williams is not the celebrity houses but 35 multifamily housing projects
Cross-checked: USC Fisher Museum of Art · USC Today · LACMA · Getty · NBC Los Angeles · Los Angeles Downtown News

Curatorial concept: taking a "celebrity architect" out of the celebrity pile. In popular accounts Paul R. Williams (1894–1980) has long been filed as the architect to Hollywood stars — houses for Frank Sinatra and Lucille Ball, a reputation for elegant eclecticism. "Paul R. Williams: An Architect Considered," organised jointly by the USC School of Architecture and the USC Fisher Museum of Art, pursues a different thread. The exhibition focuses on his contributions to modern multifamily housing, presenting 35 housing projects from a career spanning nearly six decades and pairing original archival drawings with newly commissioned works by contemporary artists and architects. The curatorial claim is explicit: what deserves remembering is not the client list but his sustained commitment to dignified solutions for collective living during a period of rapid urbanisation. Method of display: archival drawings paired with new commissions. The show sets original drawings beside newly commissioned contemporary work. That pairing has a specific purpose when handling a historical architect. Drawings alone tend to seal a figure into the past, whereas bringing in responses from living practitioners puts the operative question directly to the visitor: are the problems Williams faced still with us? In a city with a housing cost crisis as severe as Los Angeles', discussing affordable multifamily design from the 1930s to the 1970s is plainly not a matter of historical interest alone. The number 35 is worth noting too. It is not a selection of greatest hits but a sample large enough to show typological evolution — letting a viewer follow one designer handling the same class of problem across different decades and different policy regimes.

Context: the first licensed Black architect west of the Mississippi. Williams studied architectural engineering at USC and became the first Black architect licensed west of the Mississippi, the first known Black member of the American Institute of Architects nationally in 1923, its first Black Fellow in 1957, and — posthumously, in 2017 — the first and so far only Black architect to receive the AIA Gold Medal. Across nearly sixty years of practice he is estimated to have designed some 3,000 projects. These titles need their context: he practised in an era when restrictive racial covenants still governed most Californian communities, and Williams himself was excluded by law and custom from many of the neighbourhoods he designed. The widely repeated detail that he taught himself to draw upside down, so he could sit across from white clients rather than beside them, is usually retold as an anecdote — but it is a concrete trace of segregation inside professional practice. The word "Considered" in the title is aimed squarely at that anecdotal mode of remembering.
