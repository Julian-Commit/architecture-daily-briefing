Each of today's four sections has something concrete. In Lech, Austria, Snøhetta has flooded the roof of a cable car's mountain station with a shallow pool: striped stepping stones cross the water toward a single rock, and the Alps appear underfoot — an infrastructure roof turned into public space. And Blue Crow Media's Brutalist Asia documents 140 buildings in 28 countries, rewriting Brutalism's lineage from local climate, material and political conditions rather than as an imported style. Sources: ArchDaily (1 October) · Dezeen (2 October) · Snøhetta project page · Lech Zürs am Arlberg tourist board · Free York (October)
Lech Mountain Mirror sits in Lech, in the Austrian state of Vorarlberg, on the roof of the Zugerbergbahn cable car's mountain station. The move is simple: a shallow layer of water across the roof, a line of striped stepping stones set into it, leading to a rock that rises from the surface. Snøhetta partner Patrick Lüth puts it this way: the Lech Mountain Mirror transforms an infrastructure facility into a place for sensory experience; it captures the magic of the mountains for visitors in an unusual way and offers opportunities to admire the mountain world from a different perspective. Pool area, water depth, number of stepping stones and construction details have not been published, and this briefing will not guess at them.

32 kilometres of dike, 70,000 concrete blocks of 6.5 tonnes each, six 30-tonne glass domes and a four-kilometre spiral fish river: the Afsluitdijk's decade-long overhaul reaches completion
Sources: Dezeen (2 October) · Benthem Crouwel Architects · Holland.com (Netherlands Board of Tourism) · Dredging Today (14 September) · West 8 (landscape) · Maritime Journal
Completed in 1932, the Afsluitdijk runs 32 kilometres and separates the Wadden Sea from the freshwater IJsselmeer. The current programme ran from 2016 to 2026 for client Rijkswaterstaat, with Benthem Crouwel Architects on architecture, West 8 on landscape, BAM and Van Oord building, and Rebel and Invesis as investors. Five things happened. One, the dike was raised and reinforced: the Wadden Sea face is armoured with roughly 70,000 purpose-developed Levvel-blocs weighing 6,500 kg each. Two, discharge capacity: a new pumping station houses six mega pumps moving a combined 275,000 litres per second — an Olympic pool every nine seconds — capped by six glass domes nine metres across, four metres high and 30 tonnes apiece, designed to be lifted off whole so a pump can be swapped quickly. Three, two new sluices at Den Oever, between the existing Stevin Sluices. Four, ecology: a 20-metre-wide passage now lets twelve migratory fish species move between the Wadden Sea and the IJsselmeer for the first time in nearly a century, served by a four-kilometre spiral Fish Migration River that reproduces a gently sloping natural current for smelt, stickleback, young eel and young sea trout — species that cannot fight the flow through an open gate. It cost roughly €55 million and broke ground in 2018. Five, public access: a new seaward cycle route runs straight through the engineering works with viewing platforms, a fan-shaped solar park sits at Den Oever, and a service area and footbridge were added at the Vlieter.

28 countries, 140 buildings, 300-plus pages: Brutalist Asia recasts the style's lineage as parallel evolution under local conditions, and Dezeen picked ten of them yesterday
Sources: Dezeen (3 October) · Wallpaper* · ArchDaily (two pieces) · Blue Crow Media (publisher)
Brutalist Asia was compiled by Bob Pang and Derek Lamberton, published by Blue Crow Media with design by Tuomi, released on 21 September 2026: more than 300 pages, 140 buildings, 28 countries, £45. On 3 October Dezeen ran ten of them selected by Pang himself, ranging from a modernist church in South Korea to a geometric post office in Qatar. The book presents itself as the first major survey of Brutalism across the whole Asian continent, and its central claim is that Asian Brutalism is not an imported Western style but concrete architecture evolving in parallel in response to local climate, materials, culture and politics — the origins and application of Asian Brutalism seen from a local point of view. The selection pairs canonical landmarks — Le Corbusier's Palace of Assembly in Chandigarh (1962), Kenzo Tange's Yoyogi National Gymnasium in Tokyo, Khalil Khoury's Interdesign Building in Beirut — with a large body of work that has rarely appeared in print, including the Physics Building at Kasetsart University in Bangkok (1969), the Special Room Block at St Stephen's College in Hong Kong (1981) and the Inter-University Seminar House in Tokyo (1965). Eight regional scholars contribute essays, among them Christele Harrouk, Bhawna Dandona and Kyung Taek Lee; the architects foregrounded include Paul Rudolph and Le Corbusier alongside Balkrishna V. Doshi, Raj Rewal, Charles Correa and Kim Swoo Geun.

For planning students the value of the document lies in the problem it addresses: this is the classic aftermath of clearance-led postwar renewal, where the shortage is not land but density, and the planning tools have to put the density back.

Giving a cable car roof back to the mountain: with one sheet of water, a line of stones and a rock, Snøhetta has built a public space above the treeline that costs nothing to enter
Cross-checked against: ArchDaily (1 October, photography Christian Flatscher) · Dezeen (2 October) · Snøhetta project page · Lech Zürs am Arlberg tourist board · Free York (October) · public remarks by Snøhetta partner Patrick Lüth

Concept: not making a view, but borrowing one — turning the mountain itself into the exhibit. Lech Mountain Mirror stands in Lech, Vorarlberg, Austria, on the roof of the Zugerbergbahn's mountain station, commissioned by the local lift operator Lech Bergbahnen AG and designed by Snøhetta. The whole project consists of three things: a shallow pool, a line of striped stepping stones, and a rock rising out of the water. None of them is the subject. The subject is the mountain, reflected. A mirror in an alpine setting has one peculiar property — it produces no new image, it only inverts what is already there. The ridgelines, the movement of cloud, the changing angle of light over a day all land on the water surface, so what a visitor sees is an image rewritten continuously by weather and hour. The stated aim is to invite people to pause and observe that shifting landscape, which in a ski-area context is not rhetoric: a lift station is a transitional space where the default behaviour is to leave as fast as possible, and a sheet of water converts leaving into standing still.

Spatial strategy: organise the pause with a path, not a room. The pool has no enclosure and no roof; visitors walk into it rather than inside anything. What organises them is the line of stepping stones — simultaneously the route and the only surface that may be stepped on, which means the visitor's path is precisely prescribed: follow the stones, end at the rock. This path-as-architecture approach demotes what could easily have been a viewing platform into a short crossing, and bodily engagement goes up rather than down: water underfoot, the reflection breaking and re-forming beside your shoe, an entirely different register of perception from looking out over a railing. Free access is what makes the strategy work. In most ski areas the roof of a summit station is pure plant area that nobody reaches; converting it to public space without charging admission means it sits outside the lift company's revenue model and is treated instead as long-term destination-building.

Material and construction: wood and stone, two materials that need no explanation on a mountain. ArchDaily lists the materials as wood and stone, and files the work under installations and structures / landscape architecture rather than as a building. The logic at altitude is direct: freeze-thaw cycling, UV load and snow quickly eliminate any finish that needs maintenance, whereas wood and stone age predictably and their surface change stays in step with the surroundings. Worth noting is that the pool sits on the roof of a working cable car station, which means waterproofing, drainage, winter freezing and draining all had to be coordinated with the existing structure — but none of that construction detail, nor the water depth, pool area or number of stones, has been published, and this briefing will not infer it. One thing is certain: the physical scale of the intervention is very small. What it relies on is not difficulty of construction but choice of position.

Context and consequence: a ski village hands its off-season problem to architecture. Lech and neighbouring Zürs are long-established destinations in the Arlberg region, and their structural difficulty is the familiar one: extreme crowding in winter, near-dormancy in summer and autumn. The published expectation is explicit — that this free installation becomes an attraction helping position Lech as a year-round destination rather than a winter resort. Patrick Lüth's framing is that the mirror transforms an infrastructure facility into a place for sensory experience, capturing the magic of the mountains in an unusual way and offering a different perspective on the mountain world. Placed in a wider context, the project belongs to a decade-long alpine genre: minimal architectural interventions grafted onto existing lifts, tunnels and viewpoints in order to extend the usable season. Snøhetta's own Perspektivenweg viewpoint near Innsbruck, completed in 2019, was an earlier version of the same idea built with a cantilever; here the device is a water surface instead. For architecture students the transferable lesson is not formal but judgemental: when the landscape of a site overwhelms anything that could be built on it, the correct move may be subtraction — supply a framing device, then get out of the way.

Desai Chia's Chatham Residence: a retirement house on 80 wooded acres in upstate New York, its metal roof shaped after native oak leaves, corrugated siding rippling along the curves
Sources: Dezeen (2 October) · Wallpaper* · Desai Chia Architecture · Free York (October)
The house sits on an 80-acre site in Columbia County, New York, built for clients planning their retirement, with views toward the Catskill Mountains. Desai Chia Architecture derived the roof geometry from the silhouettes of native oak leaves found on the property: the roof reads as a set of curved overhangs that shade and shelter while casting shadows close to those of a tree canopy. Walls and roof are clad largely in corrugated metal siding, which traces the curves as one continuous ripple. The studio explains the choice by its duality — a rugged material common to contemporary farm buildings, and therefore at home in a rural setting, yet also a surface that catches light with minimalist precision. As a house, it is a useful example of regionalism located in the section and the overhang rather than in decorative motifs.

Swapping a municipal office block for 4,000 homes: the real story at 100 Gold Street is not the height but how city-owned land yields 1,000 permanently affordable units with no city subsidy
Cross-checked against: The Architect's Newspaper (2 October) · 6sqft (September) · NYC Mayor's Office release (September) · New York YIMBY (September) · Gothamist · Connect CRE · amNewYork

The scheme: one city-owned parcel, two towers, nearly 4,000 homes. 100 Gold Street sits in Lower Manhattan's Financial District near the Brooklyn Bridge. Today it is a nine-storey 1960s office building owned by the City of New York and occupied mainly by the Department of Housing Preservation and Development. The redevelopment would provide roughly 4,000 apartments, about 1,000 of them permanently affordable and rent-stabilised, plus a replacement senior centre for Hamilton-Madison House and a public fitness centre. GFP Real Estate was selected through a December 2025 request for proposals, with CetraRuddy Architects as designer. The released renderings show two towers, the taller apparently above 1,000 feet — a figure to treat carefully, since every outlet notes that final dimensions and unit counts will be determined through the review process. A rendering is not an entitlement.

The mechanism: no city subsidy, 485-x in exchange for permanent affordability. The most instructive part of this project is its financing, not its massing. The city states explicitly that it carries no city housing subsidy; instead it uses the 485-x tax exemption, New York State's 2024 replacement for the lapsed 421-a, which trades a long property-tax abatement for affordable units and wage standards. A roughly 25 per cent permanently affordable share with no layered subsidy means the cost of the affordable units is carried by the market-rate units together with forgone tax revenue, while what the city contributes is the land itself. The comparison the city offers is pointed: this one project would deliver twice as many permanently affordable homes as Manhattan Community District 1 produced in the previous decade — a statement about the scale of the project and about how little Lower Manhattan has built.

Process and timing: this is the start of environmental review, nothing more. The detail most likely to be misread is the schedule. What is underway is the front end of public review: a scoping hearing on 29 October, then environmental review, with the project expected to enter the Uniform Land Use Review Procedure (ULURP) only in 2027 and pass in turn through Manhattan Community Board 1, the borough president, the City Planning Commission and the City Council. In other words, at least a year of statutory process separates the announcement from any possible groundbreaking, and any headline reading "4,000 homes approved" is premature. The scheme also has continuity: former mayor Eric Adams proposed it in January 2025, and Mayor Zohran Mamdani's administration added 300 units to bring the total to roughly 4,000.

Context and consequence: downtown keeps converting, but the constraint is procedural. The Financial District has been turning offices into housing since the late 1990s. What makes 100 Gold Street different is that it is not a private owner's conversion decision but the city disposing of its own asset, which makes it simultaneously a housing-policy testbed and a target for procedural fights. Planning students should read it alongside another New York item from the same week: on 1 October the city named 12 affordable-housing fast-track community districts where, from January 2027, final approval of qualifying affordable projects shifts from the City Council to the City Planning Commission. Both stories point at the same diagnosis — that New York's housing bottleneck is believed to lie in approvals rather than in land supply, so the policy instruments are concentrating on shortening and rerouting the approval path. 100 Gold Street, by contrast, will run the full ULURP. How long it actually takes will be a concrete test of whether that diagnosis holds.

England's National Scheme of Delegation takes effect 31 October: planning applications default to officer decisions, ending automatic referral on councillor request or objection count
Sources: Strutt & Parker · Freeths (legal analysis) · Wealden District Council · Savills · HSF Kramer
The legal basis is the Planning and Infrastructure Act 2025 together with the Town and Country Planning (Discharge of Local Planning Authority Functions) (England) Regulations 2026. From 31 October 2026, all planning decisions in England are delegated to planning officers by default unless the application falls within a type listed in Schedule 2 of the Regulations. Until now each local authority set its own scheme of delegation, and common arrangements — automatic referral to committee on a councillor's request, or once objections reach a set number — cannot continue in their present form. The stated aim is to let planning committees spend their limited time on larger and more significant development, speeding up and standardising local decisions. The reform lands directly alongside the data showing English residential planning permissions at their lowest level since 2012, and the argument it tests is whether the bottleneck is procedural or market-driven.

Reopening a gap in a 1932 dike: the hardest part of the Afsluitdijk's decade-long overhaul was not raising 32 kilometres, it was letting small fish through
Cross-checked against: Dezeen (2 October) · Benthem Crouwel Architects project page · Holland.com (official) · Dredging Today (14 September construction report) · West 8 (landscape) · Maritime Journal

Concept: a flood defence redefined as three things at once — holding water out, pumping water away, letting things through. Completed in 1932, the Afsluitdijk runs 32 kilometres and turned the former Zuiderzee inlet into the freshwater IJsselmeer while holding back the Wadden Sea. It is among the most nationally symbolic engineering works in the Netherlands, and it is now a piece of infrastructure approaching a century old. The current programme ran from 2016 to 2026 for client Rijkswaterstaat, with Benthem Crouwel Architects on architecture, West 8 on landscape, a BAM and Van Oord consortium building, and Rebel and Invesis as investors. The team's approach was not simple structural reinforcement but the loading of three previously conflicting demands onto the same embankment: resist higher sea levels and stronger storms, discharge more inland water, and let both ecology and the public cross a barrier that had severed them for nearly a hundred years.

Spatial strategy: treat the plant room as architecture and the engineering face as landscape. The most legible move is the six glass domes over the pumping station — nine metres in diameter, four metres high, 30 tonnes each, covering six mega pumps below. This works on two levels. Functionally, each dome is designed to be lifted off whole, so a pump can be replaced without dismantling the building: a maintenance cycle written directly into the construction. Expressively, glass means the pumps are meant to be seen — a combined 275,000 litres per second, an Olympic pool every nine seconds, is a number that only becomes public knowledge when it is visible. The second move is the new seaward cycle route, which does not detour around the engineering works but runs straight through them, with viewing platforms and changes of surface material marking what lies beneath. A fan-shaped solar park sits at Den Oever, and a service area and footbridge were added at the Vlieter. The overall logic turns a causeway previously experienced only at 100 km/h into a linear place that can be stopped in and read.

Structure and material: 70,000 Levvel-blocs of 6,500 kg, and a four-kilometre spiral river. The Wadden Sea face is armoured with roughly 70,000 Levvel-blocs developed specifically for this project, each weighing 6,500 kg. Precast armour units are not new; what matters is the custom geometry tuned to dissipate the particular wave climate, laid dry so individual units can later be replaced. Two new sluices were added at Den Oever between the existing Stevin Sluices. But the technically distinctive piece is the Fish Migration River (Vismigratierivier): four kilometres long, laid out as a spiral, costing roughly €55 million, begun in 2018. The problem it solves is one of scale that is easy to overlook — when the dike's gates open the current is fierce, and strong swimmers such as salmon and adult eel can fight through, but smelt, stickleback, young eel and young sea trout have no chance at all. The spiral trades length for gradient: it spreads the head difference between the North Sea and the IJsselmeer into a gentle flow within a limited footprint, so weak swimmers can work their way up in stages. The accompanying passage is 20 metres wide, and the target is free two-way movement for twelve migratory species. Dutch sources describe it as the first project of its kind in the world; the Friesland conservation association It Fryske Gea provided ecological guidance, and funding came from public budgets, European contributions and private organisations.

Context and consequence: when climate rewrites the design standard, old works have to be renegotiated. The Afsluitdijk overhaul is essentially a forced redesign: sea-level rise and changing storm intensity invalidated the 1932 design standard, and the Netherlands cannot abandon the dike. What is notable is that the redesign did not make it more closed. Alongside the reinforcement, it was opened — the fish passage, the cycle route, the visible pumping station, each adding permeability to a purely defensive structure. That is consistent with the broader Dutch shift over the past two decades from fighting water to Room for the River. Two points are directly usable for students on the building-technology side. First, the role of the architect inside very large infrastructure: Benthem Crouwel was not responsible for the structure but for the interfaces at which people meet the system. Second, ecological compensation is no longer an add-on after the engineering is finished — four kilometres of migration river and €55 million mean it has entered the project's own budget and programme. It is worth noting that ArchDaily's editorial topic this month is The Architecture of Water: Territories, Tensions & Transformations, one of whose arguments is that water is ultimately political. This dike is that sentence in physical form.

CSA Group publishes CSA Z251, Design of volumetric modular structures: a first common technical framework for factory-built three-dimensional modules, complementing the National Building Code
Sources: Canadian Architect · Construction Canada · Modular Building Institute (22 September) · Daily Commercial News (October) · Ontario Construction News · Canadian Consulting Engineer
CSA Group has released CSA Z251, Design of volumetric modular structures. Volumetric modular means complete three-dimensional building modules fabricated in a factory and craned into place on site, as distinct from panelised prefabrication. The standard establishes a common technical framework for these buildings and is intended to complement the National Building Code of Canada, setting requirements for structural performance, building services, environmental separation, fire protection, manufacturing and architectural considerations. The drafting technical committee included architects, engineers, modular manufacturers and provincial and municipal building officials. The context is federal: modular construction has been identified as one of the modern methods supporting delivery at scale through Build Canada Homes. The Canadian modular industry is valued at more than CA$5.5 billion and is projected to grow more than five per cent annually through 2029. Standardisation is unavoidable here — without a common design and acceptance language, factory capacity cannot move across provincial lines.
Chryso and Ecocem launch EnviroMix 3150 ACT: paired with ACT low-carbon cement it cuts concrete's carbon footprint by more than 50 per cent, already in use on the former John Lewis HQ in London
Sources: World Cement (2 October) · Cemnet / International Cement Review · Agg-Net · Irish Construction
EnviroMix 3150 ACT is a superplasticiser jointly developed by Chryso and Ecocem for concrete incorporating Ecocem's ACT low-carbon cement technology, presented at BATIMAT 2026 in Paris, with two patent applications filed. The difficulty with reduced-clinker concrete is loss of flowability and placeability, which is exactly what the admixture targets: the stated figures are high flowability, low viscosity and workability retention beyond 90 minutes. Ecocem says ACT can cut cement carbon emissions by up to 70 per cent, and that the combined solution reduces concrete's carbon footprint by more than 50 per cent, reaching GWR5 classification and beyond. In practice the technology is being used on the refurbishment of the former John Lewis headquarters in London, where almost 1,300 m³ of low-carbon concrete is expected to have been placed by the end of 2026. The takeaway for students: decarbonising cement does not work by swapping the binder alone — the admixture system has to be redesigned in step, or the mix simply cannot be placed on site.

Not a Western style that travelled east, but concrete growing differently under different climates and regimes: Brutalist Asia rewrites a flattened history with 140 buildings from 28 countries
Cross-checked against: Dezeen (3 October, ten picks by editor Bob Pang) · Wallpaper* review · ArchDaily (two pieces) · Blue Crow Media (publisher) · Dezeen's earlier coverage of Brutalist Korea and Brutalist Japan

The book, and the account it sets out to displace. Brutalist Asia was compiled by Bob Pang and Derek Lamberton, published by Blue Crow Media with design by Tuomi, released 21 September 2026: more than 300 pages, 140 buildings, 28 countries, £45. On 3 October Dezeen published ten selections made by Pang himself, from a modernist church in South Korea to a geometric post office in Qatar. The book presents itself as the first major survey of Brutalism across the entire Asian continent. What it sets out to displace is a narrative sequence that remains common in teaching: Brutalism begins in postwar Britain, is named by the Architectural Review, has its vocabulary fixed by Le Corbusier at Marseille, and then spreads outward, with Asian examples read as a chapter in its reception. The book argues the opposite order: that concrete architecture in Asia developed as a response to local climate, materials, culture and politics — parallel evolution, not import — or in the editors' phrasing, the origins and application of Asian Brutalism from a local point of view.

Method: widen the list, then let the list argue. The argument is made not through theory but through scope. On one side sit the acknowledged landmarks: Le Corbusier's Palace of Assembly at Chandigarh (1962), Kenzo Tange's Yoyogi National Gymnasium in Tokyo, Khalil Khoury's Interdesign Building in Beirut. On the other sits a large body of work that has barely been published: the Physics Building at Kasetsart University in Bangkok (1969), the Special Room Block at St Stephen's College in Hong Kong (1981), the Inter-University Seminar House in Tokyo (1965). Once the second category is numerous enough and geographically wide enough, diffusion becomes hard to sustain as an explanation — more than two dozen countries with little contact between them, all using board-formed in-situ concrete heavily in the same period, is better explained by shared conditions than by a shared model: restricted steel imports against locally producible cement; hot and humid climates rewarding deep overhangs and the thermal mass of heavy walls; newly independent states needing public buildings that could be built quickly at controllable cost. Eight regional scholars contribute essays, among them Christele Harrouk, Bhawna Dandona and Kyung Taek Lee — a structure that itself declines the single-author survey of a continent.

The names put back in view. Besides Western pioneers such as Paul Rudolph and Le Corbusier, the architects foregrounded are largely local practitioners: Balkrishna V. Doshi, Raj Rewal, Charles Correa and Kim Swoo Geun. The point of that list is to move local modernism out of a subordinate position. Correa, for instance, treated shading devices, ventilation shafts and open terraces as climatic equipment rather than formal elements — a starting premise quite different from European Brutalism's discourse of structural honesty. Kim Swoo Geun worked under the double constraint of a national building agenda and material scarcity. Setting these cases beside European work in a single volume reminds the reader that the same material language can be produced by entirely different problems.

Context and consequence: a publishing wave attached to a live preservation question. Brutalist Asia is not an isolated title. It follows Blue Crow Media's Brutalist Japan (2024) and Brutalist Korea (2026), extending the series from single countries to a continent. Such books have an immediate practical function right now: a great many Asian concrete public buildings from the 1960s to the 1980s are in their demolition window — structures reaching the end of their service life, services obsolete, land values high — while most have not been entered on any protection register. Photography and documentation are the precondition for entering a preservation conversation at all: a building that has never been recorded cannot even be discussed. For Chinese-language readers the book carries a specific comparative value. When we discuss China's concrete public buildings of the same decades, the available comparators are usually European and American; this book supplies a whole row of neighbouring samples produced under similar climates, similar stages of development and similar institutional conditions. This briefing has seen no announcement of a Chinese edition; the publisher's statements are the authority on that.
