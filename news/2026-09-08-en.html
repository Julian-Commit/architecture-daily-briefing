EDITOR'S NOTE

Almost everything in today's architecture news circles the same question: what do we do with what already exists. MAD released an updated design for Danshuis in Rotterdam, converting a 1923 Art Deco dock warehouse into Europe's largest centre for dance. City agencies in New York, London and Brussels have, within months of one another, turned disused industrial buildings into circular construction hubs so that salvaged material can find its way back onto a site. The German Pavilion for the 2027 Venice Architecture Biennale announced CONVIVERE, a show that treats stairwells, courtyards and the patch of ground outside your door as the setting where democracy is practised. The counter-example is Jakarta, where an offshore seawall once drawn in the silhouette of a mythical bird has quietly given way to conventional dikes and pumps, leaving behind reclaimed real estate and fishing villages stripped of their mangroves. The same day, the EIU published its 2026 liveability ranking: Copenhagen holds first place, and nine Asian cities now sit in the top twenty.

Fitting a Skirt Into a Warehouse: How MAD's Danshuis Teaches a 1923 Freight Building to Dance
Cross-checked: designboom · Dezeen · ICON · STIRworld · Gramilano · e-architect

MAD has published an updated design for Danshuis, Rotterdam's house for dance. The project sits on the south bank of the River Maas and converts a 1923 Art Deco dock warehouse totalling 9,500 square metres. The client is the Droom en Daad Foundation, and once complete the building will be the largest single facility in Europe devoted to producing, rehearsing and developing dance. An initial scheme was released in 2023; this is the developed version, with completion scheduled for 2030.

Design concept: making movement into a visible architectural event. The difficulty with dance is that it has no shape — it happens in time, not in space. MAD's response is not to imitate a pose but to set a translucent, flowing form against the hard masonry of the warehouse, letting the collision of two material states carry the argument. Ma Yansong puts it this way: "Movement goes beyond architecture, becoming part of the district's character." That sentence has a specific geography behind it. Danshuis stands next to the Fenix museum of migration, also by MAD, and the two buildings read consecutively along the same stretch of dock as a pair of statements about leaving and arriving.

Structure and material: pleating as a way of softening a facade. The decisive move in the new work is a four-storey foyer wrapped in a pleated glass facade. The pleating is not merely decorative. Folding breaks the single flat reflection of plate glass into a continuously varying sequence of mirrored planes, so that daylight and drifting cloud slide across a static elevation and give it an expression that changes through the day. The folds also increase the second moment of area of the glazed skin, which helps reduce the secondary support required for a full-height glass wall. The design describes it as a dancer's skirt floating in the wind — a metaphor that holds up in construction terms precisely because the fold delivers both visual softness and structural stiffness.

Spatial strategy: turning circulation into performance. The programme includes fourteen dance studios, an open podium, a gym, exhibition space, a café and a rooftop Salle des Fêtes for large public gatherings. Binding them together is a sculptural vertical foyer that links the interior to the waterfront, with the entrance framed deliberately toward the river. At the top of the building sits a semi-circular open-air theatre modelled on the Greek agora, where the audience wraps around the performance area and the hierarchy between watching and being watched flattens out. A yellow seagull sculpture occupies the second-floor landscaped terrace, and red neon facing the water picks up the vernacular signage of the port. The intent throughout is plain: rehearsal, normally a closed process, is made visible to anyone passing.

Context and consequence: from loading cargo to loading bodies. The site belongs to Rotterdam's old harbour district, which in the twentieth century handled ocean freight and emigrant departures; the warehouse is physical evidence of that economy. Converting it into a dance centre continues a sequence of cultural reuse along this waterfront, forming with the Fenix museum a chain running from industrial remnant to cultural destination. What deserves attention is the restraint of the approach. MAD neither demolishes nor encases the original warehouse; the new volume attaches to it as a legible foreign body, and the historic elevation stays fully readable. For students working increasingly on existing fabric, this is a sample that can be taken apart directly: old and new neither merge nor fight, each stays whole, and the seam between them becomes where the architecture actually happens.

When Flood Defence Is Also a Property Deal: Twelve Years of Jakarta's Great Garuda
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · NCICD 2015 Executive Summary · Indonesia Investments · Water Alternatives · PreventionWeb / Thomson Reuters Foundation · Indonesian Ministry of Public Works (2025 statement)

In 2014 a single aerial rendering made Jakarta's coastline international news: an offshore seawall shaped into the silhouette of Garuda, the mythical bird on Indonesia's national emblem, its wings spanning Jakarta Bay. Known as the Great Garuda, the scheme was the public face of the 2015 National Capital Integrated Coastal Development (NCICD) master plan. Twelve years later, ArchDaily has revisited it in full. The bird is gone from the drawings; the question it raised is not.

The physical scale of the problem. Jakarta faces not one threat but three overlapping pressures: land subsidence, heavy rainfall and tidal flooding. Subsidence is the hardest to reverse — the city sinks by roughly 7.5 centimetres a year on average, and up to 17 centimetres in parts of the north, driven largely by groundwater extraction. That makes sea-level rise not even the dominant variable here: the city drops faster than the sea climbs. The 2015 NCICD executive summary framed the problem as insufficient space to hold water, and proposed an offshore dike combined with a retention reservoir.

The financing logic was also the pathology. What made the Great Garuda distinctive was not its shape but where the money came from. The seawall was tied to the reclamation of seventeen artificial islands; developers would profit from island real estate, and those profits would in turn pay for pumping stations, retention ponds and other public infrastructure. Fiscally attractive, but with an unavoidable consequence, which ArchDaily states directly: "Once protection infrastructure also creates valuable land, defending a city and remaking it become difficult to separate." Technical judgements about flood defence and commercial judgements about return were bound into the same drawing, and it stopped being clear which was steering.

Who carries the cost. As reclamation and dike works advanced, mangroves continued to degrade — the natural barrier that dissipates wave and tidal energy, so that losing it amounts to removing one line of defence before building another. In fishing settlements such as Kamal Muara and Muara Angke, residents faced a compounded situation in which the prospect of displacement tied to new infrastructure became, in ArchDaily's phrasing, sometimes more immediate than the flooding itself. Set against that are gated, island-based enclaves like Golf Island built on reclaimed ground. A barrier meant to protect an entire city ended up distributing risk and benefit toward opposite ends.

Where it stands now. The Garuda image has been abandoned. Sections of NCICD continue under provincial management, and what dominates instead is conventional work: seawalls, dikes, pumps and incremental coastal protection. In 2025 Indonesia's Ministry of Public Works reiterated a far larger ambition — a Giant Sea Wall running roughly 946 kilometres along Java's northern coast from Cilegon to Gresik. The scale expands enormously; the unsolved problem carries over intact. Without first curbing groundwater extraction, a barrier of any height is only chasing a city that keeps sinking.

Why architecture students should read this one. The Great Garuda offers a rare complete sample: a scheme with an extraordinarily arresting form, stripped of that form by reality over a decade until only the engineering remained; and, more importantly, a demonstration of how a public work that must raise its own funds by selling land can quietly have its beneficiary swapped out. Climate-adaptive infrastructure will appear in a great many design briefs over the coming decade. What Jakarta suggests is that the item most worth auditing on the drawings is often not the section but the funding structure.

UN General Assembly Votes 164–1 to Promote Equal-Area World Maps, with the US the Only Vote Against
Sources: ArchDaily · UN General Assembly records · The Equal Earth Projection
On September 4 the General Assembly adopted a resolution — 164 in favour, one against (the United States), six abstentions — encouraging governments, educational institutions and technology companies to adopt equal-area projections that represent the relative size of landmasses accurately. The resolution was proposed by Togo and presented by Foreign Minister Robert Dussey. The Mercator projection of 1569 was built to preserve angles for navigation, at the cost of grossly inflating area at high latitudes: Africa is roughly fourteen times the size of Greenland, yet appears comparable on a Mercator map. The Equal Earth projection, released in 2018, preserves proportional area while sacrificing accuracy of shape, distance and direction. The implication for planning and cartography teaching is that no flat map can preserve every geometric property at once, and choosing a projection is itself taking a position.

Where Does the Salvage Go: Three Cities Build Warehouses for Material Reuse — the Link Circular Construction Was Missing
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · The Architect's Newspaper · Architects' Journal · Building Design · Construction Enquirer · citydev.brussels / EU Reporter

Circular construction has been discussed in academic and policy texts for more than a decade, but on site it has consistently stalled at the same point: components taken out of a demolition have nowhere to sit before they are used again. The moment of removal and the moment a new project needs material almost never coincide, and what is missing in between is physical infrastructure for storage, sorting, testing and redistribution. In 2026, municipal agencies in New York, London and Brussels arrived at the same answer within months of one another — convert disused industrial buildings into circular construction hubs.

New York: a 20-acre dock property becomes a material clearing house. The pilot occupies MADE Bush Terminal in Sunset Park, Brooklyn — a 20-acre historic industrial property — and launched in August 2026 for one year. The operator, Our Temenos, is a Brooklyn-based ecological landscaping firm with M/WBE certification, handling recovery, storage, sorting and redistribution of construction material. The policy scaffolding is NYCEDC's Green Economy Action Plan and its 2024 Clean and Circular guidelines. Choosing a local small business over a large waste contractor as operator is itself a distributive decision.

London: 8,000 square metres, at least 950 tonnes over five years. Tipping Point East at the Royal Docks is delivered by the climate charity of the same name together with Newham Council, occupies more than 8,000 square metres, and is described as the UK's first circular construction hub, with backers suggesting it will become Europe's largest once fully operational. It is more than a warehouse: the site also carries low-carbon construction practice, vocational training and cultural programming, with partners including Material Cultures, long engaged in bio-based materials research, the spatial justice collective RESOLVE and the fabrication studio Yes Make. The team estimates diverting at least 950 tonnes from landfill over five years (ArchDaily cites a target of over 1,000 tonnes). The hub is phase one of a wider Circular Economy Village planned for Silvertown over the next five years.

Brussels: EU structural funds underwrite a public logistics layer. ReUse Park in Ganshoren occupies an empty warehouse on the citydev.brussels Darwin site, converting 6,000 square metres with support from the 2021–2027 European Regional Development Fund. It is positioned as a regional logistics hub: collecting, sorting and preparing material from demolition and deconstruction, while hosting training programmes for construction workers and companies. The project launched in February 2026, with the operator contract to be awarded through public competition in early 2027.

Why these three belong in one reading. The approaches differ — a short pilot with a local small business in New York, a charity-led composite campus in London, publicly underwritten infrastructure in Brussels — but the common ground is unmistakable: in each case municipal authority supplies the land and the start-up funding, each converts existing industrial fabric rather than building new, and each writes training into the programme. That points to a judgement often overlooked: the bottleneck in circular construction is not technical but logistical and human. Can the component be taken down intact, is there anywhere to put it, is there anyone who knows how to remove it, and will anyone dare specify it in a new project — not one of those four questions is a materials science question. Separately, the Horizon Europe project CirCoFin supports comparable hubs in Munich, Copenhagen, Lisbon and Scotland with digital marketplaces and material traceability, addressing precisely that last gap in trust.

What this changes in design. Once such hubs become ordinary infrastructure, working method shifts with them: a material schedule no longer begins with a product catalogue but with the hub's current stock; junction detailing has to allow reversible connections for future disassembly; and the design programme has to tolerate uncertain material supply as a new normal. None of these sit comfortably inside current BIM workflows — which is exactly the gap discussed in another item in this section, on material intelligence.

"Material Intelligence" Addresses What BIM Doesn't: Product Data Still Scattered Across Spreadsheets and PDFs
Sources: ArchDaily · Acelab
ArchDaily notes that BIM coordinates geometry and components, while product specification information remains distributed across platforms, spreadsheets, PDFs and databases — leaving selections, specifications and documentation persistently out of step, with no mechanism to track discontinued lines or updated certifications. Non-geometric items such as paint colours, fabrics and wallcoverings have long lacked any coordination at all. The article cites Acelab's Material Hub, organised in three layers: a database of more than 200,000 products with performance, certification and sustainability metrics; a library recording a firm's own material decision history; and a shared collaborative workflow. Its Smart Docs feature generates specifications, schedules, keynotes and sustainability reports from selections, claimed to cut documentation time by up to 50%, and links embodied carbon data directly to material choices so it can be weighed early in design rather than at the end.
Atlassian Central Reaches Full Height in Sydney: 180 Metres, 39 Storeys, the World's Tallest Hybrid Timber Tower, Upfront Embodied Carbon Halved
Sources: designboom · Newsweek · New Atlas · SHoP Architects
Designed by SHoP Architects and BVN with Eckersley O'Callaghan as structural engineers, Atlassian Central has risen to its full height of more than 180 metres across 39 storeys — roughly 100 metres taller than the previous record holder, the 25-storey Ascent MKE in Milwaukee at about 87 metres. The structure is a timber-steel-concrete hybrid: timber reduces overall weight and cuts the carbon of the frame, while the surrounding structure supplies the stiffness and lateral resistance a tower of this height requires. The project uses approximately 10,000 cubic metres of Austrian timber and contains seven timber "habitats" — four-storey workplace clusters intended to bring daylight and natural ventilation to occupants. Upfront embodied carbon is stated to be 50% lower than a conventional premium office tower of comparable class. Completion is expected in late 2026.

Exhibiting Democracy in the Stairwell: CONVIVERE and a Systematic Inventory of Housing's In-Between Spaces
Cross-checked: ArchDaily · TUM School of Engineering and Design · German Federal Ministry for Housing, Urban Development and Building · Kawahara Krause Architects · La Biennale di Venezia

The German Federal Ministry for Housing, Urban Development and Building has announced that the German Pavilion at the 20th International Architecture Exhibition will be curated by the Hamburg practice Kawahara Krause Architects — Tatsuya Kawahara and Ellen Kristina Krause — with co-curators Andres Lepik and Olga Cobuscean. The concept was selected by an expert panel through open competition. Titled "CONVIVERE — A Repertoire of Living Together," the exhibition runs May 8 to November 21, 2027, with preview days on May 6 and 7.

The premise: moving attention from inside the apartment to the patch of ground outside it. Housing exhibitions usually exhibit plans — unit types, areas, furniture layouts. CONVIVERE deliberately avoids that route, directing attention instead to what the curators call housing's in-between spaces: the threshold, the stairwell, the courtyard, the space in front of the door. In a brief, these usually appear only as circulation or common area, and on a cost plan they belong to the portion to be squeezed. They are also, precisely, where informal contact between neighbours actually occurs. The curatorial statement holds that trust, neighbourliness and life unfold in these spaces.

The starting diagnosis: three present pressures. The curators define their point of departure as a set of conditions — growing social isolation, political polarisation, and sustained pressure to build more and faster. There is a connection between the three that is not obvious but does hold: when housing supply is compressed into a question of speed and quantity, the first things optimised away are the in-between spaces that generate no sellable area; and their disappearance in turn erodes the occasions for everyday encounter. The question the exhibition puts is what architecture can contribute to the everyday foundations of democracy. The framing is deliberately narrow — it does not address the grand relationship between architecture and political systems, only the stairwell.

Method: an inventory drawn from existing German housing. The pavilion plans to present concrete spatial situations from German housing across different periods, selected to demonstrate how design shapes communal living. That orientation means the exhibits are largely re-readings of existing built cases rather than proposals for new ones. Research involves students from four German institutions: RWTH Aachen, RPTU Kaiserslautern, the Technical University of Munich and the Stuttgart State Academy of Art and Design. Bringing students into the research chain of a national pavilion has been a fairly consistent German practice in recent years.

Context: the pavilion's historical baggage and its recent path. The German Pavilion in the Giardini is a building remodelled in 1938 with unmistakable classicising marks of the Nazi period, and its own politics has always been something curators must handle — past editions have either confronted it directly or worked around it through bodily experience. CONVIVERE's route is to translate the building's political question down to everyday scale: not monumentality but the entrance hall. The shift matches a broader tendency in recent architecture exhibitions, away from showing signature buildings and toward showing the social conditions of dwelling.

What it offers architectural education. For students working on housing studios, CONVIVERE supplies a review lens that can be borrowed directly: don't only ask about unit efficiency, ask how many possible encounters occur along the walk from the street to your own front door. Is the stair wide enough for two people to stop and talk, is there somewhere by the door to set something down or sit for a moment, does the courtyard belong to anyone — all of these can be tested on a drawing.

USINA and Collective Construction in Brazil: 5,000 Homes Over Three Decades, with Mutirão as a Design Method
Sources: ArchDaily · USINA CTAH · FUCVAM
Founded in São Paulo in the early 1990s, USINA CTAH brings together architects, engineers and social scientists around Brazil's mutirão tradition of collective self-build: future residents work on construction during weekends and collective workdays while also taking on organisation, food preparation, childcare and administration, and participate substantively in decisions rather than simply supplying labour. Technical information is translated and discussed collectively so that expertise does not remain restricted to specialists. The group has delivered more than 5,000 housing units along with community centres, schools and daycare facilities. Representative projects include COPROMO in Osasco — 1,000 apartments across 50 buildings using self-supporting ceramic blocks — and Mutirão Paulo Freire; at COPROMO, participants established their own metalworking workshop to fabricate the metal staircases. The lineage connects to Uruguay's FUCVAM cooperative housing movement, founded in 1970, which has produced over 35,000 homes.
